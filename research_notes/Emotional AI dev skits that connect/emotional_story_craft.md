# Emotional story craft for very short narrative video (30 s to 3 min), and blending comedy with emotion

Scope note: I researched this on 21 Oct 2026. Every finding has a source URL. Where I could not open a primary source (some
TED/NPR/JSTOR/SAGE pages were blocked), I say so and point to the copy I did read. The Inferences apply findings to
our 45-120 s low-poly skits (Dev + Claude + Codex); they are my reasoning, not sourced facts.

## 1. What screenwriting/animation authorities say makes an audience emotionally invested fast

### Takeaway
Pixar-school craft agrees on a few rules: make the audience care first, give the character an inner want or "itch"
and a reason to root for them, show them *trying* (admiration comes from effort, not success), be specific and
honest ("what would you feel?"), and withhold enough that the audience assembles the meaning itself ("2 + 2"). Stated
feeling and fully explained meaning kill the emotion; a well-organised gap creates it.

### Cited Findings
- Andrew Stanton (TED 2012, "The clues to a great story"), transcript text: "Make me care" -- please, emotionally,
  intellectually, aesthetically, just make me care." He calls this the most important story commandment. — [Transcript copy (LingQ, reproduces TED talk text)](https://www.lingq.com/de/englisch-lernen-online/courses/58847/andrew-stanton-the-clues-to-a-great-st-235924/); talk page: [TED](https://www.ted.com/talks/andrew_stanton_the_clues_to_a_great_story) (TED transcript page did not render for me; LingQ is a language-learning copy, so treat wording as near-verbatim)
- Stanton: "the unifying theory of two plus two. Make the audience put things together. Don't give them four, give them
  two plus two." Also: "the audience actually wants to work for their meal." — [LingQ transcript](https://www.lingq.com/de/englisch-lernen-online/courses/58847/andrew-stanton-the-clues-to-a-great-st-235924/); summary of the same idea, developed with Bob Peterson on Finding Nemo: [No Film School](https://nofilmschool.com/2014/08/pixar-andrew-stanton-equation-make-audience-care)
- Stanton: "It's this well-organized absence of information that draws us in." (He discusses WALL-E's largely
  dialogue-free storytelling; "Wall-E's [spine] was to find the beauty.") — [LingQ transcript](https://www.lingq.com/de/englisch-lernen-online/courses/58847/andrew-stanton-the-clues-to-a-great-st-235924/); FilmFreeway also notes his use of silence in WALL-E: [FilmFreeway](https://filmfreeway.com/articles/andrew-stanton-on-the-art-of-the-story/)
- Stanton on the character's spine: "the character has an inner motor, a dominant, unconscious goal that they're
  striving for, an itch that they can't scratch." — [LingQ transcript](https://www.lingq.com/de/englisch-lernen-online/courses/58847/andrew-stanton-the-clues-to-a-great-st-235924/)
- Stanton on the opening promise: "A well told promise is like a pebble being pulled back in a slingshot" (it propels the
  audience to the end). — [LingQ transcript](https://www.lingq.com/de/englisch-lernen-online/courses/58847/andrew-stanton-the-clues-to-a-great-st-235924/)
- Stanton, citing playwright William Archer: "Drama is anticipation mingled with uncertainty." Also "If things go
  static, stories die, because life is never static." and "Use what you know. Draw from it." — [LingQ transcript](https://www.lingq.com/de/englisch-lernen-online/courses/58847/andrew-stanton-the-clues-to-a-great-st-235924/)
- Stanton: "Storytelling ... is joke telling" (knowing your punchline/ending and building every element toward it). — [LingQ transcript](https://www.lingq.com/de/englisch-lernen-online/courses/58847/andrew-stanton-the-clues-to-a-great-st-235924/); TED page summary: talk is about "starting at the end and working back to the beginning" — [TED](https://www.ted.com/talks/andrew_stanton_the_clues_to_a_great_story)
- Emma Coats (Pixar story artist), "22 rules", directly relevant ones, verbatim:
  - #1 "You admire a character for trying more than for their successes."
  - #4 "Once upon a time there was ___. Every day, ___. One day ___. Because of that, ___. Because of that, ___. Until finally ___."
  - #6 "What is your character good at, comfortable with? Throw the polar opposite at them. Challenge them. How do they deal?"
  - #7 "Come up with your ending before you figure out your middle."
  - #13 "Give your characters opinions. Passive/malleable might seem likable to you as you write, but it's poison to the audience."
  - #14 "Why must you tell THIS story? What's the belief burning within you that your story feeds off of? That's the heart of it."
  - #15 "If you were your character, in this situation, how would you feel? Honesty lends credibility to unbelievable situations."
  - #16 "What are the stakes? Give us reason to root for the character. What happens if they don't succeed? Stack the odds against."
  - #21 "You gotta identify with your situation/characters, can't just write 'cool'. What would make YOU act that way?"
  - #22 "What's the essence of your story? Most economical telling of it?"
  — [Pixar Touch blog, "Pixar story rules (one version)"](https://www.pixartouchbook.com/blog/2011/5/15/pixar-story-rules-one-version.html)
- Domee Shi (director, Pixar's "Bao", 2018) drew the story from her own childhood: she is an only child whose parents
  treated her as their "precious little dumpling"; animators studied her mother's dumpling-making ("they poked the
  dough and smelled the pork filling"). Her stated aim: make sure "the story made sense, that it was emotional and
  entertaining." — [NPR, 15 Jun 2018](https://www.npr.org/2018/06/15/619253624/in-pixars-first-female-directed-short-a-dumpling-child-fills-an-empty-nest); [BuzzFeed News](https://www.buzzfeednews.com/article/adambvary/pixar-bao-short-film)
- BuzzFeed News described Bao audiences "erupting with laughter, gasping in shock, and bursting into tears" as the
  mother watches the dumpling son grow apart from her: comedy first, then loss, in one ~8-minute short. — [BuzzFeed News](https://www.buzzfeednews.com/article/adambvary/pixar-bao-short-film)

### Inferences
- Our failing episodes (clipped "So?" "I'm busy." "No.") probably gave the audience neither a want/itch for Dev nor a
  reason to root for anyone. Each skit needs, in the first 5-8 s, one specific thing Dev wants that viewers recognise
  from their own lives (ship before the demo, get a "looks good" from a senior, not lose a weekend's work), and Dev
  must be *trying* (Coats #1), not just reacting.
- "Use what you know" / Bao's personal origin suggests building episodes from real developer moments (the 2 a.m. bug,
  the PR nobody reviews, the first app a parent actually uses) with one hyper-specific detail (a sticky note, a coffee
  mug from a dead startup, a commit message), which carries more feeling than a general situation.
- "2 + 2": don't have a character say the emotion ("I feel unappreciated"). Plant two pieces (e.g. a commit history
  showing Dev stayed up nightly; the manager's "who wrote this?") and let viewers add them.
- Write the ending (the emotional turn + punchline) first, then make every earlier beat point at it (Coats #7, Stanton's
  joke-telling line).

### Gaps
- I did not get verified quotes from Robert McKee, Blake Snyder (Save the Cat, incl. the "save the cat" likeability
  beat), Matthew Luhn (The Best Story Wins), or Pixar in a Box (Khan Academy) within the tool budget. Their commonly
  quoted ideas (e.g. Snyder's "save the cat" scene; McKee's "gap" between expectation and result) are NOT verified here.
- No primary interview with a Pixar SparkShorts director was retrieved.

## 2. Research on being moved, sharing, sad/bittersweet content

### Takeaway
"Being moved" (kama muta) is a measurable, cross-cultural positive emotion triggered by a *sudden intensification of
closeness/communal bonds* (reunions, kindness, sacrifice, belonging), felt as chest warmth, moist eyes, chills and a
lump in the throat. For sharing, high-arousal emotions (awe, anger, anxiety) spread more; pure low-arousal sadness
spreads less. So the target is a warm, rising "moved" peak (closeness restored), not lingering gloom.

### Cited Findings
- Zickfeld et al. (2019), "Kama Muta: Conceptualizing and Measuring the Experience Often Labelled Being Moved Across 19
  Nations and 15 Languages", *Emotion* 19(3), 402-424, DOI 10.1037/emo0000450. 3,542 participants, 19 countries, 5
  continents, 15 languages. Kama muta is a positive social-relational emotion "evoked by experiencing or observing a
  sudden intensification of communal sharing"; commonly accompanied by "a warm feeling in the chest, moist eyes or
  tears, chills or piloerection, feeling choked up or having a lump in the throat, buoyancy, and exhilaration"; it
  motivates "affective devotion and moral commitment to communal sharing". The five facets were highly correlated in
  every sample. — [eScholarship (UC) record](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/2c09161x); [ISCTE record](https://ciencia.iscte-iul.pt/publications/kama-muta-conceptualizing-and-measuring-the-experience-of-being-moved-across-19-nations-and-15/47393?lang=en)
- Cross-cultural video study ("Kama Muta: Similar Emotional Responses to Touching Videos Across the United States, Norway,
  China, Israel, and Portugal"; 7 samples, 5 countries): participants watched short heartwarming videos; indicators of
  increased communal sharing predicted feeling moved; the emotion was predominantly positive and led to feeling
  communal with the characters; same tears/goosebumps/chest-warmth signs across groups. — [University of Haifa record](https://cris.haifa.ac.il/en/publications/kama-muta-similar-emotional-responses-to-touching-videos-across-t/)
- 2020 Frontiers in Psychology study: videos of out-group members engaging in communal sharing evoked kama muta and
  increased humanization of the protagonists (and indirectly reduced blatant dehumanization of the out-group). — [PMC7328370, "Moved by Observing the Love of Others"](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7328370/)
- Berger & Milkman (2012), "What Makes Online Content Viral?", *Journal of Marketing Research*. Abstract (verbatim):
  "positive content is more viral than negative content ... Virality is partially driven by physiological arousal.
  Content that evokes high-arousal positive (awe) or negative (anger or anxiety) emotions is more viral. Content that
  evokes low-arousal, or deactivating, emotions (e.g., sadness) is less viral." Results held controlling for how
  surprising, interesting, or practically useful content was (all positively linked to virality). Data: all New York
  Times articles over three months, plus experiments. — [Author PDF (jonahberger.com)](https://jonahberger.com/wp-content/uploads/2013/02/ViralityB.pdf)
- Berger & Milkman Figure 2 (percentage change in probability of making the most-emailed list for a +1 SD increase):
  values extracted from the PDF include anger 34%, awe 30%, interest 25%, anxiety 21%, emotionality 18%, positivity 13%,
  and sadness -16%. (Caution: the figure's labels came out of the PDF text extraction out of order; the label-value
  pairing I give matches the commonly cited reading, but verify against the figure before quoting.) — [Author PDF](https://jonahberger.com/wp-content/uploads/2013/02/ViralityB.pdf)
- Berger & Milkman experiment: a high-sadness version of a story evoked less arousal (M = 2.75 vs 3.89) and was less
  likely to be shared (M = 2.39 vs 3.80); arousal mediated the effect of sadness on sharing (Sobel z = -2.32, p < .05). — [Author PDF](https://jonahberger.com/wp-content/uploads/2013/02/ViralityB.pdf)
- Poignancy research: a 2013 review describes poignancy as a mixed emotion (positive and negative co-occurring) that
  "seems specific to the experience of meaningful endings, of no longer having something that one once had." — [Advances in Psychological Science, 2013, 21(9):1643-1650](https://journal.psych.ac.cn/adps/EN/Y2013/V21/I9/1643)
- Oliver & Bartsch's "appreciation" construct (entertainment valued for meaning rather than pleasure) exists as
  published work: "Appreciation as audience response: Exploring entertainment gratifications beyond hedonism", Human
  Communication Research (2010), and "Tragic and poignant entertainment" (Routledge Handbook of Emotions and Mass Media,
  2011). I could only confirm the bibliographic records, not their findings. — [Penn State Pure record](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/tragic-and-poignant-entertainment-the-gratifi-cations-of-meaningf/); [Mary Beth Oliver CV](https://bellisario.psu.edu/assets/uploads/directory_cv/OliverMaryBethCV.pdf)

### Inferences
- For our skits the "feelable" moment should be a *closeness spike*: a cold/transactional relationship (Dev vs the AI,
  Claude vs Codex) suddenly becomes caring, loyal or self-sacrificing. That is the exact kama muta trigger. Example
  shapes: the AI that "remembers" something small Dev said; Codex quietly covering for Claude; Dev defending the AI to
  a boss.
- End on the warm rise, not on sadness alone. Sadness that resolves into connection (bittersweet) keeps arousal up;
  flat sad endings are, per Berger & Milkman, less shareable.
- Poignancy's "no longer having something one once had" maps well to developer life: deprecated projects, a model
  version being retired, the last commit on a product, a colleague leaving.

### Gaps
- I did not retrieve a study directly linking kama muta to *sharing behaviour* on YouTube/TikTok, nor primary "elevation"
  (Haidt/Algoe) papers. The Seibt/Schubert attributions for the video studies could not be confirmed from what I read.
- No retrieved study on tears specifically in response to short-form video.

## 3. How successful emotional ads/shorts do it in 60-180 s (recurring beats)

### Takeaway
The recurring structure is: ordinary, specific everyday life -> a character the viewer slightly misjudges or doesn't
notice -> small repeated acts or details (often unrewarded) -> a reveal that reframes everything -> a quiet payoff that
restores or honours a bond. A real, specific personal detail (favourite flowers, a dumpling recipe, a home movie)
carries the emotion.

### Cited Findings
- Thai Life Insurance "Unsung Hero" (Ogilvy & Mather Bangkok, ~3 min, dir. Thanonchai Sornsriwichai): a young man does
  small good deeds daily (helps an elderly street vendor, feeds a stray dog, gives money to a begging girl) while others
  disapprove; voiceover notes he gains no fame or wealth; after about two minutes the payoff reveals what he gets is
  happiness and connection (the girl is back in school, etc.). Avoids typical insurance family imagery for small
  everyday stories. — [Laughing Squid](https://laughingsquid.com/unsung-hero-a-heartwarming-commercial-about-a-man-who-changes-lives-by-performing-random-acts-of-kindness/); [FWD:labs](https://fwdlabs.com/blog/the-predecessors-to-thai-life-insurances-unsung-hero); [Brands Vietnam review](https://www.brandsvietnam.com/congdong/topic/323849-lovemark-unsung-hero-song-nhu-mot-ke-dien)
- Unsung Hero results (agency-reported, treat with caution): over 10 million views within two weeks, 17 million at
  four weeks per Cannes Lions case; an earlier write-up cited 6 million YouTube views, 800,000 Facebook shares, 22,000
  tweets in one week. — [Cannes Lions (Love the Work)](https://lovethework.com/work-awards/campaigns/unsung-hero-222662); [Digital Synopsis](https://digitalsynopsis.com/advertising/thai-life-insurance-unsung-hero/)
- Google "Loretta" (Super Bowl LIV, 2020, ~90 s, made in-house): an elderly widower asks Google Assistant to help him
  remember tiny specifics about his late wife (her favourite flowers, favourite movie). The voice is the real
  85-year-old grandfather of a Googler, whose story inspired it. — [Ad Age](https://adage.com/video/loretta-google-super-bowl-commercial-2020); [9to5Google](https://9to5google.com/2020/01/28/google-super-bowl-commercial-2020/)
- Unruly's emotion testing of "Loretta": 49% of viewers had an intense emotional response (58% more intense than the
  average US ad, highest tested); almost 2x as likely to make people smile, almost 4x more heart-warming and 12x sadder
  than the average US ad. — [Unruly](https://unruly.co/?p=36918)
- Loretta reaction was split: many viewers said it moved them to tears while others found it creepy (an AI holding a
  dead wife's memories). — [WBUR Here & Now, 4 Feb 2020](https://www.wbur.org/hereandnow/2020/02/04/googles-super-bowl-commercial)
- Apple "Misunderstood" (holiday 2013, 90 s): a teen looks glued to his iPhone at a family Christmas, seemingly
  ignoring everyone; on Christmas morning he plays the home movie he had been secretly filming of the family. Reframe
  reveal of a misjudged character. — [Engadget](https://www.engadget.com/2013-12-17-apple-airs-new-holiday-ad-misunderstood-featuring-iphone-5s-a.html); [Fortune](https://fortune.com/2013/12/17/apples-merry-christmas-ad)
- "Misunderstood" was reported to win the 2014 Emmy for outstanding commercial (single-outlet report). Ace Metrix scored
  it the most emotional tech ad of that season, though weak on product info. — [iMore](https://imore.com/apple-takes-home-emmy-award-misunderstood-commercial); [Ace Metrix](https://www.acemetrix.com/about-us/company-news/media-coverage/poll-apples-christmas-commercial-is-incredibly-sentimental-and-likeable-but-not-informative-enough/)
- Pixar "Bao": comedy (an adorable living dumpling) then escalating separation as the son grows away, then a reveal
  reframing the dumpling as the real son, then reconciliation over making dumplings together. — [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2018/06/15/619253624/in-pixars-first-female-directed-short-a-dumpling-child-fills-an-empty-nest); [BuzzFeed News](https://www.buzzfeednews.com/article/adambvary/pixar-bao-short-film)

### Inferences
- "Misunderstood" is almost a template for a dev skit: Dev (or Codex) looks cold/distracted/busy the whole episode ("I'm
  busy." "No."), then the reveal shows they were secretly building something for the other character. The clipped
  lines only work *because* the reveal reframes them. In our failed episodes the clipped lines had no reframe, so they
  just read as cold.
- "Loretta" shows that AI-assistant + memory + a very specific human detail is a proven emotional engine, but it also
  shows the creepiness risk: keep the AI as helper/witness to a human bond, not a replacement for one.
- "Unsung Hero" structure (repeat a small unrewarded act 3x, then reveal its effect) fits 60-90 s well: three quick
  repetitions establish the pattern cheaply in animation.

### Gaps
- I did not retrieve structural analyses of "Dear Sophie", Apple "The Surprise", "Piper" or "La Luna" within budget.

## 4. Comedy + emotion (dramedy): landing the heartfelt moment without sappiness

### Takeaway
Experienced TV writers say: build to the emotional beat with comedy, then *stop the jokes* when it turns serious so the
tension isn't released; the only jokes that survive inside the beat are a character's failed attempt to joke through
pain. After the beat has landed, a small callback joke can release it. Restraint and placement (often at the very end)
make it feel earned. Bluey's creator ties its adult tears to real family life.

### Cited Findings
- Jane Espenson (TV writer; Buffy, etc.), 16 Oct 2006: "A good rule of thumb is that when the going gets really
  serious, the joking should stop." "Jokes, generally, undercut emotion. When an audience laughs, they're relieving
  tension." — [janeespenson.com](https://janeespenson.com/102006/how-to-write-jokes-that-arent-funny)
- Espenson on the exceptions, jokes that don't release tension: "When the character himself is joking to try to
  relieve their own tension and it isn't working." "When the character is bitterly self-deprecating." "When the
  character is trying to appease someone who is angry with them by trying to make that person laugh." Her verdict:
  "They're endearing. Heart-crushing. But they're not funny. And they're great." — [janeespenson.com](https://janeespenson.com/102006/how-to-write-jokes-that-arent-funny)
- A sitcom-craft blog notes that Blackadder Goes Forth held back poignancy until the series' final scene, and that
  Seinfeld's writers followed a "No hugging, no learning" rule (i.e. some comedies deliberately refuse sentiment). — [Cheeky Monkey Comedy, "Poignancy in sitcom"](https://www.cheekymonkeycomedy.com/poignancy-in-sitcom)
- Bluey creator Joe Brumm (via Deadline, as reported by AOL): the heart of Bluey comes from his own family's
  interactions; parents will cry more but find more reward and meaning. — [AOL (reporting Deadline interview)](https://www.aol.com/entertainment/bluey-fans-experience-range-emotions-210939224.html); [Inside the Magic](https://insidethemagic.net/2024/04/youll-cry-so-much-bluey-creator-warns-zg1/)
- Brumm reportedly said you're crying "because you love your kids" and because you're tired (attributed by The
  Federalist; original outlet not named, so treat as secondhand). — [The Federalist](https://thefederalist.com/2021/07/07/5-quirky-parenting-lessons-and-2-to-ignore-from-the-hit-animated-series-bluey/)
- Brumm wanted to make something parents could enjoy alongside their kids; Peabody profile frames the show around
  play. — [Peabody Awards](https://peabodyawards.com/stories/bluey-creator-joe-brumm/); [KUOW/NPR "writes for both kids and grown-ups"](https://kuow.org/stories/the-creator-of-bluey-writes-for-both-kids-and-grown-ups)
- A parent's view (NPR) of Bluey's "Sleepytime": "It's just so heartwarming and empathetic, both for the difficulty it
  is for children and for the parents' point of view." (Viewer opinion, not the creator's.) — [NPR, 31 Jul 2023](https://www.npr.org/2023/07/31/1191155197/bluey-tv-show-new-series-disney)

### Inferences
- Order for a 60-90 s skit: comedy setup (jokes, fast) -> the turn (jokes stop, slower, one held silent shot) -> one
  sincere line or action -> a callback joke to release (the channel's "punchline"). Our failed episodes may have put the
  punchline too close to the turn, so the laugh cancelled the feeling before it registered.
- Use Espenson's exception deliberately: Dev cracking a weak joke to hide that he's hurt (and it not working) is a
  powerful in-beat line, much stronger than a terse "No."
- Bluey's lesson for us: the emotion is "seeing both sides" (child and parent). Show the AI's side and Dev's side of the
  same frustration so each audience member recognises themselves.

### Gaps
- Dan Harmon's story circle (You, Need, Go, Search, Find, Take, Return, Change): the Channel 101 wiki page was
  paywalled (HTTP 402), so I could not verify his wording; it is widely known but NOT verified here.
- No interview found where Brumm or Bluey writers explain the craft of "Sleepytime", "Baby Race" or "The Creek"
  specifically.
- No sourced analysis of "earned vs cheesy" beyond Espenson's restraint rule and the placement observations above.

## 5. Dialogue: clipped one-to-three-word exchanges vs fuller lines; subtext

### Takeaway
Craft sources favour subtext (characters not saying exactly what they feel) over on-the-nose lines, but warn that
subtext is not vagueness or withholding: the meaning must be readable underneath. Clipped lines work when the audience
already knows what's underneath them (want, stakes, history); without that, they read as cold and empty.

### Cited Findings
- On-the-nose dialogue = speech with no subtext where a character states exactly what they want/feel; it reads as
  artificial because people rarely say exactly what they think, and it reminds viewers they're watching fiction. — [Industrial Scripts](https://industrialscripts.com/on-the-nose-dialogue/amp); [No Film School](https://nofilmschool.com/on-the-nose-dialogue)
- Final Draft defines subtext as the gap between what a character says and what they mean; on-the-nose dialogue drains
  tension. — [Final Draft, "How to write subtext in dialogue"](https://www.finaldraft.com/blog/how-to-write-subtext-in-dialogue)
- Final Draft: characters often argue about one thing while really fighting about something else; and writers often
  mistake subtext for withholding or vagueness, when it is meaning conveyed beneath the surface. — [Final Draft, "Mastering subtext"](https://www.finaldraft.com/blog/mastering-subtext-in-screenwriting)
- Actions, gestures and pauses can carry feeling instead of stating it. — [Beverly Boy Productions](https://beverlyboy.com/filmmaking/what-is-on-the-nose-dialogue-in-film-writing/)
- Stanton's "well-organized absence of information" and WALL-E's near-silent storytelling show silence works only when
  organised around a clear want. — [LingQ transcript of TED talk](https://www.lingq.com/de/englisch-lernen-online/courses/58847/andrew-stanton-the-clues-to-a-great-st-235924/)

### Inferences
- Rule of thumb for us: a 1-3 word line must have a readable "real meaning" the viewer already holds. "No." is cold;
  "No." after we've seen Codex secretly fixing Dev's bug all night is moving. Clip only after the context is built.
- Use the "argue about one thing, fight about another" device: Dev and Claude argue about tabs vs spaces while the real
  issue is Dev feeling replaced. That gives relatable comedy on the surface and feeling underneath.
- Replace some lines with an action (Codex pushes the commit with Dev's name on it; Claude leaves a comment "you were
  right"). Animation is good at small physical acts.

### Gaps
- No source found that studies terse dialogue specifically or gives a rule for when one-word exchanges feel cold;
  the inference above is mine.

## 6. First-person narrated "storytime" animation

### Takeaway
Storytime animation (autobiographical, usually comedic, recognisable persona, simple animatic-style art) became the
most popular kind of animation on YouTube; scholars credit relatable storytelling and personas. I found no retention
data or audience study explaining *why* narration holds viewers.

### Cited Findings
- Storytime animation is described as short, autobiographical and usually comedic, built around recognisable personas,
  with simple drawing; it is "now not only the most popular type of animation on YouTube based on view and subscriber
  count"; it grew after TheOdd1sOut and JaidenAnimations took off around 2016; low skill barrier let many creators copy
  the format. — [Konstantinos Pappis, "The Odd Ones Out: The Rise of YouTube Storytime Animation", Animation Studies](https://oldjournal.animationstudies.org/?p=1617)

### Inferences
- Narration gives what dialogue-only skits lack: inner thoughts ("I didn't tell anyone I'd been awake for 30 hours"),
  explicit stakes and specific sensory detail, which quickly makes the viewer care. A first-person Dev narrator (in the
  past tense, "This happened last week...") over the skit could solve the "not connecting" problem while keeping the
  characters' lines short.
- Risk: narration can turn into telling instead of showing. Use it for inner state and stakes; let the reveal play
  visually (2 + 2).

### Gaps
- No source found on "That's Not My Dad"-style animated horror storytime Shorts, their length or their retention.
- No measured evidence on narration vs no narration for retention.

## 7. Shorts length since the Oct 2024 3-minute change

### Takeaway
YouTube allows Shorts up to 3 minutes (square or vertical) for uploads from 15 Oct 2024 and said it would improve
recommendations for longer Shorts. Third-party data (mostly marketing blogs) shows retention falls as length rises and
puts view peaks somewhere between 11 and 60 s; no credible dataset isolates 2-3 minute story Shorts.

### Cited Findings
- From 15 Oct 2024, Shorts can be up to three minutes for square or taller videos; earlier uploads not affected;
  YouTube called it a top creator request and said it would improve recommendations for longer Shorts in coming months

### Inferences
- Percentage retention will likely drop if we go from ~35 s to 90 s, but total watch time per viewer can rise. The
  emotional payoff needs enough runway (the ads above use 90-180 s), so 60-90 s looks like a reasonable first test,
  with a hook in frame 0 and an open loop ("but Codex had a secret") so viewers stay for the reveal.
- Compare stayed-to-watch % and average view duration (seconds) of a 60-90 s emotional episode against the 32-40 s
  ones, rather than percentage alone.

### Gaps
- No first-party YouTube data or credible independent study on 2-3 minute story Shorts' retention or reach.
- Most length data comes from marketing blogs with unclear methods; treat as indicative only.
