# AI-assistant and developer skits: who makes them, what hits, and why (2025-2026 landscape)

Method note: view counts, likes, lengths and dates below are snapshots read straight from YouTube page data (watch pages and channel Shorts tabs) on **21 Oct 2026**, except where marked "(channel page, 18 Oct 2026)", which came from channel pages saved earlier in this session. Comment like counts are as YouTube displayed them (rounded, e.g. "32K"). Reddit's API and search pages returned errors from this environment, and TikTok/Instagram/X could not be read, so the findings are mostly from YouTube. Where something couldn't be checked, it says so.

## 1. Which channels make AI-assistant / developer skits right now (format, length, cadence, audience)?

### Takeaway
The big players are **live-action** sketch creators: Alberta Tech (491K subs), Kai Lentit / "Programmers are also human" (401K), Jaden Williams (3.38M, general comedy with frequent AI skits), Leon Si (93.5K) and KRAZAM (189K). The **animated, personified-AI** niche our channel sits in is tiny. Agent Poly has 418 subscribers, and Inner Tab's "Claude & Me" has 11 subscribers. Animated AI-character skits have produced almost no big hits except one Agent Poly outlier, so the competition is thin but the format is unproven.

### Inferences
- Nearly all the reach in this niche comes from **live-action performers playing recognisable workplace roles** (CEO, senior engineer, vibe coder, coworker), with the AI usually offstage or played by a person. ## 2. Which specific videos are the biggest hits, what are their premises, and are they comedy or emotional?

### Takeaway
The top hits are **comedy with a sting**: the AI is incompetent or a power tool, management gets its comeuppance, and the developer's job is under threat. They run 15-51 s in Shorts. The only emotional/existential hit in the animated niche is Agent Poly's **"Prove you're human."**: an AI character fails a CAPTCHA and asks whether he is real. It got about 100x the views of any other Agent Poly episode. Cinematic "AI becomes a villain" parodies (Jaden Williams) mix comedy and dread and do very well.

### Inferences

The episodes that stayed small were either developer-insider gags (semicolons, 19 files, the database called "old") or plain sadness ("am I good enough?").
- The emotional angles that do reach a large audience are **fear and dread** (job loss, "permanent underclass", AI turning villain) and **schadenfreude** (the CEO punished). Tender loneliness skits did not appear among the hits.
- Personified-AI hits (Jaden Williams, KRAZAM) give the AI a **story arc and a stake** (release day, war, being trapped in a prompt), not just a reaction to a bug.

## 5. What does discussion of personifying AI models show about which emotional angles resonate?

### Takeaway
Real attachment to AI models is well documented: the GPT-4o backlash in Aug 2025 and again at its final retirement on 13 Feb 2026 ("He wasn't just a program"), and Claude's following among tech insiders. It is also contested: the top comments on skits mock emotional reliance ("it doesn't have emotions"). For a mass Shorts audience, **AI-as-character with an inner life works when it is epic, ominous or funny-sad** (an AI unsure it's real, an AI being "replaced", a model that "had to go"). Straight companionship or therapy scenes draw pushback.

### Inferences
- The angle with the best evidence is **an AI character's identity and mortality**: being retired or replaced by a newer model (the GPT-4o grief is real and widely known), not knowing if it's real ("Prove you're human."), being trapped in a role (KRAZAM). - Scenes where **the human leans on the AI emotionally** ("I said I was fine. Claude knew better.", "New chat. Who's this?") divide a general audience and draw "touch grass" comments. If used, the human should be the one we feel for, and the AI's limit (memory reset, context window) should be the source of the pathos, not a replacement friend.
