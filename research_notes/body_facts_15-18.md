## 15. The funny bone — `videos/funny_bone.py`
- The "funny bone" is the ulnar nerve where it passes behind the medial epicondyle (the bony bump on the inside of
  the elbow), close to the skin with little padding. A knock sends a shock-like tingle into the little finger and
  half of the ring finger. Repeated tingling without a knock can be ulnar nerve entrapment (cubital tunnel
  syndrome). AAOS OrthoInfo: https://orthoinfo.aaos.org/en/diseases--conditions/ulnar-nerve-entrapment-at-the-elbow/ ;
  Cleveland Clinic: https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/22272-ulnar-nerve-entrapment
- "Humerus = humorous" is a popular, unproven explanation of the name: the script says "Probably".

## 16. Why stomach acid doesn't digest the stomach — `videos/stomach_acid.py`
- Mucus gel from surface mucous cells, with bicarbonate trapped in it, keeps the cell surface near neutral pH;
  tight junctions stop acid slipping between cells; stem cells in the gastric glands replace surface cells.
- Surface epithelium turnover: about every 3-6 days (3-5 most often quoted). Lumen Learning A&P, "The Stomach":
  https://courses.lumenlearning.com/suny-ap2/chapter/the-stomach/ . Script: "about every three to five days".

## 17. Goosebumps — `videos/goosebumps.py`
- The arrector pili muscle at each hair contracts (cold, fear, emotion; sympathetic nerves), pulling the hair up
  and raising a bump. In furry mammals raised fur traps insulating air and makes animals look bigger; in humans the
  warming effect is largely vestigial.
- Hsu lab (Harvard) with Sung-Jan Lin, Cell 2020, in MICE: the sympathetic nerve and the muscle also regulate hair
  follicle stem cells. NIH Research Matters: https://www.nih.gov/news-events/nih-research-matters/what-goosebumps-are
  ; Cell: https://www.cell.com/cell/fulltext/S0092-8674(20)30808-4 . Script says "in mice"; it doesn't call
  goosebumps useless.

## 18. Why you can't tickle yourself — `videos/tickle.py`
- The brain predicts the sensory result of our own movements from a copy of the motor command (forward model,
  thought to involve the cerebellum) and attenuates expected touch. Blakemore, Wolpert & Frith, "Why can't you
  tickle yourself?", NeuroReport 2000 (doi 10.1097/00001756-200008030-00002); Blakemore et al., Nature
  Neuroscience 1998.
- Robot experiment: participants moved a lever, a robot arm stroked their palm; no delay felt least ticklish, a
  short delay made it feel more ticklish (it no longer matched the prediction). Yale Scientific:
  https://www.yalescientific.org/2015/05/why-cant-you-tickle-yourself/
