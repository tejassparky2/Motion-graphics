# Sketch comedy craft for 30-90 s vertical dialogue skits (2-3 recurring characters)

Scope note: researched 18 Oct 2026. Sources are sketch/improv teaching texts (UCB-derived material, Mike Trapp, Ian Roberts, David Zucker, Keith Johnstone), comedy-timing research (2011 and 2026 papers), creator interviews (Complex, Embedded, Rolling Stone, NPR/Fresh Air, Upworthy) and creator-marketing blogs (flagged as weak where used). Several requested creators (Krazam, Joel Haver, Caleb Hearon's character videos, Agent Poly Studios, Fireship, ThePrimeagen, Kai Lentit, Aaron Jack, Zach King, Brittany Broski, The Dutchess, Luke Davidson, Ian Boggs, Cas van de Pol, Guy Posting) returned no usable craft interviews in this pass; see Gaps in each section. "Consensus" below means three or more independent sources agree; "single opinion" means one practitioner.

## Key question 1: What do sketch authorities say about structure and heightening (game of the scene, beats, rule of three, buttons)?

### Takeaway
Consensus across UCB-derived teaching, Second City alumni and SNL-style how-tos: a sketch is ONE unusual thing ("the game") stated in one sentence, hit in beats that each get "more" (specifics, emotion, or stakes), with everything around the game kept grounded, and an exit as soon as the peak lands. The rule of three is the standard beat count for short pieces, but writers explicitly allow breaking at two or stretching to four.

### Cited Findings
- Definition (UCB-derived, Hoopla Impro): the Game of the Scene is "a repeated pattern of funny behaviour with escalating emotion"; the "Unusual Thing" that appears after the who/what/where is "the seed from which your beautiful Game of The Scene will grow"; each Heighten (more absurd) is followed by an Explore (make sense of it) so "each new level has a solid base"; on ending, "editing after a big laugh is a good shout" — [Hoopla Impro, A quick guide to Game of the Scene](https://www.hooplaimpro.com/quick-guide-game-of-scene)
- UCB framing questions as taught by secondary sources: heightening = "If this is true, then what else is true?"; exploring = "If this is true, then why is it true?" — [Graeme Writes, Anatomy of a scene](https://graemewrites.substack.com/p/anatomy-of-a-scene); [Wikipedia, Game of the scene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Game_of_the_scene) (notes the game is escalated by raising the stakes, taking a character's actions to the extreme, or placing them in more absurd situations)
- Will Hines (UCB teacher): "Rather than simply following the scene wherever it may go, you're looking for a game to play." "The term 'game' is purposefully broad. It can encompass anything you find funny." "Playing game is not the same as naming the game." Post-scene test: "Once we found a game, did we play it, or just run past it?" He suggests naming a game as, among other formats, a single representative line of dialogue or an action/reaction pair — [Will Hines, Name the game seven ways](https://willhines.substack.com/p/name-the-game-seven-ways)
- Three heightening levers (The Free Association, London improv school): "Heighten the details of the Game to make it more surprising" (specifics), "Heighten how much your character cares about the Game" (emotion, same behaviour but bigger feeling), "Heighten how much the world cares about the Game" (stakes). Heightening is "a bit of a moving target": "Do the comedy again, but this time, make it more", and going SMALLER can heighten (a cashier who IDs someone for chewing gum). Raised stakes do not require a new location. Levers combine; if specifics stall, raise emotion or stakes — [The Free Association, Easy ways to heighten game](https://www.thefreeassociation.co.uk/blog/easy-ways-to-heighten-game)
- Mike Trapp (sketch writer, Chuffah): "A sketch is a short, self-contained scene focused on one unusual thing that heightens to comedic absurdity." "The game is the single pattern of unusual behavior that is the focus of the sketch." "you shouldn't need more than a single sentence to describe the game." Beat rhythm: "You hit the game, the characters react, you hit the game again, the characters react again." "In each beat of the game the unusual behavior should get more exaggerated than the one before it." The target is "moments where the unexpected meets the inevitable," but "Repetition breeds predictability and comedy relies on being unpredictable," so vary beats with misdirects (a false victory or brief pattern break), timing changes (shorten gaps, or "rest the game"), and exploration (logically consistent world details). Length advice is for stage sketches ("3-5 minutes"), not Shorts — [Mike Trapp, The Basics #1: What Is A Sketch and How Does It Work?](https://chuffah.substack.com/p/the-basics-1-sketch-and-game)
- Rule of three (Trapp): pattern, pattern, break is "often effective," but you can break at the second item or add a fourth; he cites a Simpsons scene with a "broken" rule of threes using four items. (The fetch summarised his wording as "things are funnier in threes"; treat as paraphrase.) — [Mike Trapp, How to make your lines as funny as your pages](https://chuffah.substack.com/p/how-to-make-your-lines-as-funny-as)
- Rule of three + "get out" (Jason Hellerman, No Film School): establish who/what/when/where/why immediately, "the first weird thing" should occur "as soon as the setup is done," return to the game three times for laughs then wrap up, and on endings: "there's an old joke about how no one knows how to end a sketch, so just get out as quick as you can while still going big." Pitfalls: getting into the story too slowly (you have "maybe one page" for setup), and being caught outside the game with no direction. SNL sketches run about five minutes max — [No Film School, How to Write Sketch Comedy Like SNL](https://nofilmschool.com/how-to-write-sketch-comedy)
- Five-beat sketch skeleton (anonymous how-to, weak mirror domain, single source): setup; second beat where the odd thing happens again but bigger; third beat where it causes a real problem; "Blowout" = "the height of the absurdity"; "Blackout" = "the final joke and the exit" — [How To Write A Sketch](https://m.fightcancer.org/write-sketch)
- Ian Roberts (UCB co-founder, Key & Peele executive producer): keep everything except the joke grounded: "Everything but the game. Everything but the jokes." "make everything else as what you expect, and then the joke stands out in stark contrast." His job on K&P: "cutting out everything that's not related to the game, keeping it tight, and not meandering." He hires writers who "know how to find the game." The interviewer (not Roberts) credits K&P's quality to "heightening and tight closing buttons" — [The Comic's Comic, Ian Roberts on running Key & Peele](https://thecomicscomic.com/2013/09/18/ian-roberts-on-running-one-of-the-really-good-tv-sketch-comedies-key-peele/)
- Keegan-Michael Key on joke anatomy: "the setup always makes you start to assume what direction you think the joke is going in"; the most important part is "all the things that you're supposing and all the things that you're assuming when you set up the joke. And then how are you going to turn it on the back end?"; the ideal ending (citing Aristotle) is "both unexpected and inevitable"; a "hard joke" has "a real definitive setup and a real definitive punchline" — [WUSF/NPR Fresh Air, Keegan-Michael Key breaks down how he sets up a joke](https://www.wusf.org/2023-10-10/keegan-michael-key-breaks-down-how-he-sets-up-a-joke)
- Key & Peele deliberately avoided catchphrases and (mostly) recurring characters; Jordan Peele's thesis for the first sketch ("Phone Call"): sketch relies on conflict while real people avoid it, and the sketch "maximizes that tendency" — [Los Angeles Magazine, 3 comedy sketches that changed Key and Peele's lives](https://lamag.com/tv/3-comedy-sketches-that-changed-key-and-peeles-lives/)
- Heightening pace (Alex Baia quoting Mike Lacher, McSweeney's editor): heightening is "expanding your premise in new and surprising ways that still connect coherently to what you've set up earlier," avoiding "jumping so far ahead that it feels contrived"; opening with the most absurd beat fails (a Seinfeld cannibalism gag would be "too absurd to find funny" first); repeating the same joke type with small upgrades stalls; the Portlandia "Colin the Chicken" sketch works through "three big moves"; sketches "take us somewhere absurd very quickly" so they have fewer beats than longer pieces — [Comedy Bizarre, How to make your humor heighten](https://comedybizarre.substack.com/p/how-to-make-your-humor-heighten)
- Trapp on pace: "Exaggerate too quickly and you'll have nowhere to go for the rest of the sketch." "Heighten too slowly and your audience will be bored." "Sketches without at least some element of exploration can feel mechanical and predictable." — [Mike Trapp, The Basics #1](https://chuffah.substack.com/p/the-basics-1-sketch-and-game)
- Scott Dikkers (The Onion): every joke runs a hidden "subtext" through one of 11 "funny filters" (Irony, Character, Shock, Hyperbole, Wordplay, Reference, Madcap, Parody, Analogy, Misplaced Focus, Metahumor); worked example: the Character filter gives "God, an expert on miracles, is decidedly unimpressed by childbirth," the Irony filter gives "Miracle Of Birth Occurs For 83 Billionth Time" — [Big Think, Every joke falls in one of these 11 categories](https://bigthink.com/high-culture/every-joke-falls-in-one-of-these-11-categories-according-to-the-founder-of-the-onion/); [Shortform summary of How to Write Funny](https://www.shortform.com/pdf/how-to-write-funny-pdf-scott-dikkers)
- Kathy Griffin's account of advising Chris Farley's "van down by the river" team: don't start from the ending; build from the beginning so the sketch has somewhere to go, and don't fixate on chasing the one big laugh (second-hand via a quotations page; treat as anecdote) — [Goodreads, SNL quotes](https://www.goodreads.com/quotes/tag/snl)

### Inferences
- For a 30-90 s skit, the stage skeleton compresses to: line 1-2 establish base reality AND the unusual thing; beats 2 and 3 heighten by one lever each (specifics, then emotion or stakes); blowout; one-line blackout. That is four to six exchanges total, so each beat must be a single exchange, not a scene.
- "Everything but the game" (Roberts) is the most useful rule for a dev + two AI assistants show: the office, the bug, the deadline should read as normal; only the assistants' one defining behaviour is weird, and it gets "more" each beat.
- Since UCB-style heightening can go smaller as well as bigger (Free Association), an over-eager AI assistant escalating from "I also refactored your whole repo" DOWN to "I also renamed your cat" is a valid heighten.

- Could not access the UCB Comedy Improvisation Manual's own heightening chapter (only its table of contents was indexed), so "If this is true, what else is true?" is sourced to secondary teaching material.
- No Second City-authored structure text surfaced; Key's Second City background (Coach Hines) is the only link.
- No primary source defines "button"/"blow" as sketch terms; the beat skeleton that names "Blowout/Blackout" is an anonymous page on a mirror domain.

## Key question 2: What do successful short-form skit creators say about hooks, endings, length, series and recurring characters?

### Takeaway
The few on-record creators say three things consistently: the format is 2-4 characters in roughly 60-90 s; recurring characters and running gags are a "deal" with the audience that lets you skip setup; and the material comes from real, specific situations the creator actually lived. Direct statements about how they write endings were not found.

### Cited Findings
- Jane Wickline (TikTok sketch creator, 2022 interview): her format is "The two-to-four character short sketch format"; she writes sketches in a Google Doc and "color code[s] the little characters," plans shots and props, pulls backgrounds from Google Images; "there's very, very low production value"; "I spend quite a bit of time writing them"; on how the audience narrows a channel: "You realize that you now have a much more specific account than you did a month ago." — [Embedded, Jane Wickline on taking comedy TikTok to the next level](https://embedded.substack.com/p/jane-wickline-on-taking-comedy-tiktok)
- Complex feature on TikTok "sitcom" creators: Vienna Ayla's sketches run about 90 seconds; she builds recurring characters with signature bits (her "knows a guy" character keeps familiar beats across settings) and running gags (breakaway glasses); on the implicit contract: "We're asking them to suspend disbelief" and viewers "enter into this little deal with your audience"; Joey Mulinaro starts from "where would these characters be" if their shows were still airing; Tra Rags resists being locked into one persona ("even though you're asking for this character, I'm going to create something completely different") and says creators who "hold on to something" lack confidence; marketing professor Jenna Drenten: "the laugh track of today is the likes, comments, shares, and stitches on TikTok"; comment sections keep debating the plot after each sketch — [Complex, TikTok comedy sitcoms: Tra Rags, Joey Mulinaro, Vienna Ayla interview](https://www.complex.com/pop-culture/a/alec-karam/tiktok-comedy-sitcom-tra-rags-joey-mulinaro-interview)
- Druski (Rolling Stone, 2024): "My big thing on getting comedy material and stuff is it comes from real life situations." "Some of my best work is from real-life moments and being around these types of people." Skits grow from riffing with friends ("We would joke around and talk about standing on business for a minute before we even shot that skit"); on trends: "I don't feel like I created the stand on business thing, I think we heightened people using it." — [Rolling Stone, Druski on creating his viral skits](https://ca.rollingstone.com/en/music/druski-on-creating-his-viral-skits-and-standing-on-business)
- Jordan Howlett ("Jordan the Stallion"): records most videos in one take; on authenticity: "It really is me," "I don't have the bandwidth to try to put a mask on in any way"; on relatability: "I'm just happy that people can relate to that." His signature opener is the "come here" zoom (described by the writer, not explained by him) — [Upworthy, Jordan the Stallion interview](https://www.upworthy.com/tiktok-star-jordan-the-stallion-shares-new-batman-role-and-the-secret-to-his-addictive-videos/); [Wikipedia, Jordan Howlett](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jordan_Howlett)
- Keegan-Michael Key on recurring characters: Coach Hines (created at Second City) is "an amalgam of a bunch of coaches from my childhood"; "my most successful characters are these really angry characters." — [WUSF/NPR](https://www.wusf.org/2023-10-10/keegan-michael-key-breaks-down-how-he-sets-up-a-joke)
- Joel Haver (background only, no interview found): weekly rotoscoped skits of roughly 5-10 minutes built on "dry humor and awkward characters"; he plays multiple roles; his 2020 how-to video on the style led many others to copy it — [Plugged In, Joel Haver channel review](https://www.pluggedin.com/youtube-reviews/joel-haver/); [Wikipedia, Joel Haver](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joel_Haver)
- Krazam (background only): Brooklyn duo Shiva Kilaru and Benjamin Burke; sketches blend absurdist humour with tech anxiety and corporate satire; a MetaFilter commenter says the "Senior Engineer" video is one they think of before job interviews (evidence the in-group material sticks) — [TV Tropes, KRAZAM](https://www.tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/WebVideo/KRAZAM); [MetaFilter thread](https://www.metafilter.com/190652/Your-vibes-are-not-compliant-with-festival-policy)
- Shorts-marketing guidance on hooks/endings (weak, uncited blogs, but consistent with each other): "The moment the payoff is delivered, the video should end. Do not add a traditional outro, end screen, or lingering call to action." — [Subscribr, Scripting YouTube Shorts](https://subscribr.ai/p/scripting-youtube-shorts-engagement); end with a punchline, a twist, or "loop back to the beginning (to encourage rewatching)" — [Skool, Tailoring social media skits](https://www.skool.com/pressplay-cinema-skool-4134/tailoring-social-media-skits); one promotional case study: a Short "always hit completion, but it never grew" because "our ending was a dead stop"; adding "a subtly different 0.5 second 'button' at the end (a quick callback to the hook)" so the loop "felt like an intentional choice" meant "Rewatches soared. Distribution followed" (no numbers given); also "Your opener is not an intro. It's a contract," and stick to 2-3 repeating formats for 30 days before evolving — [Deeside.com promotional feature, The Shorts recommendation loop](https://www.deeside.com/the-shorts-recommendation-loop-a-practical-map-of-signals-you-can-actually-influence/); warning that "Misleading hooks, delayed answers, and artificial loops may produce a short-term metric change while frustrating the audience" — [GoFaceless, YouTube Shorts retention strategy](https://www.gofaceless.ai/fr/blog/youtube-shorts-retention-strategy); one blog claims comedy performs best at 18-25 s (no data shown) — [Opus.pro, Ideal Shorts length](https://www.opus.pro/blog/ideal-youtube-shorts-length-format-retention)

### Inferences
- The recurring-character "deal" (Ayla) is what makes 30-90 s possible: once viewers know the tired dev and the two assistants, line 1 can already be the unusual thing. New characters cost setup time you do not have.
- Rags's point cuts the other way: a recurring persona must still surprise, which matches the channel's existing rule to vary setting and format each time.
- Druski's and Howlett's "real life" emphasis suggests sourcing each skit from a real dev-tool moment (an actual over-eager refactor, an actual hallucinated API) rather than a generic "AI is dumb" premise.
- Ending on the payoff with no outro is the one point every Shorts source agrees on; the 0.5 s callback-to-hook button is a single, promotional anecdote and should be A/B tested, not assumed.

- No creator interview found that explains how they choose or write the last line of a skit.

## Key question 3: What is the evidence on comedic timing: pauses before punchlines, buttons, last line, repetition and callbacks?

### Takeaway
Practitioner consensus (Dean, Trapp, Zucker) is: put the reveal word at the very end of the line, pause for the laugh, cut on the laugh not after it, and use a punchline as the setup for a tag. Empirical evidence on pre-punchline pauses is mixed: a 2011 study of 20 jokes found no pause effect, while a 2026 study of 828 stand-up sets found pauses lengthen before high-surprise punchlines and timing predicts laughter better than content. No study gives a validated pause duration; the "500-800 ms" figure circulating online is unsourced.

### Cited Findings
- Greg Dean's joke model (via Scotty Meltzer): Setup -> Assumption ("anything that the setup makes the audience expect"; Dean's "First Story"/"Decoy Assumption") -> Connector ("the thing in your setup that is perceived in two ways"; "Connectors don't have to be words") -> Shatter/Turn (Dean's "Target Assumption"/"Second Story", the "Reinterpretation") -> Punch ("the part the audience laughs at"). Tags: "take any punch in your show and use it as a setup" for a tag — [Juggle.org, Be Funnier with Scotty Meltzer: Nice Structure](https://www.juggle.org/be-funnier-with-scotty-meltzer-nice-structure/)
- Dean on pauses (course marketing copy, so treat as his emphasis rather than data): people "generally mess up their pauses and deliver their punch-lines too quick" — [Greg Dean Stand-Up Comedy Classes](https://gregdeanstandupcomedyclasses.teachable.com/p/humor4biz); Wikipedia cites Dean's book for pausing "long enough for the laughs to start to die down before going on to the next joke or set-up line" — [Wikipedia, Comic timing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comic_timing)
- Punch-word placement (Trapp): put the reveal word at the end of the line, before a natural pause: "Let your punchlines punch. End strong!"; a strong word mid-line "splits the laugh" into several small ones; "Subvert expectations as quickly as you set them" (the next line after the setup is the longest reasonable distance); "Use fewer words: 'nuff said"; "Make your wording and grammar consistent" (parallel structure sets a pattern you can break); pick the funnier-sounding word ("goose" over "swan") — [Mike Trapp, How to make your lines as funny as your pages](https://chuffah.substack.com/p/how-to-make-your-lines-as-funny-as)
- David Zucker (Airplane!) rules: "Cut on the laugh, not after it." "it's easier to keep an audience laughing than to have them stop and start them up again." "Let the lines do the work." "you don't acknowledge the joke." Avoid "a joke on a joke." "Comedy depends on surprise." "It's funnier if you don't put it up front." "Your first draft will be too long." "A joke has to happen, and then it's over." — [How I Write, David Zucker explains](https://howiwrite.substack.com/p/david-zucker-airplane-writer-explains)
- Null result: Attardo & Pickering (2011), "Timing in the Performance of Jokes," Humor 24(2), 20 recorded joke performances: "speakers do not significantly raise or lower their speech rate at and around the punch line"; on the claim that punch lines are preceded by pauses, "Our data show no evidence supporting this claim"; "we found no differences between prepared and spontaneous jokes" — [Attardo & Pickering 2011, abstract](https://lair.etamu.edu/chssa-faculty-publications/8)
- Positive result: Ma, Peng, Lyu, Zhang & Zhu (2026 preprint), "Timing is Everything: Temporal Scaffolding of Semantic Surprise in Humor," 828 professional Chinese stand-up performances: "temporal features substantially outweigh semantic incongruity in predicting audience appreciation"; "peak semantic violations matter more than average incongruity levels"; "pauses systematically lengthen before high-surprise punchlines" (no durations or effect sizes in the abstract) — [arXiv 2605.00143 via papers.cool](https://papers.cool/arxiv/2605.00143)
- Stillness: the TIC-TALK multimodal stand-up dataset (2026) reports a negative correlation (r = -0.75) between performer movement and audience laughter across 24 topics, flagged by the authors as correlational with confounds — [arXiv 2603.21803, TIC-TALK](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.21803)
- Unverified number: a commercial article claims pauses of "500-800 millisecond" give the strongest laughs; it cites no study — [Bottega del Sarto opinion piece](https://www.shop.bottegadelsarto.com/opinion/comedy-timing-techniques-experts-swear-by-feel-counterintuitive-368366) (do not rely on it)
- Key: the ideal ending is "both unexpected and inevitable" — [WUSF/NPR](https://www.wusf.org/2023-10-10/keegan-michael-key-breaks-down-how-he-sets-up-a-joke)
- Ending on the laugh in improv: "editing after a big laugh is a good shout" — [Hoopla Impro](https://www.hooplaimpro.com/quick-guide-game-of-scene)
- Final-line "knockout" (R. Brandon Andersen): a strong final line or twist makes a piece memorable; also his "four-line setup": who, what and where clear within the first four lines; "This is the day that these characters grow in some way." — [Andersen, 3 sketch comedy tips](https://www.goodreads.com/author_blog_posts/3989568-3-sketch-comedy-tips-that-writers-should-use)

### Inferences
- For synthetic voices the "pause" research matters less than the "end on the reveal word" rule, which the TTS can honour exactly: write the surprise as the last word of the line, follow it with a full stop and a longer gap, and never add a trailing clause.
- "Peak violation matters more than average incongruity" (Ma et al.) supports the sketch rule of one big blowout rather than evenly funny lines.
- Callbacks are the sketch version of Dean's tag: the final line should re-use a connector from line 1 so it is a punch and a loop point at once.

### Gaps
- No study gives an optimal pause length for comedy; nothing at all on pauses in synthetic-voice comedy.
- No research found on callbacks or repetition counts (rule of three) as such; these remain practitioner consensus.
- Judy Carter's The Comedy Bible and Jerry Corley's material were not surfaced by the searches run.

## Key question 4: Straight man vs fool, status play, archetypes and consistency

### Takeaway
Johnstone's status framework is the standard account: comedy comes from status being raised and lowered between characters, and getting status right lets "the scene play itself." On the straight man, practitioner opinion splits: classic advice is that the foil plays everything dead serious so the fool stands out (Zucker, Roberts), while Robin Thede argues straight men should also get jokes.

### Cited Findings
- Keith Johnstone (Impro): "Status is the thing that you do all the time. It's the way that you're relating to other people." "Every inflection and movement implies a status." "One of the best ways to raise your own status is to lower the other person's." "Playing high status is saying 'Don't touch me.' Playing low status is saying 'Don't hurt me.'" "If you get the status right, you can relax and the scene will play itself." — [Antoine Buteau, Lessons from Keith Johnstone](https://www.antoinebuteau.com/lessons-from-keith-johnstone/)
- Academic review of Johnstone: he "suggests that all comedy is dependent on characters lowering and raising their own status and that of the other characters," and that a good play "reverses status between characters"; the reviewer thinks he "overstate[s] the case" — [Communication and Theater Association of Minnesota Journal review](https://d.lib.msu.edu/caj/31/OBJ/download)
- Play it straight: Zucker: "Spoofs work best when actors play it like they don't know they're in a comedy." "Let the lines do the work." — [How I Write, Zucker](https://howiwrite.substack.com/p/david-zucker-airplane-writer-explains); Roberts: K&P's cast "really commit to the acting" rather than "ironic detachment," and he criticises the "I just say the funny thing and ... act like myself" performer — [The Comic's Comic, Ian Roberts](https://thecomicscomic.com/2013/09/18/ian-roberts-on-running-one-of-the-really-good-tv-sketch-comedies-key-peele/)
- Counter-view (single opinion, low-quality aggregator page): Robin Thede holds that foils should still get laughs and rejects the idea that straight men should never have jokes — [Robin Thede believes everybody should get jokes](https://interakt.integrations.sell.do/top-news/robin-thede-believes-everybody-should-get-jokes.html) (could not verify the original outlet)
- Characters built from one trait: Key's Coach Hines is "an amalgam of a bunch of coaches"; his most successful characters are "really angry characters" — [WUSF/NPR](https://www.wusf.org/2023-10-10/keegan-michael-key-breaks-down-how-he-sets-up-a-joke)
- Heightening via emotion keeps the behaviour the same and raises how much the character cares (the lawyer goes from calm request to "sobbing on the floor" with the request unchanged) — [The Free Association](https://www.thefreeassociation.co.uk/blog/easy-ways-to-heighten-game)
- Character wants drive subtext: "Knowing what your characters want also drives subtext to their actions." — [Andersen](https://www.goodreads.com/author_blog_posts/3989568-3-sketch-comedy-tips-that-writers-should-use)

### Inferences
- In a dev + two AI assistants cast, the natural status map is: the human has nominal high status (he gives orders) but low actual status (he is tired and the tools run him); the assistants are low status in posture ("Happy to help!") and high in power. The comedy beat is each reversal. Johnstone's "best way to raise your own status is to lower the other person's" predicts the funniest assistant line is one that is polite AND quietly demotes the dev.
- Two fools need differentiation by one trait each (e.g. one over-does, one over-asks) so their lines are never interchangeable; this is an inference from the "one defining trait" examples (Coach Hines, Red Shirt), not a sourced rule.
- The straight man should carry the emotional beat (the place the viewer feels the tiredness) in one quiet line near the end before the blow; sources on where to put the emotional beat in a 60 s skit were not found, so this is an inference from "This is the day these characters grow" (Andersen) and the heighten-emotion lever.

### Gaps
- No source explicitly maps straight man/fool onto Johnstone's status terms; that link is inference.
- No source found on the ideal placement of an emotional beat inside a sub-90-second comedy piece.

## Key question 5: Animated skit channels: attachment to characters, varying vs repeating formats, templated feel

### Takeaway
Only weak, commercial sources address this directly: they claim a clear comedic identity and emotional clarity drive return viewing, and that iterating character interactions from comment feedback works. Practitioner creators (Rags, Wickline) warn that repeating a persona unchanged signals a lack of confidence and that audiences push an account toward ever-greater specificity.

### Cited Findings

Jaiden Animations: "emotional depth and clear pacing increase viewer attachment to your content." ## Key question 6: Relatability and in-group humour: insider jokes vs accessibility; does specificity beat generality?

### Takeaway
Practitioner material agrees that comedy should come from specific, lived situations, and niche creators (nurses, teachers, developers) thrive on "unit-specific" detail; the balancing rule from Roberts and Zucker is that the specificity goes in the game while the surrounding scene stays universally readable, and jokes that depend on trivia should be cut. No controlled evidence that specificity beats generality was found.

### Cited Findings
- Druski: material "comes from real life situations"; "Some of my best work is from real-life moments and being around these types of people." — [Rolling Stone](https://ca.rollingstone.com/en/music/druski-on-creating-his-viral-skits-and-standing-on-business)
- Nurse creators: humour creators "turn the realities of hospital life into relatable, hilarious content that helps nurses decompress, laugh, and feel seen"; Nurse John's POV skits range "from unit-specific humor to spot-on impressions of coworkers, patients, and hospital systems" — [Advantis Med, Top nurse creators 2026](https://advantismed.com/blog/top-nurse-creators-to-follow)
- Teacher comedy as a movement: Portland's "The Teacher Show" of working educators is "part of a growing movement in comedy, where often beleaguered working professionals like nurses and teachers poke fun at their day jobs" — [Connecticut Public/NPR, Teachers in Oregon have found a solution for burnout](https://www.ctpublic.org/2024-04-20/teachers-in-oregon-have-found-a-solution-for-burnout-stand-up-comedy)
- Tech in-group evidence (anecdotal): a viewer says Krazam's "Senior Engineer" is the video they think of before job interviews — [MetaFilter](https://www.metafilter.com/190652/Your-vibes-are-not-compliant-with-festival-policy)
- Accessibility guardrails: Zucker: "Avoid jokes that depend on trivia" and drop anything "past its prime" — [How I Write, Zucker](https://howiwrite.substack.com/p/david-zucker-airplane-writer-explains); Roberts: keep "everything but the game" as what the audience expects so the joke "stands out in stark contrast" — [The Comic's Comic](https://thecomicscomic.com/2013/09/18/ian-roberts-on-running-one-of-the-really-good-tv-sketch-comedies-key-peele/)
- Audiences narrow an account: Wickline: "You realize that you now have a much more specific account than you did a month ago." — [Embedded](https://embedded.substack.com/p/jane-wickline-on-taking-comedy-tiktok)
- Premise definition that fuses the two: a premise is "a specific, relatable situation pushed to an illogical extreme" (anonymous how-to, weak source) — [How To Write A Sketch](https://m.fightcancer.org/write-sketch)

### Inferences
- Rule of thumb for dev humour: the SITUATION must be legible to a non-dev in one second (a tired person asking a helper for one small thing), the DETAIL can be insider (the helper rewrites the whole repo, invents a library, asks seventeen clarifying questions). The insider detail is the heighten, not the premise.
- Avoid jokes that need a specific tool version or this month's model name (Zucker's trivia and staleness rules); they age within weeks.

### Gaps
- No empirical study found comparing specific vs general humour on retention or sharing.
- No interview found with a developer-comedy creator (Programmers Are Also Human, Fireship, ThePrimeagen, Kai Lentit) about balancing insider and general audiences.

## Key question 7: How dialogue should sound when read by synthetic voices (line length, rhythm, pauses)

### Takeaway
Vendor documentation and TTS-writing guides converge: the full stop is the most reliable pause control, break tags are limited (3 s max, unstable when overused, unsupported in ElevenLabs v3/v4 which use audio tags and punctuation instead), and ellipses read as hesitant. For comedy, the deadpan mismatch between flat delivery and absurd content is itself a tool.

### Cited Findings
- ElevenLabs: `<break time="x.xs" />` gives pauses "up to 3 seconds"; "Using too many break tags in a single generation can cause instability" (speed-up, noise, artifacts); dashes give short pauses and ellipses "hesitant tones" but are "less consistent"; in Eleven v3/v4 break tags are NOT supported and you control pauses with audio tags (`[short pause]`, `[long pause]`), punctuation and text structure; ellipses "add pauses and weight" (example `[sigh] …`); voices trained with filler sounds handle pauses differently — [ElevenLabs docs, Pauses](https://elevenlabs.io/docs/product/prompting/pauses)
- "a full stop is the strongest way to make AI take a break; if a sentence feels rushed, break it into two"; commas give a softer pause — [Async, How to make AI voices sound better](https://async.com/blog/how-to-make-ai-voices-sound-better/)
- Paragraph breaks (blank lines) produce a slightly longer pause than a sentence end — [Narakeet, Add pauses to TTS voiceovers](https://narakeet.com/docs/how-to/add-pauses-to-text-to-speech-voiceovers.html)
- TTS comedy mechanics (opinion, small vendor blog): humour from incongruity when "a synthetic voice reads a tragic confession or a mundane word with the same flat energy, and the gap is the payoff"; deadpan robot is the safe default, a cheerful voice over bleak content is the sharper alternative — [Voxbooster, Funny text speech](https://voxbooster.com/blog/funny-text-speech)
- Line-level writing rules that transfer directly to TTS (Trapp): reveal word last, fewer and shorter words, cut parentheticals and dependent clauses ("Don't give them pieces they won't use"), parallel wording for pattern-then-break — [Mike Trapp, How to make your lines as funny as your pages](https://chuffah.substack.com/p/how-to-make-your-lines-as-funny-as)

### Inferences
- For this pipeline (one take per sentence with a real stop between them), the script itself is the timing instrument: one idea per sentence, the punch word last, a question mark where the straight man is incredulous, and a paragraph/beat gap before the blow. Do not stack two jokes in one sentence; the voice cannot "throw away" a clause the way an actor can.
- The 2026 timing study's "pauses lengthen before high-surprise punchlines" suggests giving the blow line a slightly longer pre-gap than ordinary lines, but no duration is validated; use the channel's existing reference-video rhythm and test.
- Over-eager AI assistant voices can exploit the Voxbooster mismatch: relentlessly cheerful tag on a line that is objectively a disaster for the dev.

### Gaps
- No study or practitioner source on pause length or line length specifically for synthetic-voice comedy; vendor docs cover mechanics only.
- No source on how many words per line keep a TTS take intelligible at Shorts pace.

## Key question 8: Known pitfalls

### Takeaway
Practitioner consensus on failure modes: explaining the joke, stacking jokes ("joke on a joke", "joke grasping"), heightening too fast or too slow, meandering off the game, ironic or mugging performances, and running past the laugh instead of cutting on it. For Shorts, the added pitfalls are outros after the payoff and forced loops that frustrate viewers.

### Cited Findings
- Explaining: "Give them only half the equation" (Trapp) — [Chuffah](https://chuffah.substack.com/p/how-to-make-your-lines-as-funny-as); "Humour is very much about surprise and intellectual leaps. So every time you painstakingly set up a gag, you're draining all the humour and surprise out of it." and "the more detail you give with a punchline the less effective it is" (forum practitioners) — [British Comedy Guide forum](https://www.comedy.co.uk/forums/post/717371/); Zucker: "you don't acknowledge the joke" — [How I Write](https://howiwrite.substack.com/p/david-zucker-airplane-writer-explains)
- Too many jokes: "joke grasping" breaks scene momentum — [British Comedy Guide forum](https://www.comedy.co.uk/forums/post/899750/); Zucker: avoid "a joke on a joke"; a strong word mid-line "splits the laugh" (Trapp)
- Heightening pace: "Exaggerate too quickly and you'll have nowhere to go"; "Heighten too slowly and your audience will be bored" (Trapp); opening on the most absurd beat is "too absurd to find funny" (Lacher via Baia) — [Comedy Bizarre](https://comedybizarre.substack.com/p/how-to-make-your-humor-heighten)
- Off-game material: Roberts cuts "everything that's not related to the game"; Hellerman: being "caught outside the game with no direction" — [No Film School](https://nofilmschool.com/how-to-write-sketch-comedy)
- Weak performance of the straight role: Roberts on "ironic detachment" vs committing; Zucker: play it like you don't know you're in a comedy
- Ending late: "Cut on the laugh, not after it" (Zucker); "editing after a big laugh is a good shout" (Hoopla); Shorts: no outro after the payoff — [Subscribr](https://subscribr.ai/p/scripting-youtube-shorts-engagement); forced loops/delayed payoffs frustrate viewers — [GoFaceless](https://www.gofaceless.ai/fr/blog/youtube-shorts-retention-strategy)
- Starting from the punchline: reverse-engineering a setup from a funny word "usually produces a forced, clunky setup" audiences notice (stand-up writer) — [Funny How, Give an elevator pitch for your jokes](https://funnyhow.substack.com/p/give-an-elevator-pitch-for-the-joke); Griffin's advice to Farley's team not to start from the ending — [Goodreads SNL quotes](https://www.goodreads.com/quotes/tag/snl)
- Bad premise: a well-built sketch can still fail because "your premise might suck" (Trapp); "If you don't make them laugh, the audience will feel like they've gotten ahead of you." — [Chuffah](https://chuffah.substack.com/p/the-basics-1-sketch-and-game)
- Trivia and staleness: "Avoid jokes that depend on trivia"; drop what is "past its prime" (Zucker)

### Inferences
- Practical checklist for a 40-60 s dev + two assistants skit, derived from the above: (1) line 1 is base reality plus the unusual thing, in one sentence each; (2) three beats, each ONE exchange, each heightened by a different lever; (3) nothing funny from the dev except his reactions, which carry the feeling; (4) one blowout where the assistants' behaviour peaks (peak violation); (5) a one-line blackout that re-uses a word from line 1 (callback = loop point); (6) cut on that line, no outro; (7) every line ends on its reveal word with a full stop; (8) no joke that needs a product name or this week's news.

### Gaps
- Most pitfall sources are practitioner opinion (forums, Substacks, one Zucker interview); no measured evidence on, e.g., how much explaining costs in retention.
