# Close the expressiveness gap without cloning Mayank

Our narrator already matches the reference on pace (about 3.3 words a second), sentence pauses and tonal balance; what it lacks is variability, and description wording will not supply it. In pitch measurements made for this report, Indic Parler-TTS's "Rohit" stayed between **3.8 and 5.1 semitones of pitch standard deviation** across twenty takes, whether the description said "calm", "expressive and animated" or the top training-bin phrase "very expressive and animated". The same model's "Aman" speaker, asked for a slightly high pitch, reached **6.3-8.0 st SD and an 18.8-26.0 st p5-p95 range**, inside the band of the four reference clips (6.5-7.7 st, 20.1-24.2 st), but its Whisper word error rate on the same passage was **33-51% against Rohit's 20%**, so that range currently costs clarity. The dependable route is therefore to keep Rohit's clear articulation and widen its contour after generation: a log-domain pitch expansion in Praat of about 1.5x above the median and 1.3x below should land near SD 6.2 st and a 20 st range, with accents on one or two focus words per sentence supplying the rest. The loudness gap (a 7.2 dB speech spread against 9.3-9.9 dB) is largely self-inflicted by a compressor fast enough to flatten single syllables; a gentle 2:1, 20 ms/200 ms setting taking only 2-4 dB off the loudest words, plus the same focus-word accents, should recover it. The owner's "1:1 to Mayank" is the one target to drop: Indian courts in 2024-26 have enjoined AI imitation of a person's "voice and style" and "signature oratorical cadence", the 2026 IT Rules treat misrepresenting someone's voice as prohibited synthetic content, and YouTube, Meta, ElevenLabs and Sarvam all bar it, so targets should be genre-level and delivered in a voice listeners do not recognise as his. Whether he narrates with a licensable stock voice can be settled with an email to his business address, ElevenLabs' Voice Library audio search and our existing Resemblyzer ranking script, but only a match that stands clearly apart from the field counts; adopting the nearest-sounding voice would be the sound-alike route. Reddit could not be reached, our pitch tests cover one 68-word passage with two or three seeds per setting, and several thresholds below are reasoned starting points that are labelled as such.

## A 1:1 copy of one named creator is the one target we cannot ship

The owner asked for Mayank's "voice and style 1:1". Indian law turns that from an ethics question into a concrete legal one. Personality rights in India are judge-made, resting on *Rajagopal*, *Puttaswamy* and a line of Delhi High Court cases rather than a statute ([Cyril Amarchand Mangaldas](https://disputeresolution.cyrilamarchandblogs.com/2026/05/personality-rights-in-india-in-the-age-of-ai/)). Since the Bombay High Court's July 2024 order restraining AI voice-conversion tools from imitating Arijit Singh ([SpicyIP](https://spicyip.com/2024/08/synthetic-singers-and-voice-theft-bomhc-protects-arijit-singhs-personality-rights-part-i.html)), the courts have kept widening protection: the Delhi High Court held in October 2025 that using AI to imitate Kumar Sanu's **"voice and style"** without consent infringes his personality rights ([BananaIP](https://www.bananaip.com/intellepedia/?p=145874)), extended protection to podcaster Raj Shamani's voice in November 2025, which shows digital creators qualify, not only film stars ([SCC Online](https://www.scconline.com/blog/?p=367436)), and in May 2026 restrained misuse of Shashi Tharoor's **"signature oratorical cadence and manner of speaking"** ([The Week](https://www.theweek.in/news/india/2026/05/10/delhi-high-court-directs-removal-of-shashi-tharoors-deepfake-video-from-social-media-sites.amp.html)). Regulation now backs the courts. The IT Amendment Rules 2026, in force since **20 February 2026**, list misrepresenting a person's voice as prohibited deceptive synthetic content and cut takedown time for court- or government-flagged content to **3 hours** ([MediaNama](https://www.medianama.com/2026/02/223-meity-amendments-it-rules-synthetic-media-deepfakes/)). Two caveats keep this honest. Most of these are interim or ex parte orders, and every one involved identifiable impersonation through clones, deepfakes or misuse aimed at a known person, not a stranger narrating in a generically energetic style ([Cyril Amarchand Mangaldas](https://disputeresolution.cyrilamarchandblogs.com/2026/05/personality-rights-in-india-in-the-age-of-ai/)). Copyright does not protect "mere imitation of a recorded performance" ([Midler v. Ford summary](https://www.casebriefs.com/blog/law/intellectual-property-law/intellectual-property-keyed-to-merges/state-intellectual-property-law-and-federal-preemption/midler-v-ford-motor-co/)), and India's performers' rights cover performances, not persona ([GNLU CSEL](https://gcsel.gnlu.ac.in/post/echoes-without-consent-ai-sound-alike-performances-moral-rights-and-india-s-legal-vacuum)). This is policy information, not legal advice.

Platforms and providers draw the same line. YouTube lets a person request removal of AI content that "looks or sounds like you" and weighs whether they are uniquely identifiable, which can be by voice ([YouTube privacy guidelines](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/7671399)); its impersonation policy reaches copying a creator's branding where "the intent to imitate is clear" and warns that AI disclosure "is not a free pass to impersonate" ([YouTube impersonation policy](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/2801947)). ElevenLabs bans impersonating a living person's voice without consent and falsely implying affiliation ([ElevenLabs Use Policy](https://elevenlabs.io/use-policy)), and Sarvam requires the rights to any cloned voice ([Sarvam licensing](https://docs.sarvam.ai/api/getting-started/commercial-licensing)). None of this touches a designed voice reading original scripts. YouTube says disclosure alone **won't limit a video's audience or its eligibility to earn money** ([YouTube disclosure help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/14328491)), and Meta requires a label on organic posts with "realistic-sounding audio that was digitally created" ([Meta Newsroom](https://about.fb.com/news/2024/02/labeling-ai-generated-images-on-facebook-instagram-and-threads/)), so the cheap course is to label every Reel and tick YouTube's synthetic-content box.

The working line is therefore identifiability and association. Words per minute, pause lengths, pitch range, loudness targets and an EQ curve are properties of the energetic Hinglish-explainer genre; timbre, signature phrases and vocal tics are identity. Two features of our own project make the margin thinner than it looks, and this is our inference rather than a legal finding. The channel README already borrows his format (poster-paper cut-outs, one big red key word, a "Socho..." opener and a comment-prompt close modelled on his reels), so a voice that also lands on his cadence makes "is this Mayank's second channel?" a fair question for a listener. And every delivery target so far, including the EQ that `voice_match.py` derived, was measured on his clips alone. The fixes are cheap: re-derive the targets from three to five Hinglish explainer narrators so they are demonstrably genre-typical; keep his clips strictly as measurement inputs (never a TTS reference clip, a conversion target or fine-tuning data) and retire them once the genre benchmark exists, as the guardrail checklist advises; log each voice's model, description, seed and date as provenance; and before launch play ten to twenty listeners a blind clip asking "do you recognise this voice?". The sentence for the owner is: we can match the genre's energy and pacing, but not his voice or his signature identity.

## Wording cannot widen Rohit, and Aman's wider range costs clarity

### The training vocabulary exists, but Rohit does not respond to it

Parler-TTS learned its controls from auto-generated labels. Each acoustic feature was binned into a short phrase, and **utterance-level pitch standard deviation is the feature behind the monotone-to-animated label** ([Lyth & King 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01912v1)), which is exactly the statistic we are trying to move. Data-Speech v0.2, the annotation pipeline behind Parler, has five expressivity labels running "very monotone", "monotone", "slightly expressive and animated", "expressive and animated", "very expressive and animated", seven-step pitch and rate scales that include "slightly high-pitch", "fast" and "very fast", and quality phrases topping out at "very clean" and "very close-sounding" ([Data-Speech v0.2 text bins](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/huggingface/dataspeech/main/examples/tags_to_annotations/v02_text_bins.json)). The Indic Parler card confirms expressivity, pitch and rate are controllable, recommends Rohit and Divya for Hindi, and advises "very clear audio" for best quality, but its emotion labels (Narration, Happy, Surprise and others) are officially supported only for ten languages that **do not include Hindi** ([Indic Parler-TTS model card](https://huggingface.co/ai4bharat/indic-parler-tts)). The ceiling was always a risk: the paper reports that control works only "fairly well" ([Lyth & King 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01912v1)), the numeric SD threshold for each label is not published ([v0.2 bin edges](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/huggingface/dataspeech/main/examples/tags_to_annotations/v02_bin_edges.json)), and whether Indic Parler used v0.2's exact bins is unconfirmed.

Our measurements say the ceiling binds for Rohit. We ran pYIN (librosa 1.0, 65-500 Hz, semitones relative to each take's median, voiced frames only) over the 68-word voice-test passage. Rohit's earlier design grid, which crossed "calm, clear and slightly expressive" against "clear, engaging and expressive" and a moderate against a slightly high pitch at seeds 3 and 11, measured **SD 4.2-4.8 st and range 13.4-15.3 st**. Today's test run of the exact bin phrases at seeds 3, 11 and 42 measured SD 4.2-4.8 st and range 12.9-15.0 st for the original "calm" baseline, **4.1-4.5 st and 13.2-14.1 st for "expressive and animated"**, and **3.8-4.4 st and 12.1-13.7 st for "very expressive and animated"** with "high-pitch"; adding "very fast" gave 4.3-5.1 st and 14.1-17.8 st across its three seeds, the 5.1 being the liveliest of twenty Rohit takes. (Our pYIN settings differ slightly from the test script's own, so its printout may differ by a few tenths.) The wording change is invisible in the numbers, and dropping "calm" did not help either; only seed luck moved the needle, and not far. Use the bin phrases where they read cleaner, but expect no pitch gain from wording alone while the speaker is Rohit.

### Aman reaches the reference band, but at a clarity cost

The same grid run with Parler's "Aman" speaker produced the variability Rohit could not. At a moderate pitch Aman measured SD 4.9-6.3 st and range 15.9-19.8 st; asked for "a slightly high pitch", it measured **SD 6.3-8.0 st and range 18.8-26.0 st**, against 6.5-7.7 st and 20.1-24.2 st for the four 25-second reference excerpts measured the same way. The pitch request also lifted Aman's median from about 99-108 Hz to 135-153 Hz, close to Rohit's 145 Hz and to the 134-175 Hz spread of the reference clips. Two cautions follow. Seed matters a great deal for Aman (seed 3 against seed 11 moved SD from 6.8 to 8.0 st and from 6.3 to 7.7 st), so per-line seed selection is part of the method, not an extra. And Aman is not on the card's recommended list, and the clarity check suggests why. Scored with Whisper medium against the passage text, the four slightly-high-pitch Aman takes had word error rates of **33%, 39%, 46% and 51%** (a moderate-pitch take reached 57%), against **20%** for a Rohit take scored the same way. Our simple token-level WER has a high floor on Hindi because of spelling variants, so only the comparison matters, and it says Aman's wide contour currently comes with slurred or unstable articulation. The interpretation we draw, an inference, is that each named speaker carries the expressivity of its own training recordings and the description adjusts around that baseline; the speaker is the coarse control and the adjectives the fine one. Aman stays worth a cheap side test, because per-line seed selection gated on clarity might find usable takes and the card's "very clear audio" phrasing was not in those descriptions, but it is not a drop-in replacement. Both speakers also passed the distinctiveness check: Rohit scored 0.74 and Aman 0.73-0.76 in Resemblyzer similarity to the reference clips, the same level as unrelated stock voices (0.728 in our earlier test) and far below his 0.97 self-similarity. Named speakers are built from the model's CC-BY training data, so using either is the licensed-stock route, and the channel should credit the model and datasets.

### Log-domain pitch expansion of about 1.5x is the dependable route

With Rohit kept for clarity, DSP has to do the widening. The documented Parselmouth route is Praat's Manipulation object: extract the pitch tier, modify it, put it back and resynthesise with overlap-add ([Parselmouth docs](https://parselmouth.readthedocs.io/en/stable/examples/pitch_manipulation.html)). Praat's one-call "Change gender" has a pitch-range factor, but open-source code using it scales in hertz around the median ([Amphion data_augment.py](https://huggingface.co/spaces/halobang/maskgct/blob/main/processors/data_augment.py)), which over-raises peaks relative to troughs; a pitch-tier formula of the form m·(f/m)^k multiplies every semitone deviation by k instead. Rubberband, which our pipeline uses for tempo, shifts pitch globally and is the wrong tool for reshaping a contour. Artefacts rise with the modification factor: TD-PSOLA degrades at large factors and depends on accurate pitch marks, and F0-modified resynthesis can introduce echo and buzzy fricatives ([ATR TR-IT-0170](https://results.atr.jp/35th/techreport/TR-IT-0170/TR-IT-0170.pdf); [DAFx 2004](https://dafx.de/paper-archive/2004/P_307.PDF)). No listening test we found puts a number on a safe range-expansion factor.

Measured consistently, the arithmetic is simpler than the brief's figures implied (they suggested 1.5x for SD but 1.8x for range). Measured per take with one tracker, Rohit averages SD 4.4 st and range 14.5 st, and the reference averages 7.1 st and 22.7 st, so both statistics call for k of about **1.55-1.6**, and the two distributions have the same shape (range about 3.2 times SD on both sides). A uniform 1.6 would push Rohit's 5th-percentile troughs from about 7 st to about 11.6 st below a 145 Hz median, roughly 74 Hz, where a TTS voice tends to turn creaky. The safer setting is asymmetric, about 1.5 above the median and 1.3 below; our estimate is that this lands near **SD 6.2 st and range 20 st**, the reference's low end, with focus-word accents supplying the rest. We also checked the worry that the reference's two-octave span might be pitch-tracker error under its music bed: removing frames that jump more than 7 st away from a 0.1 s running median discarded at most 0.3% of reference frames, so isolated octave errors are not inflating it, although sustained errors and bed leakage are not ruled out without a separated vocal stem. No published norm for Hindi narration F0 range exists to sanity-check either number.

### Emphasis and pauses finish the job

Parler has no SSML; its only prosody handles are the description and punctuation, where commas add short pauses ([Indic Parler-TTS model card](https://huggingface.co/ai4bharat/indic-parler-tts)). Emphasis therefore has to be added after generation, on the word timings `narrate.py` already produces for on-screen text. Our starting values come from general practice, not a Hindi study, so treat them as an A/B grid: +2 to +4 dB on one or two focus words per sentence with 10-20 ms ramps, a local +2 st pitch peak on the same span, 10-25% lengthening of the stressed vowel, and a 150-300 ms pause before a reveal. Accents do double duty, adding pitch tails a global stretch cannot add safely and widening the loudness spread; expanding pitch without them tends to sound wobbly rather than energetic. Pace and sentence pauses already match: lines are stretched to 3.3 words a second, and the 0.43 s and 0.53 s gaps plus about 0.07 s of trimmed edge give roughly 0.50-0.60 s against the reference's 0.5-0.6 s. One conflict needs fixing: `pace()` squeezes every internal silence longer than 0.3 s down to 0.24 s, which would erase a deliberate pre-reveal pause, so marked pauses should be exempt. Log the rubberband tempo ratio per line as well: Aman's design takes of the same passage ran from 18.6 to 26.5 s against Rohit's 22.9-24.3 s, so its natural pace varies more and the 0.85-1.35 clamp may bind on some lines.

## A syllable-speed compressor is erasing about 2.5 dB of contrast

Tonal balance is solved; dynamics are not. The reference pairs a tightly held phrase level (LRA 2.4 LU over 3 s windows) with lively syllables (9.3-9.9 dB speech spread), the signature of slow levelling plus gentle peak control. Our chain does the opposite, running a 3:1 compressor from −22 dB with a 5 ms attack and 70 ms release, which acts at syllable speed on TTS that is already "unnaturally consistent" in level ([Sonarworks](https://www.sonarworks.com/blog/learn/parallel-processing-techniques-for-ai-generated-vocals)). Engineering guidance on speech points the same way. Sound On Sound warns that on spoken voice "even relatively small amounts of compression can be very obvious" and favours 2:1-3:1 with limited gain reduction ([SOS](https://www.soundonsound.com/techniques/recording-spoken-voice)); iZotope defaults to "low ratios, medium time constants" catching no more than a dB or two ([iZotope cheat sheet](https://www.izotope.com/community/blog/vocal-cheet-sheet)) and a 2:1 start for podcasts ([iZotope podcast guide](https://www.izotope.com/community/blog/podcast-production-guide-part2)); Audacity suggests 100-300 ms release for voice ([Audacity](https://www.audacityteam.org/features/compressor/)); and a narrator who stacked processing found commanding passages turned "robotic" ([Adobe community](https://community.adobe.com/t5/audition-discussions/my-voice-sounds-robotic-after-adding-very-few-effects/td-p/12870553)).

The fix is a gentler insert: **ratio 2, attack 20 ms, release 200 ms, RMS detection**, with a threshold that takes only 2-4 dB off the loudest words. At 2:1 the gain reduction is half the overshoot, so a threshold 6 dB below the 95th-percentile short-window RMS of the loudest words yields about 3 dB. Keep `alimiter` at `level=false`, because its auto-level defaults to on (confirmed in the local FFmpeg 8.1.1 help), and keep its gain reduction to 1-2 dB. Test bypassing `afftdn`: clean TTS has little noise to remove, and our own code notes that a stronger setting already dulled the 5-9 kHz range. Our expectation, which needs measuring, is that the compressor change alone lifts the spread from 7.2 dB to roughly 8.5-9.5 dB, with the accent layer covering the rest.

Saturation fills a different gap, since a long-term EQ match cannot add overtone density. Small amounts fatten a voice and help it cut through ([Waves](https://www.waves.com/essential-steps-for-mixing-lead-vocals)), while exciters add presence but brighten quickly ([iZotope](https://www.izotope.com/community/blog/4-ways-to-add-augment-or-excite-upper-harmonics)). Two FFmpeg forms parse on our build: an insert that drives `asoftclip` by 2 dB after the compressor, or a parallel branch blended at about 20%, in line with Sonarworks' 15-30% guidance for AI vocals, which was written for singing ([Sonarworks](https://www.sonarworks.com/blog/learn/parallel-processing-techniques-for-ai-generated-vocals)). No study ties saturation amount to a perceived "studio narrator" quality, so treat it as optional, A/B it, and re-run `voice_match.py` afterwards because saturation shifts the spectrum. For delivery, aim at **−14 LUFS integrated with true peak at or below −1 dBTP**; YouTube's −14 is third-party measurement rather than an official spec, and Instagram publishes no target and applies client-side loudness metadata ([Fora Soft](https://www.forasoft.com/learn/audio-for-video/articles-audio/lufs-targets-per-platform-2026); [Meta Engineering](https://engineering.fb.com/2023/04/11/video-engineering/high-quality-audio-xhe-aac-codec-meta/)). Use two-pass `loudnorm` and confirm the JSON reports linear normalisation, or its own AGC will undo the dynamics we restored. Matchering is not a shortcut: it matches whole-mix RMS, frequency response and peak level for music masters, not compression character ([Matchering](https://github.com/sergree/matchering)). Compare spreads only with the same window, gating and source on both sides, since a music bed fills pauses and narrows the low percentiles, and treat LRA on clips under a minute as indicative ([EBU Tech 3342](https://tech.ebu.ch/publications/tech3342)).

## One audio search and a decision rule settle the stock-voice question

Nothing public names Mayank's voice or tools. His Instagram bio lists business, science, history and psychology, about 64K followers and a business-enquiries address, with no voice credit, and web searches surfaced no interviews or tutorials about the channel ([Instagram](https://www.instagram.com/meanwhilemayank/)). That leaves three legitimate routes. The cleanest is to ask him through that business address, a plain licensing question. The second is ElevenLabs' Voice Library search, which accepts an uploaded audio or video file and shows **"the original voice used... if it exists in the Voice Library"**, otherwise similar voices; it wants speech without music or noise, and long clips are not required ([ElevenLabs Voice Library docs](https://elevenlabs.io/docs/eleven-creative/voices/voice-library)). Isolate the vocal first and use two to four clean 10-30 s segments; private, removed or custom voices, including a creator's clone of himself, only ever return "similar" results ([Seedance guide](https://www.seedance.tv/blog/elevenlabs-find-voice-from-video)). This uploads his audio for identification, not cloning, so keep the clips private and never send them through an instant-clone flow.

The third route is our own ranking, which `find_stock_voice.py` already implements: it lists male Hindi library voices from `/v1/shared-voices`, downloads each one's Hindi preview and ranks them by Resemblyzer similarity to his clips. The endpoint needs a key, since unauthenticated requests returned "You must be logged in to use filters", and voice objects carry per-language preview URLs ([ElevenLabs shared-voices API](https://elevenlabs.io/docs/api-reference/voices/voice-library/get-shared)). Two extensions are worth adding: a pass without `language=hi` (for example `accent=indian`), because Hinglish channels often run non-Hindi-tagged voices through multilingual models, and a re-score of the top five after generating the same Hinglish line in each. Usage data says where to look first: Bunty "Reel Perfect Voice" ranks third of about 1,265 Hindi library voices, Viraj "Energetic, Clear Narrator" generated 957.6M characters in a year, and Raunak M bills itself as a "Viral & Relatable Reel Voice" ([JSON2Video Hindi list](https://json2video.com/ai-voices/elevenlabs/languages/hindi/); [Viraj](https://json2video.com/ai-voices/elevenlabs/voices/FmBhnvP58BK0vz65OOj7/); [Raunak M](https://json2video.com/ai-voices/elevenlabs/voices/oHxj8sUpVpscBK7Mmroq/)). Detectors only narrow the provider. ElevenLabs' Audio Detector checks for its own watermark, falls back to a classifier, and returns a yes/no on ElevenLabs origin, never a voice name ([Audio Detector](https://elevenlabs.io/docs/eleven-creative/audio-tools/audio-detector); [Classifier vs Detector](https://elevenlabs.io/docs/help-center/legal/audio-detector/what-is-the-difference-between-the-eleven-labs-ai-speech-classifier-and-the-new-audio-detector-tool)). Timing makes it slightly more useful here, since ElevenLabs says no audio made before June 2026 carries SynthID ([ElevenLabs help](https://help.elevenlabs.io/hc/en-us/articles/48099714323857-Is-my-audio-being-watermarked-right-now)) and his reels date from 16-27 October 2026, but whether the watermark survives Instagram's re-encode is unverified, so a negative result means little. Sarvam, Azure and Google offer no audio search; their candidates can only be generated and scored the same way.

The decision rule is where this goes right or wrong. In our earlier test his own clips scored about **0.97** against each other while the best free Microsoft voice reached **0.728** (recorded in `channels/hinglish/README.md`). A genuine stock match should stand clear of its neighbours by a visible gap and hold up when the same line is regenerated; previews are short and may use another model, so the right voice can score below 0.97 but should not sit in a cluster. If nothing separates, the likeliest explanations are his own voice or a private clone, and there is nothing to license. The tempting next step, adopting whichever voice scored highest, is the sound-alike route the guardrails rule out, because it selects a voice for resemblance to one person. Even a confirmed, licensed match carries residual risk: his exact voice on a channel that already borrows his look invites the "intent to imitate" reading of YouTube's impersonation policy and ElevenLabs' bar on implied affiliation. If a match is found, using it commercially needs at least the Starter plan ($6 a month, which adds the commercial licence), because the free plan is non-commercial and library voices are not available through the API on the free tier ([ElevenLabs pricing](https://elevenlabs.io/pricing); [Voice Library docs](https://elevenlabs.io/docs/eleven-creative/voices/voice-library)). The same Resemblyzer tooling doubles as a guard on our own voice: as we tune, its similarity to his centroid should stay where unrelated voices score, not climb toward his self-similarity.

## If Parler tops out, license expressiveness rather than borrow it

If Rohit plus DSP and accents stalls below about 6 st SD or the processing becomes audible, and no Aman takes pass the clarity gate, buy expressiveness from a licensed engine and measure it with the same script. Sarvam's bulbul:v4-flash, launched 13 October 2026, is the only engine found with named Hindi documentary, story and suspense personas such as `anand_hi_documentary`, `ratan_hi_documentary`, `shubh_hi_stories_dramatic` and `mukul_hi_suspense`, plus `_enhi_` Hinglish voices, with pace 0.5-2.0 and pitch −0.5 to 0.5 but no temperature or SSML ([Sarvam voices](https://docs.sarvam.ai/api-reference-docs/api-guides-tutorials/text-to-speech/voices); [Sarvam v4-flash guide](https://docs.sarvam.ai/api/api-guides-tutorials/text-to-speech/best-practice-guide-for-bulbul-v-4-flash)). It costs **₹20 per 10K characters** (₹10 promotional through 12 November), assigns output rights to the user and allows commercial use even from free credits ([Sarvam pricing](https://www.sarvam.ai/api-pricing); [Sarvam licensing](https://docs.sarvam.ai/api/getting-started/commercial-licensing)). ElevenLabs' v4, launched around 28 September 2026 with Hindi and stackable audio tags ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/09/28/elevenlabs-new-v4-speech-model-supports-more-expression-control-and-90-languages/)), takes tags such as `[excited]` and `[curious]`, but its speed tops out at 1.2x and pacing follows each voice's source recording ([ElevenLabs prompting guide](https://elevenlabs.io/docs/best-practices/prompting/eleven-v3)); its Hindi page lists Bunty, "Punchy, Crisp and Reel King", among popular voices ([ElevenLabs Hindi](https://elevenlabs.io/text-to-speech/hindi)). Google's Gemini TTS is generally available for hi-IN and takes a free-text style prompt plus tags like `[extremely fast]` ([Gemini-TTS docs](https://docs.cloud.google.com/text-to-speech/docs/gemini-tts)), and Azure's MAI-Voice-2.1 offers hi-IN Dhruv and Arjun with an `excited` style but is a preview "not recommended for production" ([Azure MAI-Voice](https://learn.microsoft.com/azure/ai-services/speech-service/mai-voices)). Choose among them on measured SD and range, Whisper clarity and Hinglish handling, never on similarity to him. Locally, Indic-Speak permits commercial use and offers Hindi voices Amit and Kavya with no cloning, but its style field is "not yet consistent enough to rely on" and its 3.78B parameters need 4-bit quantisation for a 4 GB card ([Indic-Speak](https://huggingface.co/bodhan-ai/indic-speak)); IndicF5, XTTS-v2 and Chatterbox inherit prosody from a reference clip, so they are usable only with our own synthetic reference ([IndicF5](https://huggingface.co/ai4bharat/IndicF5)).

The highest-fidelity way to get human prosody without taking anyone's voice is a consenting performer converted into our designed voice. F0-following conversion such as RVC passes the performer's intonation, timing and loudness through while replacing timbre; RVC trains on about ten minutes of clean speech, claims to run "even on poor graphics cards" and uses the RMVPE pitch extractor ([RVC README](https://github.com/RVC-Project/Retrieval-based-Voice-Conversion-WebUI/blob/main/docs/en/README.en.md)). The target would be 15-60 minutes of our own Parler voice, so the output timbre is synthetic. Licensing needs a check, because RVC is described as MIT but a hosted copy of its licence adds a research-use caveat ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Retrieval-based_Voice_Conversion); [licyk/rvc-model](https://huggingface.co/licyk/rvc-model)), and the alternative, Seed-VC, is GPL-3.0 and was archived on 21 November 2025 ([Seed-VC](https://github.com/Plachtaa/seed-vc)). ## Conclusion and ranked recommendation

The request framed the job as copying a person; the measurements frame it as restoring variability the pipeline removed. A stock speaker whose delivery sits near the monotone end, a compressor that flattened syllables and a pace routine that trims internal pauses each pulled toward monotony, while the description wording we suspected turned out not to matter. In Indic Parler the speaker sets the expressive range and the adjectives barely move it, and the one speaker that reaches the reference's range pays for it in clarity, so the dependable path is to keep the clear speaker and add the movement afterwards, where every decibel and semitone is under our control and measurable. That has a useful consequence for the ethics as well as the engineering: the delivery traits that read as "his style" (wide pitch swings, punched focus words, quick but breathing pace) are exactly the genre-generic ones we can legitimately match, while the traits that make him identifiable, timbre and signature phrases, are the ones we neither need nor may take. The ranked plan below follows that logic, cheapest and most certain first, with each step checked against the same measurements before the next. The compressor goes first because it is a one-line change and later listening tests should not be judged through a chain that flattens every accent.

The measurement used throughout this report, to run identically on our takes and on any genre benchmark clips (research venv, which already has librosa 1.0):

```python
import numpy as np, librosa, scipy.ndimage as ndi

def f0_stats(path, fmin=65, fmax=500):
    y, sr = librosa.load(path, sr=22050, mono=True)
    f0, voiced, _ = librosa.pyin(y, fmin=fmin, fmax=fmax, sr=sr, frame_length=1024, hop_length=256)
    f = f0[voiced & ~np.isnan(f0)]
    st = 12 * np.log2(f / np.median(f))                  # semitones re the take's own median
    st = st[np.abs(st - ndi.median_filter(st, size=9)) < 7]   # drop octave-type jumps
    p5, p95 = np.percentile(st, [5, 95])
    return {"sd": st.std(), "range": p95 - p5, "median_hz": float(np.median(f))}
```

The pitch-expansion step for rank 2 slots in between the raw Parler file and `pace()`. It needs `pip install praat-parselmouth` in the Parler venv and is untested here, so check the `Formula` and `Stylize` commands on one file first.

```python
import parselmouth
from parselmouth.praat import call

def widen(src, dst, k_up=1.5, k_down=1.3, floor=75, ceil=400, stylize_st=None):
    snd = parselmouth.Sound(src)
    man = call(snd, "To Manipulation", 0.01, floor, ceil)
    m = call(snd.to_pitch(pitch_floor=floor, pitch_ceiling=ceil), "Get quantile", 0, 0, 0.5, "Hertz")
    pt = call(man, "Extract pitch tier")
    if stylize_st:                                  # variant: expand only the phrase-level contour
        call(pt, "Stylize", stylize_st, "Semitones")
    call(pt, "Formula", f"if self > {m} then {m}*(self/{m})^{k_up} else {m}*(self/{m})^{k_down} fi")
    call([pt, man], "Replace pitch tier")
    call(man, "Get resynthesis (overlap-add)").save(dst, "WAV")
```
