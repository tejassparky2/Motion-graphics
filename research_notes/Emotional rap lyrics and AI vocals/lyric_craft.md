## 1. Prosody: stress on strong beats, line length, phrase balance, rhyme types and stable vs unstable

### Takeaway
Pattison's whole system is one test: does every element (stress placement, line length, line count, rhyme scheme,
rhyme type) support what the line *means*? Resolved, certain feelings get stable structure (matched line lengths, even
line counts, perfect/family rhyme, ABAB/AABB); longing, doubt, dread and grief get deliberately unstable structure
(a shortened or extra line, odd line counts, ABBA or asymmetric schemes, assonance/consonance rhyme, an unrhymed line).

### Cited Findings
**Stress on beats**
- Pattison's core rule: "match each with their own type, stressed notes with stressed syllables, unstressed with unstressed"; a mismatch sounds unnatural and pulls attention away from the emotion — [Pat Pattison, "Looking for the Perfect Match"](https://www.patpattison.com/perfect-match)
- Stressed syllables are higher, louder and longer. Multi-syllable words have a fixed dictionary stress. One-syllable *content* words (nouns, verbs, adjectives, adverbs) are stressed. Function words (prepositions, articles, conjunctions, auxiliaries, pronouns) are normally unstressed, but any of them takes stress when it carries a contrast — [patpattison.com/perfect-match](https://www.patpattison.com/perfect-match)
- In 4/4, beats 1 and 3 are stronger than 2 and 4, and the same strong-weak pattern holds at every subdivision ("architectonic"). An anticipation (a note just before the beat) can carry stress. Put the most important words on the strongest positions. Avoid "greedy" settings (stressed syllables on weak beats, which sound hurried) and "overwrought" ones (unnatural stresses that lose the listener). Example pair: "bridegroom" (strong-weak) cannot swap with "come back" (weak-strong) in the same melody slot — [patpattison.com/perfect-match](https://www.patpattison.com/perfect-match)
- When "you" is contrasted with "I", both pronouns are stressed, and "you" should land on a strong beat/downbeat so the contrast registers — [Pattison, "POV & Verse Development"](https://www.patpattison.com/pov)
- Berklee example of a deliberate mis-setting: on John Mayer's "Waiting for the Avalanche", co-writer Keppie Coutts had stressed the normally weak word "for". Pattison, Coutts and Mayer tried to fix it, and Mayer kept it: "There's prosody in this missetting." Coutts teaches that verbs usually take stress, compound words stress the first syllable, and pronouns and words ending in -ty or -ly are often mis-stressed in songs; rules can be broken on purpose — [Berklee Now, "Berklee Riffs: Natural Music in Language"](https://www.berklee.edu/berklee-now/news/berklee-riffs-natural-music-in-language)

**Line length, line count, phrase roadmaps**
- Line length is counted in *stressed* syllables, not total syllables. Matched lengths create an expectation ("Since line 3 mimics line 1, we expect line 4 to mimic line 2"). Mismatched lines "feel unstable and tell us to keep going, though it's not entirely clear where." Stable means closure and confidence; unstable means suspense, longing or disappointment. A section that breaks off and returns to its opening lines can itself express a character who can't finish the story — [Pattison, "Structure Creates Expectations"](https://www.patpattison.com/structure-creates-expectations)
- An *unrhymed* line acts as a "target": it tells the ear a match is still coming and throws extra weight onto the key word (often the title) when it arrives ("sonic targeting") — [patpattison.com/structure-creates-expectations](https://www.patpattison.com/structure-creates-expectations)
- Pattison (Sound On Sound, Mar 2022): "Whatever you are saying, all of the elements should support it." For a song about longing: "You can use an unstable rhyme scheme — ABBA, for example — or a stable rhyme scheme like ABAB"; "use more remote rhyme types, assonance rhyme or consonance rhyme"; "You could use an even number of lines and, say, shorten your fourth line"; an asymmetrical rhyme scheme or an odd number of lines also unsettles — [Sound On Sound, "Pat Pattison: Writing Better Lyrics"](https://www.soundonsound.com/node/4927197)
- Lyric and melodic phrase "roadmaps" must agree: "Creating compatible roadmaps melodically and lyrically is essential"; "If you try to follow both maps, you'll end up not knowing where you are." Fixes: change the melody, change the lyric, repeat the last word of one line at the start of the next, or hold a note across the division. Mismatched roadmaps "diminish the impact of the ideas" — [Pattison, "Lyric & Melodic Phrases"](https://www.patpattison.com/lyric-and-melodic-phrases)
- Berklee's Coursera course (Pattison) teaches judging a lyric stable/unstable from line lengths, number of lines, stressed/unstressed syllables and melody — [search snippet only: Coursera "Songwriting: Writing the Lyrics"](https://www.coursera.org/learn/songwriting-lyrics)

**Rhyme types and their emotional weight**
- Pattison's scale from most to least stable: perfect, family, additive, subtractive, assonance, consonance. Perfect = same stressed vowel and everything after (cat/hat); family = same vowel with phonetically related final consonants (cat/hack). Perfect rhyme "stops motion fully and slams the gate shut"; "As you move to less stable rhyme types, the rhyme will open the gate further, creating uncertainty and longing" (Pattison in American Songwriter, as quoted). Perfect, assonance and consonance are older terms; family, additive and subtractive are Pattison's own — [Song Cage, "Rhyme Schemes for Songwriters" (Sep 2026)](https://songcage.com/blog/rhyme-scheme/). The original American Songwriter column was not located.
- Berklee Online's prosody article: perfect and family rhyme suggest stable/resolved meaning; assonance suits unstable/unresolved meaning. Definitions given there: family = matching vowels plus related consonants (mud/truck, love/blush); assonance = matching vowels with unrelated consonants (read/cheap, life/tide) — [search snippet only: Berklee Online, "Prosody in Music and Songwriting"](https://online.berklee.edu/takenote/prosody-in-music-and-songwriting/) (the page returned 503 when opened). Note: one uploaded copy of Pattison material defines family rhyme differently; Berklee's wording is the one to trust.
- Additive rhyme = the second word adds consonant sound after the shared vowel; subtractive = it drops one. The less sound added or removed, the closer to perfect. Consonance (same final consonants, different vowels) is the weakest/most tense — [search snippet only: secondary summaries, e.g. Goodreads/flashcard pages for *Essential Guide to Rhyming*](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/22654633). [Uncertain: secondary.]
- Sound fabric beyond end rhyme: internal rhyme, assonance of stressed vowels close together, and "hidden assonance" (the parts of diphthongs: long *a* contains a short *e* sound, long *i* contains *ä* + *ē*) knit a line together even when the listener can't name the device; Pattison analyses a John Mayer line this way — [Pattison, "Knitting A Sonic Fabric, Part 1", American Songwriter, 13 Jan 2016](https://americansongwriter.com/knitting-a-sonic-fabric/)

### Inferences
- For the three reference styles: the *dark storytelling song* (evil as a charming, polite man) gets its menace from *stable, polite, perfectly rhymed surface* contrasted with sinister content, then one unstable line (shortened, unrhymed, or a consonance rhyme) where the mask slips. The *confessional song to God / a lost person* should sit mostly in unstable territory (assonance rhymes, a short last line, an unrhymed question) and resolve with a perfect rhyme only on the line where acceptance arrives. The *struggle-but-keep-going rap* moves from unstable verses into a stable, perfectly rhymed hook that "slams the gate shut" on the resolve. This is applying Pattison's stable/unstable rule, not a documented analysis of those songs.
- AI vocal tools sing whatever stress the melody gives them, so mis-stresses ("to-DAY" set as "TO-day", "for" on beat 1) are amplified. Check stresses by speaking each line over a 4/4 click before generation, and put the key word on beat 1 or 3.

- No quantitative study of rhyme type vs listener emotion was found.

## 2. Object writing, show-don't-tell, one-idea songs, point of view, title/hook placement, the twist line

### Takeaway
Pros earn the right to state a feeling by showing a concrete, sensory object or moment first ("You can't tell unless
you show first"), choose a deliberate point of view (direct address is the most common and most intimate), and make
the repeated hook gain meaning by changing its grammar or context. Andrea Stolpe's "destination writing" and
Pattison's daily object writing are the standard drills for finding the specific image that makes a line feel lived.

### Cited Findings
- Pattison's rule: "YOU CAN'T TELL UNLESS YOU SHOW FIRST." "Showing makes the telling more powerful, because BOTH your senses and your mind are engaged." A small physical object (a dog's collar) carries a whole childhood and loss more efficiently than a statement of feeling. Abstract lines about "nostalgic nights and lost love" tell the listener what to feel; concrete period details let them feel it. Put the concrete image first in a section: "Colors drip DOWN, not UP" — [Pattison, "Rusty's Collar"](https://www.patpattison.com/rusty-s-collar)
- Sensory images "stimulate your listeners' senses, what they see, smell, hear, taste, touch", making listeners participants rather than observers, especially valuable early in a song; object writing as a morning warm-up, plus the metaphor exercises in *Songwriting Without Boundaries* — [Sound On Sound, Mar 2022](https://www.soundonsound.com/node/4927197)
- Object writing = "sense-bound free association" on one object across seven senses (sight, hearing, smell, taste, touch, organic/body sensation, kinesthetic/motion); usually 10 minutes a day — [search snippet only: iZotope, "Object Writing: Your Secret to Vivid Song Lyrics"](https://www.izotope.com/en/learn/object-writing-your-secret-to-vivid-song-lyrics.html)
- Stolpe's destination writing: start from one *place* word, write a free verse there through touch, taste, smell, sight, sound and movement, without rhyme or meter, then mine it for lyric lines. "Detail allows your audience to step into your shoes, know what you know, and feel how you feel" — [search snippet only: Berklee Online Take Note, "Use Destination Writing and Object Writing…"](https://online.berklee.edu/takenote/songwriting-tips/) and [Berklee Online, "Small, Sensory Moments"](https://online.berklee.edu/takenote/small-moments-write-the-song/)
- Point of view: decide "Who are you talking to, and why?" before writing; direct address ("you") is a close-up focused on feelings, first-person narrative a middle-distance shot; "The song should, somehow, always be about them!"; try all four POVs before finishing a song — [Pattison, "POV & Verse Development"](https://www.patpattison.com/pov)
- "There are four points of view" (first, second, third person, direct address); "The most used point of view in songwriting is the fourth: direct address"; a set of only I/you songs can feel static — [Sound On Sound, Mar 2022](https://www.soundonsound.com/node/4927197)
- Re-colouring a repeated line: isolate a fragment and repeat it as a new grammatical type. A statement repeated as a question adds doubt ("creates new energy, and adding a new idea"); drop the question word and repeat from the auxiliary ("Who will you love?" → "Will you love?"); drop the subject to turn a line into a command (first/second person only) — [Pattison, "Productive Repetition"](https://www.patpattison.com/productive-repetition)
- An unrhymed line before the title "targets" it, giving the title extra weight when it lands — [Pattison, "Structure Creates Expectations"](https://www.patpattison.com/structure-creates-expectations)
- Berklee songwriting chair Rodney Alejandro's exercise: "Tell us your main idea for the song that would normally be three minutes" (a one-idea discipline). Students now write mini songs first and expand them later — [Berklee Now, "TikTok Is Changing the DNA of Hit Songs" (24 Oct 2023)](https://www.berklee.edu/berklee-now/news/tiktok-is-changing-the-dna-of-hit-songs-and-artists-are-taking-note)

### Inferences
- "Personifying evil as a charming, polite man" works because it is *third-person character* or *character speaking in first person* plus concrete manners (a held door, a tipped hat, a soft voice). That is show-don't-tell applied to a villain: the listener deduces the evil. A competent-but-flat lyric names it ("he is the devil, he is evil").
- The confessional "addressed to God / a lost person" style is direct address. Pattison's advice implies each verse should *ask the addressee for something different* (to answer, to forgive, to come back), so the hook means more each time.
- A twist or reframe line is easiest to build with Pattison's grammar trick: the same hook words, repeated as a question or command, or with one pronoun swapped (I/you), on a strong beat.

### Gaps
- Jimmy Webb's *Tunesmith* and Jeff Pearce material were not reached. Pattison's direct guidance on *where* to place the title (first/last line of the chorus) was not on the pages opened.

## 3. Hip-hop specific: multisyllabic and internal rhyme, flow, cadence, pocket, breath; emotional and melodic rap

### Takeaway
Emotional and melodic rap trades density for space: fewer syllables per bar (about 8-10 for a sung bar), open vowels on
held end-words, tight predictable rhyme on held notes, and planned breath gaps. Technical multisyllabic/internal rhyme
stays in the verses, not the hook. Most sources here are practitioner blogs (lower authority). The authoritative book,
Paul Edwards' *How to Rap* (interviews with 104 rappers), was confirmed but its chapter content could not be read.

### Cited Findings
- Melodic rap density: about 8-10 syllables for a bar you want to sing, up to about 12 for rap-leaning bars. At 16 syllables there is "no room to sustain". A 4/4 bar at 90 BPM lasts about 2.6 s, so each extra syllable shrinks every vowel. The end-word of each bar is the longest-held note, so choose open vowels (OH, AY, AH, EE). Closed vowels (IH, EH, UH) cut notes short, which suits punchy rap but not singing. Use tight AABB rhyme in melodic sections; scattered ABAB/ABCB "work against the melody". Hum open vowels over the beat before writing words. Alternate sung bars with rapid bars to keep 16 bars interesting. Auto-Tune needs a stable vowel, so packed syllables glitch — [RhymeFlux, "How to Write Melodic Rap" (Apr 2026, upd. Oct 2026)](https://rhymeflux.com/blog/how-to-write-melodic-rap/) [practitioner blog; numbers are rules of thumb, not research]
- Emotional rap: a simple, plain line about loss can outperform a complicated rhyme scheme; a bar that is clever but doesn't *feel* right should be cut — [search snippet only: JBZ Beats, "How to Write Emotional Rap Lyrics"](https://jbzbeats.com/how-to-write-emotional-rap-lyrics-that-resonate-with-fans/) [low authority]
- Breath: write deliberate "breath holes" into long verses; keep the hook simple and save dense multisyllabic writing for the verses; multisyllabic rhyme = rhyming several syllables in a row, not just the last one — [search snippet only: Musosoup, "How to make a rap"](https://musosoup.com/blog/how-to-make-a-rap) [secondary]
- Paul Edwards, *How to Rap* (2009) is compiled from interviews with 104 rappers. Reviewers describe the sequel (2013) as covering rhythm, melody, pitch, timing, enunciation, percussion, playing characters, rhyme schemes and rhyme patterns; the sequel names techniques such as triplets, flams, "lazy tails" and breaking rhyme patterns — [Wikipedia, "How to Rap"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/How_to_Rap); technique list [search snippet only: publisher/bookshare listing](https://www.bookshare.org/browse/book/1933451)
- "Playing characters" is a named rap technique in Edwards' sequel (relevant to the "evil as charming man" persona song) — [Wikipedia, "How to Rap"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/How_to_Rap)
- A$AP Rocky described "Purity" (in a Genius interview, cited on Wikipedia) as an attempt to be more emotionally honest and less braggadocious — [search snippet only: Wikipedia "Purity"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Purity_(ASAP_Rocky_song)) [secondhand; Genius page not opened]
- Pattison's "sonic fabric" (internal rhyme + assonance + hidden assonance running through a line) is the songwriting-school version of hip-hop internal/multisyllabic rhyme — [American Songwriter, Pattison, Jan 2016](https://americansongwriter.com/knitting-a-sonic-fabric/)

### Inferences
- For AI-generated vocals, the syllable ceiling matters more than for a human rapper: crowded bars make AI singers rush, slur or drop words. Cap sung bars at about 10 syllables and end them on open vowels.
- Emotional rap's "struggle but keep going" shape: verses can carry multisyllabic density (the struggle, fast and crowded), and the hook drops to a few plain words on long notes (the resolve). The density change itself becomes prosody.

## 4. What makes a lyric clip-able on TikTok/Shorts/Reels

### Takeaway
Industry voices agree on the unit: one self-contained, immediately readable line or 4-16 bars that grab within
seconds ("you have to get somebody in two seconds"), about a common human problem, in a form people can reuse
(quotable, act-out-able, confessional). Hard data on *which lyric features* go viral is thin; most numbers are about
speed of virality, not lyric content.

### Cited Findings
- Songwriter Maegan Cottone (Little Mix, Kylie Minogue) at Music Ally Connect 2026: sessions chasing virality want "one punch-line, and you have to get somebody in two seconds"; writers hunt for "that little seed, that little hook that might go off" and "nobody really knows what that is"; she described one TikTok-popular refrain as more like a social-media caption than a song, and urged writers to "let the art lead". Musixmatch's Rio Caraeff: "Lyrics are a powerful visual surface area for engagement and attention", and audiences can tell genuine pathos from contrived work. YouTube Music's John Oberbeck: lyrics open an artist's "world-building"; subtitle habits make viewers comfortable reading lyrics while listening — [Music Ally, Stuart Dredge, 13 Feb 2026](https://musically.com/2026/02/13/lyrics-and-the-pressure-to-go-viral-you-have-to-get-somebody-in-two-seconds/)
- Users look for "4-16 bars that make an immediate impression"; clips are short and the *original song* is the audio, so clever/quotable lines, and lines people can act out with faces, outfits or gestures, do best — [Grammy.com, "What Music Goes Viral On TikTok?" (Nov 2019, quoting Haulix)](https://www.grammy.com/news/what-music-goes-viral-tiktok/) [older source]
- Berklee songwriting chair Rodney Alejandro: what comes first "can't be too complicated. It has to be super catchy, really fast"; many writers lead with the chorus but shouldn't reveal too much of it, so listeners stay curious; bridges have largely vanished and second verses are following. Rosie Scher calls number/ABC countdown choruses "TikTok hooks". The article also notes early TikTok-chart songs were light rather than serious — [Berklee Now, 24 Oct 2023](https://www.berklee.edu/berklee-now/news/tiktok-is-changing-the-dna-of-hit-songs-and-artists-are-taking-note)
- Peer-reviewed content analysis (Polish): songs that went viral on TikTok tended to have lyrics about "typowe ludzkie problemy" (common human problems), plus fast tempo, simple choreography, pop-culture resonance and influencer activity; sample size not stated on the page — [Sawicka, *Studia Etnologiczne i Antropologiczne* 24(2), pub. 2025](https://journals.us.edu.pl/index.php/SEIA/article/view/22879)
- Chartmetric (Nov 2025): average days to 100k TikTok posts fell from about 340 (2020) to about 48 (2025), while Spotify streams per TikTok post fell from about 738 to about 275. Hooks are "designed to loop" and lyrics "trimmed for catchiness"; over-teased snippets can peak before release; the same clip spreads in sped-up/slowed/reverb versions. The author says this is correlational, not causal; the sample description is internally inconsistent (49/year vs 299 tracks) — [Chartmetric / How Music Charts, J. Haddad, 7 Nov 2025](https://hmc.chartmetric.com/why-tiktok-songs-go-viral-faster/)
- MIDiA's "All Eyes, No Ears" (as cited by Chartmetric): many young listeners discover music via clips but don't move on to sustained listening — [Chartmetric, Nov 2025](https://hmc.chartmetric.com/why-tiktok-songs-go-viral-faster/) [MIDiA report not opened]
- TikTok's own study (547 tracks) claimed 84% of 2024 Billboard Global 200 debuts went viral on TikTok first — [search snippet only, reported via inspiredbybeatz.com](https://www.inspiredbybeatz.com/en/hook-first-2026-songs-for-the-first-15-seconds/) [platform's self-interested figure; secondary; unverified]

### Inferences
- A clip-able 15-30 s moment needs: (1) a first line that is a scene or confession understandable with zero context; (2) one quotable line someone would put as a caption over their own video (Cottone's "caption" remark cuts both ways: caption-like lines spread); (3) a hook short enough to read on screen and sing back; (4) closure inside the clip: an unstable setup that resolves (Pattison's stable/unstable arc in miniature).
- For lyric-video Shorts the lyric *is* the visual (Caraeff's "visual surface area"), so each on-screen line should be one image or one confession, readable in about 2 s.

- No Billboard, Rolling Stone, The Verge or Hit Songs Deconstructed analysis of *lyrical* features of viral clips was found or opened. No study isolates sad/confessional songs. No data on Shorts/Reels specifically (vs TikTok).

## 5. Common amateur mistakes and how pros revise

### Takeaway
The recurring amateur failures are naming emotions instead of showing them, stock images, rhymes that bend the
syntax, crowded or mis-stressed lines, unanswered rhetorical questions, and inconsistent pronouns or POV. The pro
revision loop is: show first, swap to a nearer-than-perfect rhyme rather than twist the sentence, read or sing aloud
over the beat, and check stresses, POV and pronouns.

### Cited Findings
- Telling vs showing: swap abstract feeling words for concrete images; "You can't tell unless you show first" — [Pattison, "Rusty's Collar"](https://www.patpattison.com/rusty-s-collar)
- Clichés such as "burning flames of love"/"hearts that beat as one" make people tune out; forced rhymes bend sentences unnaturally, so prefer near/slant rhymes that let you say what you mean; replace abstract concepts with concrete images — [search snippet only: Wisseloord, "Common Songwriting Mistakes"](https://wisseloord.org/?p=33953)
- Jason Blume (BMI): unanswered rhetorical questions ("How could you hurt me?") weaken a lyric. Images or direct statements evoke more emotion. After a draft, check for unanswered questions, consistent pronouns and conversational word order — [search snippet only: BMI, "5 Common Lyric Mistakes—and How to Avoid Them"](https://bmi.com/news/entry/5-common-lyric-mistakesand-how-to-avoid-them) (page returned 403)
- Clichés can be used deliberately (irony, camp) when the writer is aware of them — [search snippet only: Sonicbids, "5 lyrical clichés that can ruin a song"](https://blog.sonicbids.com/5-lyrical-cliches-that-can-ruin-a-song)
- Mis-stress ("greedy" or "overwrought" settings) and mismatched lyric/melody roadmaps lose the listener — [patpattison.com/perfect-match](https://www.patpattison.com/perfect-match); [patpattison.com/lyric-and-melodic-phrases](https://www.patpattison.com/lyric-and-melodic-phrases)
- POV drift: decide who is talking to whom before writing; try all four POVs before finishing — [patpattison.com/pov](https://www.patpattison.com/pov)
- Over-stuffed bars (about 16 syllables) leave no room to sustain; count syllables and drop words — [RhymeFlux (2026)](https://rhymeflux.com/blog/how-to-write-melodic-rap/)
- Contrived writing "rings false"; audiences detect it (Caraeff paraphrased) — [Music Ally, Feb 2026](https://musically.com/2026/02/13/lyrics-and-the-pressure-to-go-viral-you-have-to-get-somebody-in-two-seconds/)

### Inferences (a revision checklist built from the cited rules)
1. Circle every emotion word (sad, broken, pain, lost, alone, darkness, demons). Replace each one in the verses with an object, a place or an action the listener can see; keep at most one plainly stated feeling, after the image (Pattison "show first").
2. Replace any image you've heard in another song (tears like rain, heart in pieces, fighting demons, chasing dreams) with one that could only come from this story.
3. Read each line over a 4/4 click: content words and the title on beats 1/3, no function word on a downbeat unless it's a contrast.
4. Count syllables per bar (sung bars about 8-10) and end held lines on open vowels.
5. Choose rhyme type by feeling: perfect rhyme only where the song resolves; assonance/family/consonance where it aches; one unrhymed line to set up the title.
6. Lock POV and pronouns: one speaker, one addressee, one reason for speaking.
7. Hook test: does a 15-30 s window contain a zero-context first line, one caption-worthy line and a resolution?
8. Replace any rhetorical question that gets no answer with a statement or image, unless the unanswered question is the deliberate final line.
