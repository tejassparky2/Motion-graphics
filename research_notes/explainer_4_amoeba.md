- Kerala's 2025 outbreak gives the Indian stake. Jal neti gives an everyday Indian anchor.

## Fact check (each claim, and where it comes from)

| Claim | Source |
|---|---|
| Lives in warm fresh water (lakes, rivers, ponds, hot springs), and in poorly kept pools | CDC, About Naegleria fowleri / Causes |
| Infections usually follow long hot periods (warmer water, lower water levels) | CDC, Causes |
| Enters through the nose, travels to the brain and destroys brain tissue | CDC |
| Route: olfactory nerve through the cribriform plate (the "bone with tiny holes") | CDC; standard anatomy (Gray's Anatomy figs. 149, 771) |
| Swallowing contaminated water does not infect you; not spread person to person | CDC |
| Symptoms 1-12 days after exposure (median 5): headache, fever, nausea, vomiting, then stiff neck, confusion, seizures, hallucinations | CDC, Symptoms |
| Progresses rapidly; death usually within about 5 days (range 1-18) | CDC |
| Fatality over 97%: 4 survivors of 167 known US cases, 1962-2024 (on-screen footnote) | CDC |
| Trophozoite ~10-35 µm, thinner than a human hair (~17-180 µm) | CDC DPDx; hair-width references |
| Kerala 2025: about 170 amoebic brain infections, 42 deaths (several amoeba species, so the script says "brain infections from amoebas") | Government of India, Lok Sabha reply, Dec 2025 |
| Kerala's survivors: active testing of encephalitis cases for amoebas and early combination treatment | The Lancet, 2025, "Naegleria fowleri in Kerala, India: prevention over panic"; Kerala health department reports |
| Prevention: hold the nose or use a nose clip; avoid stirring up sediment in shallow warm fresh water; rinse the nose only with boiled (and cooled), distilled or sterile water | CDC, Prevention |

Left out as unsettled: exact survival rates (media figures vary). The script only says "some patients survived".
Nothing in it is treatment advice; it says "see a doctor fast, and tell them you swam".
