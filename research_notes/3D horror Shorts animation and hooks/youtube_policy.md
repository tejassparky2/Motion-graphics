# YouTube policies for an interactive 3D horror Shorts channel (late 2026)

Scope and method: every YouTube Help page below was downloaded as raw HTML on **25 Oct 2026** and quoted from the extracted page text (not from a summariser), unless a line says otherwise. YouTube Help pages carry no "last updated" date (footer only says "©2026 Google"), so "current" means "live on 25 Oct 2026". Tags: **[Official]** = YouTube/FCC text, verbatim; **[Official via secondary]** = YouTube statement as reported by press; **[Secondary]** = press or law-firm summary; **[Interpretation]** = my reading, not policy text; **UNVERIFIED** = could not confirm against a primary source.

Channel being assessed: 70-90 s horror Shorts, code-rendered 3D animation (no generative AI images/video), synthetic TTS narration (open-source Chatterbox Turbo stock voices, no cloning of a real person), ending each episode with "Comment X or Y, the most comments decide Part 2."

## 1. Spam / engagement bait: is "Comment X or Y, the most comments decide Part 2" allowed? What about comment-keyword automation?

### Takeaway
Asking viewers to comment is explicitly allowed in two separate YouTube policies, and a story vote with no prize, no coercion and a real follow-through is not "incentivization" under any current wording. The risks are (a) the vote is fake or Part 2 never comes (misleading viewers to boost engagement), (b) offering a *reward* (a guide, a file, a shout-out) for a comment keyword, and (c) settings that switch comments off (made for kids, Restricted Mode), which silently break the mechanic. YouTube has published no Shorts-specific "engagement bait" rule that I could find.

### Cited Findings
- [Official] Spam Policy, opening: "We do not allow content, metadata, or behavior designed to take advantage of the YouTube community, including spamming users or manipulating the platform in ways that mislead them. We also prohibit misleading viewers to boost content engagement or to flood community spaces with inorganic promotion." Applies to "all types of content on YouTube, including unlisted and private content, comments, links, posts and thumbnails, and coordinated networks of channels." — [YouTube Help 2801973, Spam Policy](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/2801973?hl=en) (accessed 25 Oct 2026)
- [Official] Same page, the explicit permission: "**Engagement manipulation:** Repetitive or templated content aimed at artificially inflating engagement through bots, coercion, or offering rewards in exchange for likes, views, or subscribers. **It's okay to ask viewers to like, comment, or subscribe.** Example: Using coordinated "sub-for-sub" schemes." — [Help 2801973](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/2801973?hl=en)
- [Official] Same page, the nine listed spam types (verbatim labels): Engagement manipulation; Fake engagement ("Exploiting community features, such as Polls or Image Posts, to force engagement rather than building genuine enthusiasm..."; example "Posting high volumes of low-effort, repetitive polls (e.g., "Click the heart if you like food") multiple times a day"); Comment spam ("Using high-volume, repetitive, or deceptive comments, live chats, or other messages to drive traffic to or engagement with content"); Off-platform diversion; Detection evasion; Automated or synthetic mass-production; Scraped content; Scams; Malicious clickbait ("Using maliciously misleading titles, thumbnails, descriptions, or imagery to trick users into clicking on a video that does not deliver what was promised"). — [Help 2801973](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/2801973?hl=en)
- [Official] Same page, "Automated or synthetic mass-production: Using automated tools or AI to churn out high volumes of similar content with minimal changes. While testing out new creation tools or posting a few variations of a video is ok, we don't allow use of these tools to flood our platform with repetitive content... Example: Channels that use the exact same background music and repetitive AI generated imagery across many videos, with each video reading out an AI-generated script." — [Help 2801973](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/2801973?hl=en)
- [Official] Same page, enforcement: "we may suspend your monetization or terminate your channel or account... For some violations, we may remove the content and issue a warning or a strike... If you get 3 strikes within 90 days, your channel may be terminated." — [Help 2801973](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/2801973?hl=en)
- [Official] Fake engagement policy: "YouTube doesn't allow anything that artificially increases the number of views, likes, comments, or other metrics either by using automatic systems or serving up videos to unsuspecting viewers. Also, **content that solely exists to incentivize viewers for engagement (views, likes, comments, etc) is prohibited.**" and "We consider engagement to be legitimate when a human user's primary intent is to authentically interact with the content. We consider engagement illegitimate, for example, when it results from coercion or deception, or when the sole purpose of the engagement is financial gain." Listed note: "**Note: You're allowed to encourage viewers to subscribe, hit the like button, share, or leave a comment**". — [YouTube Help 3399767, Fake engagement policy](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/3399767?hl=en) (accessed 25 Oct 2026)
- [Official] Fake engagement example of a violation: "A video that tries to force or trick viewers into watching another video through deceptive means (for example: a misleadingly labeled info card)." — [Help 3399767](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/3399767?hl=en)
- [Official] Contest rules (apply only if there is a prize/contest): "Your contest must be free of charge to enter"; "You and any third-party may not manipulate metrics on the YouTube service to misrepresent genuine viewer engagement..."; contests need "Official Rules" that "clearly state that YouTube is not a sponsor"; "YouTube does not allow contests to be run through ad units." — [YouTube Help 1620498, contest policies](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/1620498?hl=en) (accessed 25 Oct 2026)
- [Official] Comments are switched off when "The channel or video's audience is set as "made for kids,"" when Restricted Mode is on, and "YouTube may have turned off comments on some content for safety reasons, like to protect minors". — [YouTube Help 9706180, Learn why comments are turned off](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/9706180?hl=en) (accessed 25 Oct 2026). Restricted Mode: "When Restricted Mode is turned on, you can't view comments on the videos you watch." — [YouTube Help 174084](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/174084?hl=en)
- [Official] Comment automation via the API: "you must not automate or trigger views, uploads, comments, likes, dislikes, or other actions without the user's prior specific and express consent"; and "API Clients must not offer or provide incentives, rewards, or other compensation to users for engaging with YouTube Applications (directly or indirectly) by performing actions like viewing content, liking content, sharing content, subscribing to channels, adding comments." — [YouTube API Services Developer Policies](https://developers.google.com/youtube/terms/developer-policies) (accessed 25 Oct 2026)
- [Secondary, contrast: other platforms] Meta (Facebook) has demoted "engagement bait" since Dec 2017, including "vote baiting" (using reactions/comments/shares as votes) and "comment baiting" (asking users to reply with a specific word, e.g. comment "Yes" if you like rock music); posts are demoted, not banned, with Page-level demotion for repeat use. — [Search Engine Journal](https://searchenginejournal.com/facebook-demoting-engagement-bait/228071); [Fox News](https://www.foxnews.com/tech/facebook-starts-demoting-engagement-bait-posts.amp) (via search summaries, not fetched; wording UNVERIFIED). This is Meta's policy, not YouTube's.
- [Secondary] A search for YouTube / Creator Liaison statements on "engagement bait" or "comment bait" in Shorts (2023-2026) found only third-party blogs; one 2023 Creator Liaison post advises Shorts creators to "make a strong call-to-action, promise them something of value, and then make sure you over-deliver" (about driving viewers to long-form, not about bait). — [blog.youtube, Rene's Top Five, 26 Oct 2023](https://blog.youtube/creator-and-artist-stories/renes-top-five-on-youtube-october-26-2023/) (quote via search summary, not fetched)
- UNVERIFIED (archive fetch blocked): older versions of the Spam page used the heading "Spam, deceptive practices, & scams policies" and an "Incentivization spam" item (content that sells engagement metrics or exists only to boost metrics, e.g. "Sub4Sub"). The term "incentivization spam" does **not** appear on the page as of 25 Oct 2026; its substance now sits under "Engagement manipulation" (Help 2801973) and the Fake engagement policy (Help 3399767).

### Inferences
- [Interpretation] "Comment X or Y, the most comments decide Part 2" is an ask to comment, which both policies explicitly allow. It offers no reward "in exchange for" engagement (the viewer's influence on the story is the content itself, not a payout), involves no bots or coercion, and the video does not "solely exist" to harvest comments as long as it is a complete story. It is not a contest (no prize), so the contest rules don't apply. Adding a prize (e.g. "best comment gets their name in Part 2", merch) would turn it into a contest and require Official Rules.
- [Interpretation] The real policy exposure is deception: if the "vote" is not actually honoured, or Part 2 is never posted, the CTA becomes "misleading viewers to boost content engagement" (Spam Policy opening line) and edges toward "Malicious clickbait... a video that does not deliver what was promised". Checklist: actually count the votes, post Part 2, and say in Part 2 which option won (e.g. "62% chose the stairs").
- [Interpretation] Coercion wording to avoid: "comment or the character dies", "if this gets 1,000 comments I'll post Part 2", "comment to unlock". These threaten/withhold content in exchange for engagement, which is closer to "coercion" and to content that "solely exists to incentivize" engagement.
- [Interpretation] "Comment SURVIVE to get the guide" (keyword-for-reward) is the riskiest pattern for YouTube: it is a reward in exchange for a comment, the delivery usually needs automated replies (API rule: no automated comments without the user's "prior specific and express consent"), and if the guide is off-YouTube it also invites "Off-platform diversion" scrutiny. Recommendation: don't use keyword-for-reward CTAs on this channel.
- [Interpretation] Avoid identical pinned comments or replies pasted across hundreds of videos ("Comment spam"); vary the pinned question per episode.
- [Interpretation] Don't use a YouTube Poll / Community post every few hours to run the vote ("high volumes of low-effort, repetitive polls" is a named example). One poll per episode as a supplement is fine.
- [Interpretation] If the clips are cross-posted to Facebook/Instagram Reels, Meta's "vote baiting"/"comment baiting" demotion is a stricter standard than YouTube's; the same CTA may lose reach there.
- [Interpretation] Settings check: Not made for kids (else comments are off), and keep content out of Restricted Mode's filter where possible (viewers in Restricted Mode can't see comments at all).

### Gaps
- No official YouTube statement (blog, Help, Creator Liaison) specifically addressing "engagement bait", "comment bait" or "Part 2 bait" in Shorts, 2023-2026, was found.
- Whether YouTube's ranking discounts comments that are one-word votes (vs. "genuine" comments) is not documented anywhere I found.
- The prior "Incentivization spam" wording could not be retrieved from the Wayback Machine (request failed), so the exact old text is UNVERIFIED.

## 2. YPP "inauthentic content" (renamed 15 Jul 2025): exact text, what counts, and how a recurring horror series stays compliant

### Takeaway
Since 15 Jul 2025 the monetization rule formerly called "repetitious content" is "inauthentic content". It allows a series with the same intro/outro and recurring characters if "each video has a distinct storyline, focus, or concept", and bans "characters... put in the same situation over and over again with the same outcome". A newer "Unsatisfying or Off-putting Content" section is the one most specific to horror: it bans content that "repeatedly uses disturbing themes (such as violence or loss) without building a cohesive narrative" and content "designed to shock or surprise viewers for the sole purpose of getting views".

### Cited Findings
- [Official] Change log: "July 15, 2025: We're making a minor update to our "repetitious content" policy to better clarify this includes content that is repetitive or mass-produced. We are also renaming this policy from "repetitious content" to "inauthentic content." This type of content has always been ineligible for monetization under our existing policies, where creators are rewarded for original and authentic content. There is no change to our reused content policy which reviews content like commentary, clips, compilations, and reaction videos." — [YouTube Help 1311392, channel monetization policies](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/1311392?hl=en) (accessed 25 Oct 2026)
- [Official] "Generic or Repetitive Content... includes content that looks like it's made with a template, or that may feel repetitive to viewers after watching several videos in a row from the same channel." "We know many channels create content that follows a similar pattern. What's important is that the substance of each video should be materially varied and deliver creative, educational, or other value." — [Help 1311392](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/1311392?hl=en)
- [Official] Allowed: "Same intro and outro for your videos, but the bulk of your content is different"; "Similar content, like a series following a set of characters across episodes or a channel that does product reviews, but in which each video has a distinct storyline, focus, or concept". — [Help 1311392](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/1311392?hl=en)
- [Official] Not allowed: "Similar or repetitive content with low educational value, commentary, narratives, or minimal variation across videos"; "Videos where characters are put in the same situation over and over again with the same outcome (i.e., using a highly similar storyline template across multiple videos)"; "Image slideshows, templated storylines, or scrolling text with minimal or no narrative, commentary, or educational value"; "AI-generated content made with generic or unoriginal templates giving the impression of mass production without adding the creator's original, authentic insights or perspective". Also: "channels where content feels interchangeable from video to video are not allowed to monetize." — [Help 1311392](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/1311392?hl=en)
- [Official] "Unsatisfying or Off-putting Content refers to content that relies heavily on emotionally manipulative formulas, mimics existing formats or stories to a degree that the videos feel interchangeable, or appears designed to shock or surprise viewers for the sole purpose of getting views." "If you use automated tools or templates to help create your content, the final product must still demonstrate your creative vision and provide educational or entertainment value." — [Help 1311392](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/1311392?hl=en)
  - Allowed: "Content with a cohesive storyline that doesn't rely solely on shock value to generate views"; "Content that showcases your authentic perspective when building on a popular video format or theme, like a Short that puts your own personalized spin on a trend"; "Content that expresses your unique creative voice, like using AI to visualize a unique character and narrative you invented"; "Content that utilizes creative tools to assist in delivering a unique, well-researched, or creative narrative, like using AI to edit your video scripts or generate a unique background visual".
  - Not allowed: "Content that repeatedly uses disturbing themes (such as violence or loss) without building a cohesive narrative"; "Channels that heavily rely on generic templates or emotionally manipulative themes, like a series showing repetitive scenarios of animals in exaggerated distress or peril"; "Content that lacks a clear narrative arc or logical progression, such as videos that stitch together unrelated or inconsistent AI clips to surprise or shock viewers"; "Content that uses deceptive or misleading imagery or narratives, such as realistic visuals tricking viewers into believing a fake celebrity death or natural disaster has occurred".
- [Official] Review is channel-level: reviewers may check "Main theme", "Most viewed videos", "Newest videos", "Biggest proportion of watch time", "Video metadata (including titles, thumbnails, and descriptions)", "Channel's "About" section". — [Help 1311392](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/1311392?hl=en)
- [Official via secondary, 13 Jul 2025] YouTube's statement to Social Media Today: the update targets "Channels that upload narrative stories with only superficial differences between them" and "channels that upload slideshows that all have the same narration"; "YouTube welcomes creators using AI tools to enhance storytelling, and channels that use AI in their content remain eligible for monetization"; reused content "can continue to be monetized if you've added significant original commentary, modifications, or educational or entertainment value to the original video." — [Social Media Today via Yahoo Finance, Andrew Hutchinson, 13 Jul 2025](https://finance.yahoo.com/news/youtube-clarifies-changes-monetization-rules-194232823.html)
- [Official via secondary] Creator Liaison Rene Ritchie described it as a "minor update" to a long-standing policy, saying such content "has already been ineligible for monetization for years", and that it is not a crackdown on reaction videos or transformative content. — [Cord Cutters News](https://cordcuttersnews.com/youtube-is-cracking-down-on-bad-ai-content-but-dont-worry-say-youtube/); [exchange4media](https://www.exchange4media.com/digital-news/youtube-issues-clarification-on-monetisation-policy-update-after-creator-backlash-145327.html) (paraphrase via search summary; exchange4media returned HTTP 403; Ritchie's exact words UNVERIFIED). The Yahoo/SMT article paraphrases rather than quotes him.
- [Official, from prior repo notes, not re-fetched today] 1 Oct 2026 Shorts ranking change: "Rather than making minor technical edits or template-based changes simply to alter a video, focus on bringing your own voice, storytelling, or distinct point of view." — [YouTube Help Community, "Prioritizing original content on Shorts"](https://support.google.com/youtube/blog/470890423/prioritizing-original-content-on-shorts?hl=en)
- [Official] The Spam Policy's mass-production example names "the exact same background music and repetitive AI generated imagery across many videos, with each video reading out an AI-generated script" (see Q1). — [Help 2801973](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/2801973?hl=en)

### Inferences
- [Interpretation] A fixed HUD, title card and end card is the "same intro and outro" case and is fine **if** the bulk of each episode (setting, threat, story beats, ending) differs. The danger zone is "same situation, same outcome": e.g. every episode = character alone at night, phone buzzes, thing appears, jump scare, "Comment A or B". Checklist: vary the setting, the threat, the structure of the scare, and the ending (escape / caught / twist / ambiguous / threat was benign), and log the outcome per episode so two in a row don't match.
- [Interpretation] The comment-vote format helps here: viewer choices naturally create branching, different outcomes. But each episode must still be a complete arc ("lacks a clear narrative arc" is not monetizable); a Part 1 that is only a setup and a cliffhanger is weaker than one that resolves a smaller beat before posing the choice.
- [Interpretation] Horror-specific red lines from "Unsatisfying or Off-putting": jump scares or gore with no story; "disturbing themes (such as violence or loss) without building a cohesive narrative"; repeated "animals in distress/peril" scenarios; anything that looks like a real disaster or death notice. A cohesive story with stakes is the stated safe harbour.
- [Interpretation] The channel's code-rendered 3D (no generative AI imagery) avoids the "AI-generated content made with generic or unoriginal templates" and "repetitive AI generated imagery" language, but the same TTS voice + same music bed + same HUD across every upload adds up to several "template" signals. Varying the music per episode and the camera grammar per story reduces that.
- [Interpretation] Inauthentic content is judged per channel, not per video: a run of near-identical early uploads can block YPP approval for the whole channel.

### Gaps
- The "Generic or Repetitive", "Unsatisfying or Off-putting" and "AI Personas" sections carry no date on the page; I could not confirm when each was added (they were live on 18 Oct 2026 per prior notes and on 25 Oct 2026).
- No YouTube statement specifically about interactive/choose-your-path series or "Part 2" chains under this policy was found.
- Ritchie's exact spoken words (his July 2025 video) were not retrieved; only paraphrases.

## 3. Altered or synthetic content disclosure: needed for TTS narration, 3D animation, or clearly fictional horror?

### Takeaway
Disclosure is required only for **realistic** generated/altered content (real person saying/doing something they didn't, altered real events/places, realistic scenes that didn't happen, AI music as the main focus). The March 2024 launch post explicitly exempts content that is "clearly unrealistic, animated". A stock synthetic narrator voicing an invented horror story in a stylised 3D world falls in the exempt zone, with one caveat: the 2024 post listed "synthetically generating a person's voice to narrate a video" as a disclosure example, and YouTube can auto-label content its systems detect as AI.

### Cited Findings
- [Official] Current rule: "Creators must disclose GenAI content that: Makes a real person appear to say or do something they didn't do. Alters footage of a real event or place. Generates a realistic scene that didn't actually occur. Creates music that's the main focus of the video. This could include content that is fully or partially altered or created using AI tools." — [YouTube Help 14328491, "Disclosing use of GenAI content"](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/14328491?hl=en) (accessed 25 Oct 2026)
- [Official] "Realistic AI content and meaningful changes require disclosure, while non-realistic or minor edits don't." "Creators don't need to disclose non-realistic content that's made with AI, or edits to realistic content that are minor." — [Help 14328491](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/14328491?hl=en)
- [Official] No-disclosure examples, "Not realistic": "Someone riding a unicorn through a fantastical world"; "Green screen used to depict someone floating in space"; "**Using an AI-generated or altered animation of a missile in a fully animated video**". "Minor": "Applying beauty filters"; "Color adjustment or lighting filters"; "Special effects filters, like adding background blur or vintage effects"; "Production assistance, like using generative AI tools to create or improve a video outline, script, thumbnail, title, or infographic"; "Caption creation"; "Video sharpening, upscaling or repair and voice or audio repair"; "Idea generation"; "Cloning one's own voice to create voice overs or dubs"; "Gameplay footage from video games"; "AI generating or extending a backdrop to simulate a moving car"; "Using effects to enhance previously recorded audio". — [Help 14328491](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/14328491?hl=en)
- [Official] Disclosure-required examples: "AI generated music"; "AI generated extra footage of a real place..."; "AI generated realistic videos of a match between two real professional tennis players"; "Making it appear as if someone gave advice that they did not actually give"; "Showing a realistic depiction of a tornado or other weather events moving toward a real city that didn't actually happen"; "Making it appear as if hospital workers turned away sick or wounded patients"; "Depicting a public figure stealing something..."; "Making it look like a real person has been arrested or imprisoned". — [Help 14328491](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/14328491?hl=en)
- [Official] How: in YouTube Studio upload, "In the Attributes section, under "AI use," tap Yes if your content meets the disclosure requirements or No if not." Labels: "For AI-generated music or content that is photorealistic, a label in the video player may also appear." "Labels may appear in the expanded description for non-music AI-generated audio or AI content that is non-photorealistic or animated." "Note: Disclosing AI content alone won't limit a video's audience or impact its eligibility to earn money." — [Help 14328491](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/14328491?hl=en)
- [Official] Auto-labels: "YouTube may automatically apply an AI label on the video player or in the expanded description for: Content made using YouTube's GenAI tools; Content that contains C2PA metadata; Content that our internal systems detect is AI generated or altered." Creators can change a wrong auto-label "in most cases by selecting No", except for content made with YouTube's AI tools, C2PA content, or "content labeled after manual review". — [Help 14328491](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/14328491?hl=en)
- [Official] Penalties: "Creators who consistently choose not to disclose this information may be subject to manual application of a label, or penalties from YouTube, including removal of content or suspension from the YouTube Partner Program." — [Help 14328491](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/14328491?hl=en)
- [Official, 18 Mar 2024] Launch post: "today, we're introducing a new tool in Creator Studio requiring creators to disclose to viewers when realistic content – content a viewer could easily mistake for a real person, place, scene, or event – is made with altered or synthetic media, including generative AI. **We're not requiring creators to disclose content that is clearly unrealistic, animated, includes special effects, or has used generative AI for production assistance.**" Exempt list: "Clearly unrealistic content, such as animation or someone riding a unicorn through a fantastical world". Disclosure examples then included "Using the likeness of a realistic person: Digitally altering content to replace the face of one individual with another's or **synthetically generating a person's voice to narrate a video**." Sensitive topics: "for videos that touch on more sensitive topics — like health, news, elections, or finance — we'll also show a more prominent label on the video itself." — [YouTube Official Blog, "How we're helping creators disclose altered or synthetic content", 18 Mar 2024](https://blog.youtube/news-and-events/disclosing-ai-generated-content/) (accessed 25 Oct 2026)
- [Official] The Spam and Child safety pages both add: "Creators should disclose when content is generated or meaningfully altered with AI, but this disclosure is not a free pass to violate our policies." — [Help 2801973](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/2801973?hl=en); [Help 2801999](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/2801999?hl=en)
- [Official] Impersonation (relevant to voices): "It may also include using AI to copy the voice or likeness of an individual, to make it appear as if the channel is owned or authorized by that individual." — [YouTube Help 2801947, Impersonation policy](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/2801947?hl=en) (accessed 25 Oct 2026)

### Inferences
- [Interpretation] Code-rendered stylised 3D horror with invented characters is "clearly unrealistic, animated" content: no disclosure required. This is not generative AI imagery at all, so the AI-use question only arises for the voice (and any AI music).
- [Interpretation] Stock TTS voice narrating fiction: the current Help page's four triggers don't cover it (it doesn't make a real person say anything, doesn't alter a real event, isn't a realistic scene). The 2024 example "synthetically generating a person's voice to narrate a video" sits under "Using the likeness of a realistic person", i.e. a real, identifiable person's voice; a stock voice that is nobody's likeness is outside it. Reasonable setting: "AI use: No". A cautious alternative is "Yes": the page says disclosure "won't limit a video's audience or impact its eligibility to earn money" and for animated/non-music audio it shows only in the expanded description.
- [Interpretation] Situations that WOULD flip it to "Yes": a photoreal render of a real place under attack, a realistic "news report" or emergency broadcast about a real city, a narrator voice that sounds like a specific real person, or AI-generated music that is the main focus of the video.
- [Interpretation] Don't strip C2PA/provenance data from any third-party assets; auto-labels from C2PA can't be reversed, which is fine, but stripping provenance is not.
- [Interpretation] Checklist: no real people (living or dead) as characters or voices; no realistic recreations of real tragedies; invented towns/companies; set the AI-use attribute deliberately on every upload, the same way each time.

### Gaps
- No official YouTube text addresses stock (non-cloned) TTS narrators specifically; the conclusion above is an interpretation of the "realistic" test.
- Whether YouTube's detector auto-labels Chatterbox-type TTS audio is undocumented.
- The page has no change log, so I could not date the switch from the "Altered content" checkbox to the "AI use" attribute.

## 4. Violent/graphic content, age restriction and ad suitability for fictional animated horror; how Shorts handle age-restricted content

### Takeaway
Community Guidelines: fictional animated violence is generally not removed ("such as with animated content or video games"), and animation is treated more leniently than live action. Age restriction is possible for graphic dramatized scenes (torture, dismemberment, decapitation, mass violence) and for cartoons with adult themes that "could easily be confused with family content". Ads: "Dramatized" explicitly includes animation; dramatized non-graphic or graphic violence earns full ads, "severe and shocking injuries" get limited ads, and gore for shock's sake gets none. The word "horror" appears only in the kids sections. Age-restricted videos "are not shown in certain sections of YouTube"; no official text names the Shorts feed.

### Cited Findings
- [Official] Violent or graphic content, not allowed: "Inciting others to commit violent acts..."; "Fights involving minors."; "Footage, audio, or imagery involving road accidents, terrorist attack aftermath, street fights, physical attacks, immolation, torture, corpses, robberies, or other such scenarios with the intent to shock or disgust viewers."; "Footage or imagery showing bodily fluids, such as blood or vomit, with the intent to shock or disgust viewers."; "Footage of corpses with massive injuries, such as severed limbs." Dramatized: "Dramatized or fictional footage of content prohibited by these guidelines where the viewer is not given enough context to understand that the footage is dramatized or fictional." — [YouTube Help 2802008, Violent or graphic content policies](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/2802008?hl=en) (accessed 25 Oct 2026)
- [Official] Same page: "We may age-restrict dramatized violence when it contains graphic scenes, such as mass violence against non-combatants or people being tortured, dismembered, or decapitated. We may also age-restrict content that focuses on human corpses with these severe injuries or violent deaths of a person that show blood. We make a distinction between dramatized violence featuring real human actors and content featuring animations or video games. Dramatized violence featuring real human actors may be more likely to face age-restrictions, especially if the content is particularly graphic or realistic. **Generally, we do not remove dramatized violence when the content or metadata lets us know that the content is fictional, or when it's apparent from the content itself, such as with animated content or video games.**" — [Help 2802008](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/2802008?hl=en)
- [Official] Factors weighed: "Whether violent or gory imagery is the focus of the video"; "Whether the title, description, tags, or other data show an intent to shock or disgust viewers"; "Whether violent imagery or audio has been blurred, masked, or obscured"; "The amount of time the violent images or audio is in the content"; "Whether there's context that lets viewers know that the imagery is dramatized or fictional. For example, through info in the video, title, or description"; "Whether the dramatized violence is depicted with real human actors or animation including video games." Applies to "videos, video descriptions, thumbnails, comments, live streams". "Age-restricted content isn't viewable to anyone who's under 18 years of age or signed out." — [Help 2802008](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/2802008?hl=en)

- [Official] "Horror" appears only in the kids section: "Shocking content aimed toward kids: Content that is safe for adults, but could shock or scare kids, such as adult horror characters or jump scare themes such as kidnaping, horror movies, etc." → "Limited ad earnings or no ad earnings" for "Content that is made to appear appropriate for kids and families, but contains content to scare or shock children", e.g. "Characters aimed to scare kids, such as Momo or adult horror characters"; "Content that depicts gore or other graphic violent content"; "Non-graphic content that could frighten kids such as kidnapping, horror scenes, syringes being used as weapons, etc." — [Help 6162278](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/6162278?hl=en)
- [Official] Controversial issues (if a horror plot touches suicide, self-harm, abuse): full ads for "Non-graphic but descriptive or dramatized content related to domestic abuse, self-harm, suicide, adult sexual abuse..."; limited for "Thumbnails with graphic depictions of controversial issues, including real or dramatized"; no ads for "Animated depiction of controversial issues presented in a sensational manner". — [Help 6162278](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/6162278?hl=en)
- [Official] Profanity: "words like "hell" or "damn" in the title, thumbnail, or video" are in the full-ads tier; "Moderate profanity (like "shit") in the title or thumbnail" is limited. — [Help 6162278](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/6162278?hl=en)
- [Official] Restricted Mode filters "potentially mature content" using "many signals, like video title, description, metadata, Community Guidelines reviews, and age restrictions". — [Help 174084](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/174084?hl=en)

### Inferences
- [Interpretation] Ad tiers for this channel: suspense, chases, shadows, a creature attack with a cut-away or brief non-graphic injury = full ads ("Dramatized content depicting non-graphic violence or graphic violence"). Visible severe wounds, broken bones, lingering agony, gory body parts = limited ads. Gore as the point of the video, or a gory thumbnail/first frame = no ads.
- [Interpretation] The "first 7 seconds" and thumbnail rules are written for gaming, but they show how YouTube weighs openings: the hook frame (frame 0, which on Shorts often acts as the thumbnail) should be tense, not gory. Put the scare on implication (a silhouette, a wrong detail), not on a wound.
- [Interpretation] Low-realism stylised 3D sits at the lenient end of every scale (Community Guidelines: animation is "generally" not removed; ads: "Low realism"). Photoreal human renders would move toward "moderate/high realism".
- [Interpretation] Biggest age-restriction risk is not gore but the cartoon look: "cartoons that contain adult themes such as violence, sex, or death" that "could easily be confused with family content". Mitigation: visual style that reads as teen/adult (darker palette, mature character design), no kid-show tropes (bright mascots, nursery-rhyme music), titles/descriptions that signal horror for teens/adults, never "for kids" tags.
- [Interpretation] An age-restricted Short is invisible to signed-out and under-18 viewers and is "not shown in certain sections", which for a Shorts channel likely means losing most feed distribution. Treat age restriction as a reach-killer, not just an ads issue: aim every episode for no age restriction.
- [Interpretation] Avoid in titles/thumbnails: "Warning: lots of blood"-style shock wording (named as a removal signal for real footage), strong profanity, graphic imagery.
- [Interpretation] Keep self-harm/suicide out of the plot or keep it non-graphic and non-sensational; "Animated depiction of controversial issues presented in a sensational manner" = no ads.

### Gaps
- No official YouTube text states how age-restricted content is handled in the Shorts feed specifically; only "not shown in certain sections of YouTube".
- No dedicated "horror genre" guidance exists for adult/general audiences; only kids-section mentions.
- Jump-scare loud audio has no stated rule outside the kids section.

## 5. Made for Kids / COPPA: when is animated content "made for kids", and how should a horror channel with cartoon-styled 3D set it?

### Takeaway
Horror is "not made for kids": YouTube's own guidance says content with "violence... or other mature themes not suitable for young audiences" and age-restricted content are not MFK, and "Animated content that appeals to everyone" is general audience. But cartoon-styled characters are one of the FTC factors, and labelling scary cartoons as kids' content is a Child Safety violation ("Misleading family content"). Setting MFK would also switch off comments, which kills the vote mechanic.

### Cited Findings
- [Official] "If your video features actors, characters, activities, games, songs, stories, or other subject matter that reflect an intent to target children, it's likely made for kids." Factors include "Whether children are your intended audience... or the actual audience"; "Whether the video includes characters, celebrities, or toys that appeal to children, **including animated characters or cartoon figures**"; "activities that appeal to children, such as play-acting, simple songs or games, or early education"; "songs, stories, or poems for children"; "Whether the content is advertised to children." — [YouTube Help 9528076, Determining if your content is "made for kids"](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/9528076?hl=en) (accessed 25 Oct 2026)
- [Official] Not MFK: "Content that contains sexual themes, violence, obscene, or other mature themes not suitable for young audiences."; "Age-restricted videos that aren't appropriate for viewers under 18." A video is not MFK "just because: It's safe or appropriate for everyone to watch... It covers an activity that's traditionally associated with children... Children may incidentally see it." General audience examples include "**Animated content that appeals to everyone**" and "A gaming video that features adult humor". "General audience content is content that could appeal to everyone, but isn't intended specifically for children, or content that is intended for a teen or older audience." — [Help 9528076](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/9528076?hl=en)
- [Official] Mixed audience: "Mixed audience content... is considered a type of made for kids content. This is content that targets children as one of its audiences, even if it's not the main or primary audience". Age: "The age of a "kid" in the United States is defined as anyone under the age of 13." — [Help 9528076](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/9528076?hl=en)
- [Official] Responsibility: "it is your legal responsibility to comply with COPPA and/or other applicable laws and designate your content accurately... If you fail to categorize your content correctly, there may be consequences on YouTube, as well as legal consequences under the Children's Online Privacy Protection Act (COPPA)". "YouTube Analytics (YTA) is not designed to help determine if your content is child directed." — [Help 9528076](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/9528076?hl=en)
- [Official] Override and disabled features: "We'll also use machine learning to help us identify videos that are clearly directed to young audiences... we may override your audience setting choice in cases of error or abuse." Features restricted on MFK content: "Cards or end screens", "Comments", "Notification bell", "Personalized advertising", "Playback in the Miniplayer". — [YouTube Help 9527654, Set your channel or video's audience](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/9527654?hl=en); [Help 9632097](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/9632097?hl=en) (accessed 25 Oct 2026)
- [Official] Child safety, "Misleading family content: Content that targets young minors and families, but contains: Sexual themes; Violence; Obscenity or other mature themes...; Medical procedures; Self harm; **Use of adult horror characters**; Other inappropriate themes intended to shock young audiences." Also: "Family friendly cartoons that target young minors and contain adult or age-inappropriate themes such as violence, sex, death, drugs, and more. **We don't allow content labeled as suitable for kids in the video's title, description, tags, or in the audience selection if it contains age-inappropriate themes.**" "Make sure your titles, descriptions, and tags match the audience you're targeting." More examples: "Using cartoons, puppets, or family entertainment characters to appeal to children where content features adult themes like violence and sex." — [Help 2801999](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/2801999?hl=en)
- [Official] Monetization: "Content that is "made for kids" must be appropriate for a family audience in order to monetize on YouTube." — [Help 6162278](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/6162278?hl=en); "If a channel is found to have a strong focus on low-quality "made for kids" content, it may be suspended from the YouTube Partner Program." — [Help 1311392](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/1311392?hl=en)
- [Official] YouTube notes the FTC's Nov 2019 guidance for creators: "In November 2019, the FTC released more information to help YouTube creators decide if their content is "made for kids" or not." — [Help 9528076](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/9528076?hl=en)

### Inferences
- [Interpretation] Channel setting: "No, it's not made for kids" at channel level and per video. Horror with violence/death themes is "mature themes not suitable for young audiences" by YouTube's own wording.
- [Interpretation] Because "animated characters or cartoon figures" is a listed MFK factor and the ML system can override, the creative must not *look* aimed at under-13s: avoid toddler-proportioned mascots, bright primary palettes, nursery music, toy-like characters, kid-show names, and words like "kids", "for children", "cartoon for kids" in metadata. Write titles/descriptions that target teens/adults ("horror", "creepy", "don't watch alone").
- [Interpretation] The same choices protect against the age-restriction trigger in Q4 ("could easily be confused with family content") and the "Misleading family content" removal rule.
- [Interpretation] The "comment X or Y" mechanic only works if the video is not MFK; if YouTube's classifier flips an episode to MFK, comments disappear. Check each upload's audience status in Studio after publishing.

### Gaps
- No public detail on what signals YouTube's MFK classifier uses for stylised 3D animation.
- Rules outside the US (e.g. India's DPDP Act children's provisions) were not researched here.

## 6. Fictional phone messages / emergency alerts imitating real apps or EAS/WEA alerts, and real company logos

### Takeaway
The legal risk is the **sound**, not the visual: US federal rules forbid transmitting the EAS Attention Signal/codes or the WEA alert tone "or a recording or simulation thereof" outside real emergencies, tests or authorised PSAs. The FCC has fined networks for movie trailers and promos, including simulated tones and one excerpt taken from a YouTube video, and fined a public-radio producer in Dec 2025 for a history documentary. I found no FCC action against a YouTube-only creator, but the rule's wording ("No person") is broad and the advisory names "dramatic, entertainment" programs. On YouTube, realistic fake alerts about real places run into the "deceptive... imagery... fake... natural disaster" and AI-disclosure rules; real logos/app UIs run into trademark and impersonation policies.

### Cited Findings
- [Official, US regulation] 47 CFR 11.45(a): "No person may transmit or cause to transmit the EAS codes or Attention Signal, or a recording or simulation thereof, in any circumstance other than in an actual National, State or Local Area emergency or authorized test of the EAS; or as specified in §§ 10.520(d), 11.46, and 11.61 of this chapter." — [47 CFR § 11.45, Cornell LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/47/11.45) (accessed 25 Oct 2026)
- [Official, US regulation] 47 CFR 10.520 (WEA phone alert tone): pattern "one long tone of two (2) seconds, followed by two short tones of one (1) second each, with a half (0.5) second interval between each tone... repeated twice"; "fundamental frequencies of 853 Hz and 960 Hz transmitted simultaneously" (960 Hz on monophonic devices). 10.520(d): "No person may transmit or cause to transmit the WEA common audio attention signal, or a recording or simulation thereof, in any circumstance other than in an actual National, State or Local Area emergency or authorized test, except as designed and used for Public Service Announcements (PSAs) by federal, state, local, tribal and territorial entities, and non-governmental organizations in coordination with those entities..." — [47 CFR § 10.520, Cornell LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/47/10.520)
- [Official] FCC Enforcement Advisory No. 2019-05 (DA 19-758, 15 Aug 2019): "Any transmission of the EAS codes or Attention Signals, or simulations thereof, by any person under any circumstances other than a genuine alert, an authorized test of the EAS system, or an authorized PSA, violates federal regulations". "We remain concerned about the misuse of the EAS codes and EAS and WEA Attention Signals, or simulations thereof, to capture audience attention during advertisements; **dramatic, entertainment, and educational programs**". Definition: a "simulation" includes "not only recordings of actual EAS codes or Attention Signals... but also sounds that mimic or are substantially similar to them, such that an average listener could reasonably mistake the sounds for actual EAS codes or Attention Signals. **By contrast, general alarms or other loud noises, including bells; klaxons; and police, fire, or civil defense sirens, are not considered "simulations"**". Scope: "applies to entities that distribute programming containing a prohibited signal intended for subsequent or simultaneous transmission to the public, regardless of whether or not they deliver the unlawful signal directly to consumers". Also cites Communications Act §325(a) on false distress signals, and past forfeitures of "$1,120,000 against Viacom Inc." and "$280,000 against ESPN Inc." Rationale: misuse "can confuse people or lead to "alert fatigue"" and "could result in false activations of the EAS". — [FCC DA 19-758](https://docs.fcc.gov/public/attachments/DA-19-758A1.txt) (accessed 25 Oct 2026)
- [Official] FCC consent decree, released 10 Dec 2025: American Public Media Group pays an "$86,400" voluntary contribution and adopts a compliance plan over "Emergency Alert System tones (EAS Tones) during an episode of the program BBC Witness History entitled "Chasing the World's Biggest Tornado"". — [FCC DA 25-1035](https://docs.fcc.gov/public/attachments/DA-25-1035A1.txt) (accessed 25 Oct 2026)
- [Secondary, 6 Nov 2013] FCC proposed a $25,000 fine against Turner Broadcasting for a Conan promo that "included a simulation of the EAS codes and attention signal", and a $39,000 consent decree with a Kentucky TV station for a store ad; Enforcement Bureau chief: "inexcusable to trivialize the sounds". — [TV Technology / Radio World](https://www.tvtechnology.com/news/-fcc-warns-against-airing-false-eas-tones)
- [Secondary, UNVERIFIED primary] The FCC fined Fox $504,000 (2021) for a 9-1-1: Lone Star promo with a ~3-second EAS tone excerpt that Fox said was downloaded or recorded from a YouTube video. — reported in search summaries of [Broadcast Law Blog](https://www.broadcastlawblog.com/tags/eas-attention-signal) and [TV Technology](https://www.tvtechnology.com/news/updated-fcc-levels-19-million-in-eas-fines); FCC order not fetched.
- [Official] YouTube monetization, not allowed: "Content that uses deceptive or misleading imagery or narratives, such as realistic visuals tricking viewers into believing a fake celebrity death or natural disaster has occurred". — [Help 1311392](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/1311392?hl=en)
- [Official] AI disclosure required for "Showing a realistic depiction of a tornado or other weather events moving toward a real city that didn't actually happen". — [Help 14328491](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/14328491?hl=en)
- [Official] Spam/Scams example: "A video pretending to be a real company or government service that tricks viewers into clicking a link for a "free payout"..." — [Help 2801973](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/2801973?hl=en)
- [Official] Impersonation: "Impersonation may include deceptively copying the branding, content, or usernames of individuals, channels or entities... YouTube also enforces trademark holder rights." — [Help 2801947](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/2801947?hl=en)
- [Official] Trademark: "Trademark infringement is improper or unauthorized use of a trademark in a way that is likely to cause confusion as to the source of that product. YouTube policies prohibit videos and channels that infringe trademarks. If your content uses someone else's trademarks in a way that might cause confusion, your videos can be blocked. Your channel may also be suspended." YouTube "doesn't mediate trademark disputes" and "will remove content in clear cases of infringement". — [YouTube Help 6154218, Trademark](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/6154218?hl=en) (accessed 25 Oct 2026)

### Inferences
- [Interpretation] Sound design checklist: never use the real EAS attention signal (853+960 Hz dual tone), the EAS SAME data bursts ("duck-duck" headers), or the WEA 2 s / 1 s / 1 s pattern at 853/960 Hz, nor anything an average listener would mistake for them. Use an invented alert sound (different pitches and rhythm) or the explicitly excluded categories: generic alarms, klaxons, bells, sirens.
- [Interpretation] Even if the FCC has not (to my knowledge) acted against a YouTube-only creator, a Short that goes viral can be re-aired by TV/radio (news segments, compilations), which is exactly the scenario the FCC enforces; and the SAME data bursts could trip broadcast EAS decoders if re-aired. The 2025 APM case shows "educational/documentary" framing is no defence.
- [Interpretation] Visual fake alerts: use an invented agency and invented town, a phone UI that isn't iOS/Android/WhatsApp-identical, and an in-frame fictional cue. Avoid "Presidential Alert", "FEMA", "National Weather Service", real city names with realistic imagery, and anything that could be screenshotted and passed off as a real alert.
- [Interpretation] Real logos/apps: a recognisable real brand UI (WhatsApp, iMessage, Snapchat, a real bank) in a horror plot risks a trademark complaint (YouTube forwards complaints and removes "clear cases") and impersonation flags; parody/fictional brands avoid both. Mild, non-confusing background presence of a real product is common on YouTube, but nothing in the policy text guarantees it is safe, so fictional brands are the low-risk default.

### Gaps
- No YouTube Community Guidelines text specifically about fake emergency-alert sounds or fake alert graphics was found.
- No FCC action against an online-only (YouTube/TikTok) creator was found; whether the FCC would assert jurisdiction over a platform-only upload is unresolved.
- The Fox 2021 fine details are from search summaries; the FCC order was not read.
- Non-US emergency-alert rules (e.g. India's cell-broadcast alerts, Philippines) were not researched.

## 7. Music and sound: Content ID risk for synthesized sound; YouTube Audio Library licence

### Takeaway
Content ID only matches against reference files from owners with **exclusive** rights; non-exclusively licensed and "royalty-free" library music is not supposed to be registered, but YouTube takes no responsibility for third-party "royalty-free" sources. Audio Library tracks and sound effects are "copyright-safe" and won't be claimed via Content ID; Creative Commons tracks need attribution in the description. Original code-synthesised sound has no reference file to match, so claims would only come from false matches. AI-generated music that is the main focus of a video needs the AI-use disclosure.

### Cited Findings
- [Official] Content ID eligibility: "Copyright owners must have the exclusive rights to the material that's evaluated. Common examples of items that may not be exclusive to individuals include: mashups, "best of"s, compilations, and remixes of other works; video gameplay, software visuals, trailers; unlicensed music and video; **music or video that was licensed, but without exclusivity**; recordings of performances". Content ID "will match your reference content against every upload to YouTube." — [YouTube Help 1311402, Qualify for Content ID](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/1311402?hl=en) (accessed 25 Oct 2026)
- [Official] "Content ID is available to copyright owners who meet specific criteria. To be approved, they must own exclusive rights to a substantial body of original material that is frequently uploaded to YouTube." — [YouTube Help 2797370, How Content ID works](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/2797370?hl=en) (accessed 25 Oct 2026)
- [Official] Audio Library: "In the Audio Library in YouTube Studio, you can find royalty-free production music and sound effects to use in your videos. Music and sound effects from the YouTube Audio Library are copyright-safe. The Audio Library is found exclusively in YouTube Studio." "If you're using a track with a Creative Commons license, you must credit the artist in your video's description." "If you're in the YouTube Partner Program, you can monetize videos with music and sound effects from the Audio Library. Copyright-safe music and sound effects downloaded from the Audio Library won't be claimed by a rights holder through the Content ID system." "Only music and sound effects from the Audio Library are known to YouTube to be copyright-safe. YouTube is not responsible for issues that arise from "royalty-free" music and sound effects from YouTube channels or other music libraries." Also: "if your video uses music with a Creative Commons license, the attribution info is still required in your video's description." — [YouTube Help 3376882, Use music and sound effects from the Audio Library](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/3376882?hl=en) (accessed 25 Oct 2026)
- [Official] AI disclosure is required for "AI generated music" and GenAI content that "Creates music that's the main focus of the video". — [Help 14328491](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/14328491?hl=en)
- [Official] Mass-production example names "the exact same background music" across many videos as one marker. — [Help 2801973](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/2801973?hl=en)
- [Official] Reused content: "Songs modified to change the pitch or speed, but are otherwise identical to the original song" are not monetizable; "Detection evasion: Technical manipulation (such as speeding up audio...)" is spam. — [Help 1311392](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/1311392?hl=en); [Help 2801973](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/2801973?hl=en)

### Inferences
- [Interpretation] Code-synthesised music and SFX (procedural plucks, drones, stingers) are the safest audio: nothing to match in Content ID, no attribution, no licence scope questions. Residual risk is false matches against registered tracks built from common sample packs; keep project files/seed code so a dispute can show the sound was generated in-house.
- [Interpretation] If using the Audio Library: download only from Studio, paste CC attribution into every description that uses a CC track, and remember YouTube's guarantee covers YouTube only (see gap on off-platform use).
- [Interpretation] Avoid third-party "royalty-free"/"no copyright" horror tracks from YouTube channels: YouTube says outright it isn't responsible for them, and those are a common source of Content ID claims on Shorts.
- [Interpretation] Algorithmic (non-generative-AI) background music isn't "AI generated music"; if an AI music generator is ever used for a track that is the main focus, disclose it.
- [Interpretation] Vary the music bed per episode to avoid the "exact same background music across many videos" mass-production marker.

### Gaps
- The Audio Library licence terms for use **outside** YouTube (e.g. cross-posting the same Short to Instagram/TikTok) are not stated on the Help page fetched; UNVERIFIED either way.
- No official data on false Content ID claim rates for synthesized/procedural audio.
- Whether Chatterbox Turbo's built-in "sound tags" (e.g. screams, gasps) could false-match any Content ID references is unknown.
