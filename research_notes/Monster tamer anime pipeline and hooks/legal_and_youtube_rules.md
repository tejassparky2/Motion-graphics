# Legal and YouTube limits for an original, AI-assisted monster-taming animated series (as of 30 Oct 2026)

Not legal advice. Facts carry a link; "Inferences" are analysis drawn from those facts. Where sources are weak (SEO blogs, single
secondary reports) that is said next to the claim.

## 1. Nintendo / The Pokémon Company (TPC) / Game Freak enforcement history

### Takeaway
Nintendo and TPC enforce hard against anything that uses their actual assets, names or characters (fan games, mods, a mod's
YouTube video, a Chinese clone game), but against Palworld, whose creatures were original designs, they sued only on game-mechanic
**patents**, not copyright or trademark. That case is still pending in Tokyo (court's preliminary view due 9 Nov 2026), has been
narrowed to old Palworld versions, and several related Nintendo patents/applications have been rejected or put under reexamination.

  "We will continue to assert our position in this case through future legal proceedings" —

### Inferences
- The pattern: **assets/names → copyright or trademark action, fast (DMCA, C&D, platform takedown, including YouTube); original designs
  + similar mechanics → patents only (and only against a commercial game).** An original animated series with original creature
  designs and names sits in the zone Nintendo has not attacked so far.
- Money and visibility trigger action (McGowan; Uranium after 1.5M downloads; Palworld after huge sales). A monetized, growing channel
  should assume it will be looked at and be clean on assets, names and marks from day one.
- The Tokyo outcome (9 Nov 2026 preliminary view) is pending; whatever it is, it concerns game software, not videos (see section 3).

## 2. How legally distinct monster-tamers stay safe; what copyright protects (expression vs ideas)

### Takeaway
The genre (catch, train, battle creatures) predates Pokémon and is shared by many franchises that have never been sued; copyright and
Indian law protect specific expression (designs, art, text, music), not ideas, systems, names or short phrases, though names and
catchphrases can be trademarks.

### Cited Findings
- Monster-taming is a recognised RPG subgenre: Megami Tensei (1987) recruited demons, Dragon Quest V (1992) recruited monsters, Digimon
  (1997), Yo-kai Watch (2014, built as a Pokémon competitor), Temtem (>500,000 copies in a month), Coromon; the only lawsuit the article
  records is the 2024 Palworld patent suit —
  [Wikipedia: Monster-taming game](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monster-taming_game)
- No lawsuits against Temtem, Cassette Beasts or Coromon were found; forum commentary notes the real risk is protected names like
  "Pokemon" or "Bulbasaur" (fan opinion, not law) —
  [Steam discussion](https://steamcommunity.com/app/1623730/discussions/0/4852154959747769920); [TheGamer](https://www.thegamer.com/could-nintendo-sue-over-temtem/)
- US law excludes protection for "any idea, procedure, process, system, method of operation, concept, principle, or discovery,
  regardless of the form in which it is described, explained, illustrated, or embodied"; protection extends "only to the original
  expression" — [US Copyright Office, Circular 33](https://www.copyright.gov/circs/circ33.pdf)
- Names, titles and short phrases are not copyrightable, including "The name of a character" and "Catchwords or catchphrases", but
  "may be protectable under federal or state trademark laws" — [Circular 33](https://www.copyright.gov/circs/circ33.pdf)
- India: R.G. Anand v. Delux Films (Supreme Court, 18 Aug 1978): themes, plots and storylines stay in the public domain; infringement
  needs substantial similarity of expression judged by whether an average viewer would see the later work as a copy; trivial
  similarities do not count — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/R._G._Anand_v._Deluxe_Films);
  [BananaIP](https://www.bananaip.com/intellepedia/rg-anand-vs-delux-films-copyright-infringement-india/);
  [judgment PDF](https://api.sci.gov.in/jonew/judis/5032.pdf)
- The Chinese clone that lost (¥107M) copied Pokémon game content and was also found to be unfair competition —
  [Automaton](https://automaton-media.com/en/news/the-pokemon-company-wins-15-million-copyright-infringement-lawsuit-against-china-based-game-developers/)

### Inferences
- **Free to use (ideas/genre):** humans befriending or binding creatures, creature "types" and weaknesses as a concept, battles,
  evolution/transformation as a concept, a young trainer's journey, rivals, gyms-as-an-idea (a ladder of challenges), creature
  companions with one-word cries.
- **Keep away from (expression and marks):** any existing Pokémon design or a near-trace of one (silhouette + palette + signature
  feature together), names that echo Pokémon names or the "-mon"/"Poké" pattern, a red-and-white split sphere with a centre button,
  "Gotta catch 'em all", "I choose you", Pokédex-style device design and wording, the type-chart's visual layout and icons, the 18-type
  roster copied one-for-one, Pikachu-like yellow electric mascot, Ash/Team Rocket analogues, the anime's "Who's that Pokémon?" bumper,
  battle-music motifs, cry sounds, recall-beam effect.
- A sensible test, given R.G. Anand's "average viewer" standard: if a casual viewer looking at a still frame or hearing the jingle
  would say "that's a Pokémon/Poké Ball", change it.
- Distinctness also helps YouTube standing (inauthentic/reused policies reward a recognisable original world).

### Gaps
- No court decisions found that measure how close a creature design may be (US "sufficiently delineated character" case law was not
  researched here).
- Monster Rancher, Cassette Beasts, Monster Sanctuary, Nexomon: no legal history found either way.

## 3. Do Nintendo's Palworld-related patents matter for a non-interactive animated video?

### Takeaway
Very unlikely: the patents claim game software/systems/methods, and patent rights cover making, using or selling the claimed
invention, not depicting the idea in a story. They become relevant only if the owner later builds a game or interactive app.

### Cited Findings
- Japan Patent Act Art. 2(3): "working" of a product invention (including a computer program) means producing, using, assigning
  (etc.) it; the patentee holds the exclusive right to work the patented invention as a business (Art. 68) —
  [Japanese Law Translation: Patent Act](https://www.japaneselawtranslation.go.jp/en/laws/view/42/en);
  [MOJ translation PDF](https://www.moj.go.jp/content/000004471.pdf)
- The US summon patent is literally titled "storage medium, information processing system, information processing apparatus, and game
  processing method" — [Games Fray](https://gamesfray.com/u-s-patent-examiner-rejects-nintendos-summon-subcharacter-and-let-it-fight-in-1-of-2-modes-patent-as-obvious-non-final-ruling/)
- The Tokyo suit concerns in-game mechanics (throwing a capture item, riding), and Pocketpair avoided exposure by changing game code
  (v0.3.11, v0.5.5) — [Nintendo Life](https://nintendolife.com/news/2025/05/palworld-dev-announces-disappointing-game-changes-resulting-from-nintendos-lawsuit)
- Commentators note the patents could only be enforced in "highly specific circumstances" and list affected **games** (Shin Megami
  Tensei, Digimon, Palworld) — [Pocket Tactics](https://www.pockettactics.com/nintendo-switch/creature-patent)

### Inferences
- An animated episode that **shows** a character throwing a capture device is a depiction, not a program, system or method being
  produced/used/sold, so it should fall outside these patent claims. This is my reading of patent basics, not an expert opinion on
  animation specifically.
- Still avoid copying the Poké Ball *look* (trademark/copyright issue, not patent), and if a companion mobile game is ever planned,
  get a patent-clearance opinion first (capture-by-throw, ride, summon-to-fight mechanics).

### Gaps
- No lawyer or court source was found that explicitly addresses whether game-mechanic patents can reach a film or animation.
- Full claim text of JP 7545191 / 7493117 / 7528390 not read.

## 4. Trademark risk in titles, thumbnails, tags and descriptions ("Pokémon", "Pokémon-like", "Poké Ball")

### Takeaway
Saying the word "Pokémon" to describe or compare is a nominative use that is generally defensible in the description; putting it in
titles, thumbnails or tags of a video that is not about Pokémon invites trademark complaints and breaks YouTube's misleading-metadata
and spam rules. Never use Poké Ball imagery.

### Cited Findings
- YouTube: trademark infringement is "improper or unauthorized use of a trademark in a way that is likely to cause confusion as to the
  source"; such videos "can be blocked", channels may be suspended; YouTube does a "limited review of reasonable complaints and will
  remove content in clear cases of infringement" — [YouTube trademark policy](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/6154218?hl=en)
- YouTube spam policy prohibits "maliciously misleading titles, thumbnails, descriptions, or imagery to trick users into clicking" —
  [Spam, deceptive practices and scams](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/2801973?hl=en);
  "Adding excessive tags to your video description is against our policies on spam, deceptive practices, and scams" —
  [YouTube tags help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/146402?hl=en)
- Enforcement example: YouTube terminated Screen Culture and KH Studio (AI fake movie trailers, >2M subs combined) in Dec 2025 for
  "clear violations of our spam and misleading metadata policies"; they had first been demonetized (March 2025) and had used
  big-franchise names without clear "fan/concept" labelling —
  [TheWrap](https://www.thewrap.com/industry-news/tech/youtube-terminates-screen-culture-kh-studio-over-ai-fake-movie-trailers/);
  [Film Stories](https://filmstories.co.uk/news/youtube-has-deleted-two-major-channels-which-specialised-in-fake-film-trailers/)
- US nominative fair use (New Kids on the Block v. News America, 9th Cir. 1992): allowed when the thing is not readily identifiable
  without the mark, only as much of the mark is used as needed, and nothing suggests sponsorship or endorsement —
  [Davis+Gilbert](https://www.dglaw.com/nominative-fair-use-defense-may-enable-use-of-anothers-trademark/);
  [The Fashion Law](https://www.thefashionlaw.com/new-kids-on-the-block-v-new-america-publishing-inc/)
- India: Trade Marks Act 1999 s.30(1) permits use of another's mark in accordance with "honest practices in industrial or commercial
  matters" that takes no unfair advantage; s.30(2)(d) is a narrow necessity defence —
  [Mondaq](https://www.mondaq.com/india/trademark/879442/doctrine-of-nominative-fair-use39-under-trademark-law);
  [BananaIP](https://www.bananaip.com/intellepedia/trademark-violations-comparative-advertising-india-legal-analysis/)

### Inferences
- **Titles/thumbnails:** no "Pokémon", "Poké", "Pikachu", "Pokéball", "Pokémon but…", no red-white ball. Sell the show on its own hook.
- **Tags:** no Pokémon terms (the video is not about Pokémon; it reads as misleading metadata and as trading on the mark).
- **Description:** a single plain, truthful line is the lowest-risk use if wanted at all, e.g. "An original monster-taming story,
  not affiliated with or endorsed by Nintendo, The Pokémon Company or Game Freak." Avoid "Pokémon-like" as a keyword strategy.
- **Pinned comments / debate questions** comparing to Pokémon are commentary and lower risk than metadata, but keep them rare.
- Don't name creatures or the series with Poké-/-mon/Pocket Monster patterns; check a new series name in the USPTO and Indian TM
  registries before branding.

### Gaps
- No YouTube statement found specifically on using a competitor's brand in tags; no reported case of TPC filing a YouTube trademark
  complaint against an original animation.

## 5. YouTube monetization rules affecting an AI-assisted animated series (Oct 2026)

### Takeaway
AI tools are allowed; what is demonetized is templated, mass-produced, interchangeable content. A character series is explicitly
allowed if each episode has a distinct storyline; but "characters put in the same situation over and over again with the same outcome"
is explicitly not. Animated, clearly unrealistic AI content does not need the altered/synthetic disclosure. YouTube removed large
AI-slop channels in Dec 2025–early 2026, including a Dragon Ball-themed AI animation channel.

### Cited Findings
**YPP "inauthentic content" (renamed 15 July 2025)**
- Notice: "We're making a minor update to our 'repetitious content' policy to better clarify this includes content that is repetitive
  or mass-produced… We are also renaming this policy from 'repetitious content' to 'inauthentic content.'" —
  [YPP policies](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/1311392?hl=en)
- Allowed: "the substance of each video should be materially varied and deliver creative, educational, or other value"; "Same intro
  and outro for your videos, but the bulk of your content is different"; "Similar content, like a series following a set of characters
  across episodes… in which each video has a distinct storyline, focus, or concept" — [YPP policies](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/1311392?hl=en)
- Not allowed: "channels where content feels interchangeable from video to video"; "Videos where characters are put in the same
  situation over and over again with the same outcome (i.e., using a highly similar storyline template across multiple videos)";
  "Image slideshows, templated storylines, or scrolling text with minimal or no narrative…"; "AI-generated content made with generic or
  unoriginal templates giving the impression of mass production without adding the creator's original, authentic insights or
  perspective" — [YPP policies](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/1311392?hl=en)
- **16 July 2026 clarification**: three non-monetizable types: (1) generic, repetitive or template-based content made easily with AI,
  CGI or templates with little variation; (2) off-putting/distressing content made to upset or manipulate (example: an animal in
  distress then rescued); (3) AI personas of real people on finance, legal, health or medical topics. Channels with too much of any
  type can't monetize; channels dedicated to off-putting content are removed from YPP. Trust & safety chief Matt Halprin: the target is
  content farming, videos that are "very generic and don't really have a narrative arc"; AI itself is not banned —
  [TechCrunch, 20 July 2026](https://techcrunch.com/2026/07/20/youtube-clarifies-policies-around-ai-slop-and-upsetting-videos/)

**Reused content**
- Reused = repurposing content from YouTube or elsewhere "without adding significant original commentary, substantive modifications, or
  educational or entertainment value"; applies to the channel as a whole; "applies even if you have permission"; separate from
  copyright — [YPP policies](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/1311392?hl=en)

**Altered or synthetic content disclosure**
- Required when content "Makes a real person appear to say or do something they didn't do", "Alters footage of a real event or place",
  "Generates a realistic scene that didn't actually occur", or "Creates music that's the main focus of the video" —
  [YouTube Help: disclosing altered or synthetic content](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/14328491?hl=en)
- Not required for clearly unrealistic content, e.g. "Someone riding a unicorn through a fantastical world" or "an AI-generated or
  altered animation of a missile in a fully animated video"; also minor: AI for "outline, script, thumbnail, title, or infographic",
  caption creation, and "Cloning one's own voice to create voice overs or dubs"; YouTube may still show an AI label in the expanded
  description for some non-photorealistic content and AI audio — [same page](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/14328491?hl=en)
- "Disclosing AI content alone won't limit a video's audience or impact its eligibility to earn money"; consistent non-disclosure can
  bring penalties "including suspension from the YouTube Partner Program" — [same page](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/14328491?hl=en)

**Enforcement reports**
- Dec 2025: Screen Culture and KH Studio terminated (spam/misleading metadata) —
  [TheWrap](https://www.thewrap.com/industry-news/tech/youtube-terminates-screen-culture-kh-studio-over-ai-fake-movie-trailers/)
- Early 2026: AI-slop channels removed, including **CuentosFacinantes** (Dragon Ball-themed AI videos, ~5.9M subscribers, named by

  The classic monster-of-the-week loop (meet creature → battle → hero wins) is the "same situation… same outcome" pattern; rotate
  outcomes (loss, draw, refusal to fight, creature chooses someone else, rule backfires) as the channel already does for stories.
- Avoid the "animal in distress then rescued" hook as a recurring device (named example of off-putting content); creature injury
  scenes should serve story, not bait.
- Show the human authorship: owner's own lines, a recurring human-written narrator voice, behind-the-scenes or design-sketch Shorts,
  and an "About" that states the creators write and direct each episode.
- Disclosure: fully animated fantasy creatures need no toggle; turn it on only for realistic AI footage, AI music that is the focus of
  a video, or any real person's likeness/voice. Never use a real person's voice clone (already a channel rule).
- Fan-IP + AI is the riskiest combination seen in enforcement (Dragon Ball AI channel, AI fake trailers). Being original is both the
  legal and the monetization protection.

## 6. Copyright in our own AI-assisted characters and creatures (US, India) and AI tool licences

### Takeaway
Pure AI output is not protected in the US and its status is unsettled in India; human-made designs, human edits, selection/arrangement
and scripts are protectable. To own the creatures, keep the human hand in the designs (sketches, model sheets, edits) and records of it.

### Cited Findings
- US Copyright Office Part 2 (29 Jan 2025): "prompts alone do not provide sufficient human control"; protection is available where a
  human-authored work is perceptible in the output or for "creative arrangements or modifications of the output"; using AI assistance
  or including AI material in a larger human work does not by itself bar protection; no change in law needed —
  [Copyright Office NewsNet 1060](https://copyright.gov/newsnet/2025/1060.html);
  [IPWatchdog](https://ipwatchdog.com/2025/01/29/part-two-copyright-office-ai-report-says-creative-prompting-doesnt-constitute-authorship/)
- Thaler v. Perlmutter: D.C. Circuit (2025) upheld human-authorship requirement; Supreme Court denied certiorari on 2 March 2026;
  Allen v. Perlmutter (D. Colo.) pending on how much human input suffices —
  [Holland & Knight, Mar 2026](https://www.hklaw.com/en/insights/publications/2026/03/the-final-word-supreme-court-refuses-to-hear-case-on-ai-authorship);
  [Reed Smith](https://www.reedsmith.com/our-insights/blogs/viewpoints/102mlpl/supreme-court-denies-certiorari-in-thaler-v-perlmutter-human-only-rule-for-ai/)
- India: Copyright Act 1957 s.2(d)(vi) makes the author of a computer-generated work "the person who causes the work to be created"
  (1994 amendment); whether prompting counts as "causing" is unresolved —
  [Khurana & Khurana](https://www.khuranaandkhurana.com/ai-generated-content-and-the-copyright-act-1957-is-there-a-gap-in-indian-law);
  [Chambers IP 2026](https://gpg-pdf.chambers.com/Intellectual-Property-2026/120/)
- Indian Copyright Office rejected a DABUS application naming the AI as author; headline: "AI-generated work can get copyright but AI
  system cannot be author" — [Bar & Bench, 31 Aug 2026](https://www.barandbench.com/news/litigation/ai-generated-work-can-get-copyright-but-ai-system-cannot-be-author-indian-copyright-office);
  one blog says the order found Thaler was the s.2(d)(vi) person (unverified) —
  [Intepat](https://www.intepat.com/blog/dabus-copyright-order-india-ai-authorship)
- "Suryast" (Ankit Sahni + RAGHAV AI) registered in India in Nov 2020 with the AI as co-author; withdrawal notice Nov 2021; status
  reported as unresolved; refused in the US (Dec 2023) —
  [SpicyIP](https://spicyip.com/2023/12/ankit-sahnis-ai-co-authored-artwork-denied-registration-by-us-continues-to-be-registered-in-india.html)
- DPIIT committee (formed Apr 2025) working paper Part 1 (8 Dec 2025) covers AI **training** inputs; not a statute; output authorship
  left open — [S.S. Rana](https://ssrana.in/articles/who-owns-what-the-machine-makes-authorship-and-ownership-of-ai-generated-works-under-indian-law/)
- Tool licences: ElevenLabs free plan "does not include a commercial license" —
  [ElevenLabs help](https://elevenlabs.io/docs/help-center/legal/can-i-publish-the-content-i-generate-on-the-platform);
  Chatterbox is MIT-licensed and every output carries Resemble's imperceptible PerTh watermark —
  [GitHub resemble-ai/chatterbox](https://github.com/resemble-ai/chatterbox)

### Inferences
- For a monetized series, the ElevenLabs free plan is not usable for new episodes (no commercial licence); Chatterbox Turbo (MIT) is
  fine commercially; keep its watermark intact (channel rule: never strip provenance watermarks).
- To make the creatures ownable: owner/team sketches or paint-overs as the design source, a human-written bible (names, lore, rules),
  human editing of every AI image, and a dated design log. Register key designs in India (s.2(d)(vi) route, human named as author)
  if the series takes off.
- Check each image model's terms for output ownership and commercial use before use (not researched per tool here).

### Gaps
- Image-model terms (e.g. whichever generator the pipeline uses) not checked.
- Exact text and date of the Indian DABUS order not read.

## 7. Music and sound

### Takeaway
Use only owned, commissioned or properly licensed music (YouTube Audio Library is safe for monetized videos); write original motifs
and creature cries; AI music that is the main focus of a video needs disclosure.

### Cited Findings
- "If you're in the YouTube Partner Program, you can monetize videos with music and sound effects from the Audio Library"; such tracks
  "won't be claimed by a rights holder through the Content ID system"; Creative Commons tracks require crediting the artist in the
  description — [YouTube Audio Library help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/3376882?hl=en)
- AI-generated music that is the main focus of a video requires the altered/synthetic disclosure —
  [YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/14328491?hl=en)
- Reused-content policy lists "Songs modified to change the pitch or speed, but are otherwise identical to the original song" as not
  allowed — [YPP policies](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/1311392?hl=en)

### Inferences
- No Pokémon melodies, battle-theme interval patterns, level-up/heal jingles, evolution sting or cry samples, even re-performed (music
  copyright protects the melody, and a recognisable jingle also invites Content ID).
- The channel's own plucks + sub-bass bed works; give the series one original 3–5 note signature motif and original creature cries
  made from processed voice or foley.

### Gaps
- No Pokémon-specific Content ID cases were researched.

## 8. In-story rewards for subscribing ("every new subscriber gets a house with their name on it") and safer alternatives

### Takeaway
A reward given **in exchange for subscribing** (a viewer's name on a house or a creature because they subscribed) matches YouTube's
own spam-policy wording, "offering rewards in exchange for likes, views, or subscribers", so it is not safe for a monetized channel.
Asking people to subscribe is fine. Picking a comment and building or naming what it asks for, with no subscribe or like condition,
fits the policies far better. The "most liked comment" version sits in between, and the "made for kids" setting would switch comments
off altogether.

### Cited Findings
- Spam, deceptive practices and scams policy, item 1, "Engagement manipulation": "Repetitive or templated content aimed at
  artificially inflating engagement through bots, coercion, or offering rewards in exchange for likes, views, or subscribers."
  Example: "Using coordinated 'sub-for-sub' schemes." Allowed: "It's okay to ask viewers to like, comment, or subscribe."
  Consequences: "we may suspend your monetization or terminate your channel or account"; for some violations a warning or strike;
  "If you get 3 strikes within 90 days, your channel may be terminated" —
  [YouTube spam policy](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/2801973?hl=en)
- Fake engagement policy: "YouTube doesn't allow anything that artificially increases the number of views, likes, comments, or other
  metrics"; "Content that solely exists to incentivize viewers for engagement (views, likes, comments, etc) is prohibited"; bans
  "Offering to subscribe to another creator's channel only if they subscribe to your channel ('sub4sub')"; allowed: "You're allowed to
  encourage viewers to subscribe, hit the like button, share, or leave a comment". Violating content is removed; a first violation is
  usually a warning, then strikes; three strikes in 90 days may terminate the channel —
  [YouTube fake engagement policy](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/3399767?hl=en)
- Contest policy: a contest must be free to enter, Official Rules must say "YouTube is not a sponsor of your contest" and release
  YouTube from liability, and "You and any third-party may not manipulate metrics on the YouTube service to misrepresent genuine viewer
  engagement… views, likes, dislikes, or subscribers"; "You cannot ask the viewer to give all rights for, or transfer the ownership of,
  their entry to you" — [YouTube contest policies](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/1620498?hl=en)
- Giveaway-tool vendors read the fake engagement policy as forbidding a required subscription as an entry condition and recommend
  comment-based entries instead (vendor reading, not YouTube text) —
  [Gleam: YouTube subscribe entry rules](https://gleam.io/faq/legal-and-compliance/youtube-subscribe-entry-rules)
- Made-for-kids: content can be "made for kids" if it includes "characters, celebrities, or toys that appeal to children" (animated
  figures are one factor; no single factor decides) —
  [YouTube: determining if content is made for kids](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/9528076?hl=en). On made-for-kids
  videos "Comments", "Notifications", "Cards or end screens", "Personalized advertising" and others are unavailable —
  [YouTube: features unavailable on made for kids content](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/9632097?hl=en)
- No reported enforcement case was found specifically against "your name in my video if you subscribe" formats —
  [search result: only the policy pages came back](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/3399767?hl=en)

### Inferences
- **"Every new subscriber gets a house with their name on it": avoid.** Their name in the world is a reward, and subscribing is the
  condition. That is the case the spam policy names. Using names a viewer did not post publicly, or posting them without consent, adds
  privacy problems on top. Enforcement against this format seems light and is unreported, but channel-level demonetization or a strike
  is a lot to risk for a small growth trick.
- **"The most liked comment? I build whatever you ask": medium risk. Reword it.** The reward goes to one commenter, and the vote is the
  likes on comments, not the video's likes, views or subscribers, so it is not the listed case. It still invites people to like
  comments for a prize. Safer wording turns it into a story vote: "Comment what the village should build next. I'll pick one for
  Episode 4." The creator chooses, and likes are not a condition.
- **Safer alternatives, all with no subscribe or like condition:**
  - *Comment-driven choices:* end on the channel's existing either/or question ("Does Kiro keep the creature or set it free?") and let
    the next episode follow the side that made the stronger argument. This also fits the channel's debate rule.
  - *Naming from comments:* "Name this creature in the comments. The best name joins the story," credited to the commenter's public
    @handle. Keep the human choice in the creator's hands, never require a subscription or like, and keep the right to use the idea
    (the contest policy forbids taking ownership of entries, so treat it as a suggestion box, not a contest).
  - *Community posts and polls:* allowed, but the spam policy also lists exploiting Posts or Polls to force engagement, so use them for
    real story choices, not "like this to unlock".
  - *A plain subscribe ask* in the series chip or end tag ("Follow the journey") is explicitly allowed, and matches the channel's tested
    silent `END_TAG`.
- **If the series is set as made for kids**, comments are off, so comment choices and naming from comments won't work. Choose the
  audience setting honestly from YouTube's factors. A teen-or-older tone with real stakes and debate questions points to "not made for
  kids".

### Gaps
- No YouTube statement or reported case deals specifically with crediting subscribers or commenters by name inside an animation.
- Indian consumer and promotion rules for prize-style comment contests were not researched. A simple creative credit with no prize
  value is assumed.

## 9. Practical summary for the owner (analysis drawn from sections 1–8)

### Takeaway
Original designs + original names + no Pokémon marks in metadata + varied episode outcomes + visible human authorship = low legal and
monetization risk. The Palworld patents are a game problem, not a video problem.

### Cited Findings
- See sections 1–7 for each source.

### Inferences
- **Creatures:** design from real animals/myth/objects with a new rule each; avoid Pokémon silhouettes, the yellow electric-mouse
  mascot, starter-trio of fire/water/grass designs that echo the originals, and Pokémon type icons/colours.
- **Capture device:** not a ball; e.g., a song, a carved token, a shared name, a bargain, a tattoo, a lantern; avoid red/white split
  sphere and the beam-recall effect.
- **Words:** no "Poké", "-mon", "Pocket Monsters", "Gotta catch 'em all", "I choose you", "trainer badge/gym leader/Pokédex" trios
  used together; invent the world's own terms.
- **Metadata:** no Pokémon terms in title, thumbnail or tags; at most one non-affiliation line in the description.
- **Episodes:** different conflict and outcome each time; no repeated "animal suffers, hero saves" hook.
- **AI disclosure:** off for fully animated fantasy; on for realistic AI footage, featured AI music or any real-person likeness.
- **Voices/music:** commercial-licence tools only (not ElevenLabs free); Audio Library or original music; keep watermarks.
- **Ownership:** keep human design steps and dated records so the creatures are protectable.
- **Engagement:** no in-story reward for subscribing or liking (e.g. "your name on a house"). Use comment-driven story choices and
  creature names picked from comments, with no conditions, plus a plain subscribe ask.
- **Watch:** Tokyo District Court preliminary view on 9 Nov 2026; Nintendo's response to the US reexamination of 12,403,397.

### Gaps
- Everything above is research, not legal advice; a short consultation with an Indian IP lawyer before trademarking the series name
  is cheap insurance.
