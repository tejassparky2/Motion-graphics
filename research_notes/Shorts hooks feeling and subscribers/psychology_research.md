# Psychology of starting, finishing, feeling and sharing: evidence for 40-50 s story Shorts

Scope note: this file adds to `research_notes/Shorts retention for animated explainers/` (story_structure.md already covers
Loewenstein 1994, Kang et al. 2009 and Green & Brock 2000 briefly; visual_pacing_captions.md covers Cutting et al. shot
length; narration_pace_audio.md covers speech rate). New material here: Zeigarnik replication status, clickbait
experiments, emotion/virality effect sizes and their replications, controversy, transportation meta-analyses,
suspense/surprise, insight ("aha") memory, and Mayer meta-analyses. Evidence grade used below:
**STRONG** = meta-analysis or large field/registered study; **MODERATE** = several studies or one large field study,
not yet meta-analysed; **WEAK** = single small study, failed or mixed replication, or theory without direct test.

## 1. Curiosity / information gap: how big must the gap be, and does an over-hyped gap backfire?

### Takeaway
Curiosity is real and measurably boosts memory, and it peaks when people feel they *almost* know the answer (moderate
confidence), not when the gap is huge (MODERATE: several small fMRI/behavioural studies agree). But controlled
experiments find that "clickbait" wording tricks (questions, "you won't believe", superlatives, "this one thing")
do **not** reliably raise curiosity or clicks, and exaggeration costs credibility (MODERATE). The gap must be a
concrete, answerable question the viewer can nearly solve, not hype.

### Cited Findings
- Loewenstein (1994, *Psychological Bulletin* 116, 75-98) defines curiosity as "a form of cognitively induced deprivation that arises from the perception of a gap in knowledge or understanding" (information-gap theory). Theory paper, not an effect size. — [ResearchGate record](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/232440476_The_Psychology_of_Curiosity_A_Review_and_Reinterpretation)
- Kang, Hsu, Krajbich, Loewenstein, McClure, Wang & Camerer (2009, *Psychological Science*) "The Wick in the Candle of Learning": fMRI, **19 participants**, 40 trivia questions. Curiosity followed an **inverted U against confidence** (highest at moderate confidence); curiosity correlated with caudate (anticipated reward) activity; higher curiosity predicted better recall of answers ~10 days later, especially when the answer was surprising. Small sample. — [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2009.02402.x); [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=1308286)
- Wade & Kidd (2019, *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review*): curiosity is best predicted by a learner's **estimate** of their own knowledge rather than objective knowledge; people were most curious when "almost sure, but not completely sure" they knew the answer; curiosity gave "only a small boost in learning". — [Kidd Lab PDF](https://www.kiddlab.com/_files/ugd/0975fd_fe8691bfabc3409b96b14d668cc6bf39.pdf); [Learning & the Brain summary](https://www.learningandthebrain.com/blog/im-curious-does-curiosity-promote-learning/)
- Gruber, Gelman & Ranganath (2014, *Neuron* 84, 486-496): in immediate and one-day-delayed tests, people remembered better both the trivia answers they were curious about **and unrelated incidental material (faces) shown while they were curious**; midbrain and nucleus accumbens activity rose during high curiosity, and midbrain-hippocampus connectivity predicted the incidental-memory benefit. Small fMRI sample (exact N not retrieved here). Dopamine is the authors' interpretation, not directly measured. — [PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4252494/); [Neuroscience News](https://neurosciencenews.com/curiosity-memory-learning-neuroscience-1388)
- Scott (2021, *Journal of Pragmatics* 175, 53-66), corpus + relevance-theory analysis: clickbait headlines over-use (i) definite referring expressions ("this man", "the reason") and (ii) superlatives/intensifiers, to a statistically significant degree; they make readers open new "conceptual files" while giving little content to fill them. Descriptive, not an engagement experiment. — [Kingston eprints](https://eprints.kingston.ac.uk/47719); [AAM PDF](https://eprints.kingston.ac.uk/id/eprint/47719/3/Scott-K-47719-AAM.pdf)
- Molina, Sundar, Le, Lee, Hassan & Rony (CHI 2021) "Does Clickbait Actually Attract More Clicks?": Study 1 (MTurk, power analysis required 114) found participants clicked "read more" and "share" **more often on non-clickbait** headlines than on any of 7 clickbait features (e.g., 84 vs 55-77 read-more clicks; 23 vs 13-22 shares). Study 2 (**N = 249**, same story, headline varied by one clickbait feature only): **no significant effect** on read-more (Wald chi2 = 4.42, p = .73) or share (Wald chi2 = 2.91, p = .89), and no difference in perceived curiosity (F(7,236) = 1.22, p = .30), credibility or entertainment. Study 3: four automated clickbait detectors agreed only **47%** of the time. — [CHI 2021 PDF](https://pike.psu.edu/publications/chi21.pdf); [Penn State record](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/does-clickbait-actually-atract-more-clicks-three-clickbait-studie/)
- A controlled MTurk experiment (200 participants) found clickbait headlines significantly reduce the credibility of the same news item; a Dutch thesis (N = 140) found non-exaggerated headlines rated more credible. Secondary/thesis-level evidence (WEAK). — [Brock Univ. summary](https://kumu.brocku.ca/digitalculturesociety/Effects_of_Clickbait_Headlines_on_User_Responses:_An_Empirical_Investigation); [Radboud thesis](https://theses.ubn.ru.nl/handle/123456789/14307)
- Robertson, Pröllochs, Schwarzenegger, Pärnamets, Van Bavel & Feuerriegel (2023, *Nature Human Behaviour* 7, 812-822; Registered Report): Upworthy A/B tests (~105,000 headline variants; ~5.7 million clicks over 370 million+ impressions in the registered sample; final paper reports larger totals): each additional **negative** word raised click-through by about **2.3%**; positive words lowered it; sadness words had the largest effect per Nieman Lab coverage. Same story, different headline, so it isolates wording. STRONG (very large, preregistered), but about clicking a headline, not finishing a video. — [LMU open-access PDF](https://epub.ub.uni-muenchen.de/94951/1/s41562-023-01538-4.pdf); [Nieman Lab](https://www.niemanlab.org/2023/03/negative-words-in-news-headlines-generate-more-clicks-but-sad-words-are-more-effective-than-angry-or-scary-ones/%20)

### Inferences
- "How big must the gap be": the evidence says *moderate*. The best line-1 questions are ones a viewer thinks they can
  almost answer ("How did the kid make the genie's rule cancel itself?"), not mystery for its own sake ("You won't
  believe what happened"). That matches the channel's own data (scene-with-something-wrong hooks at 75-78%, bare topic
  questions at 36-52%).
- Hype words and "this one trick" phrasing are not shown to help and do cost credibility; Scott's two markers
  (definite "this kid / the reason" + superlatives) are what make a line read as clickbait. A concrete scene supplies
  the gap without the hype.
- Gruber et al. suggest a curiosity state also lifts memory for whatever is shown *during* it: keep the key logic step
  on screen while the question is still open, not after the reveal.
- Negativity raises clicks on titles (Robertson et al.), so a title that names a problem/threat ("The genie's rule
  that trapped him") may beat a cheerful one; this is about clicking, not about comments or completion.

### Gaps
- No experiment found that directly tests an *unresolved* gap (a video that never answers) against a resolved one for
  satisfaction or return viewing; the "over-promise backfires" claim rests on credibility studies, not video data.
- No peer-reviewed study of curiosity gaps in Shorts/TikTok hooks with retention as the outcome was found.

## 2. Zeigarnik effect and "open loops": what does the replication literature say?

### Takeaway
The memory version of the Zeigarnik effect (people remember unfinished tasks better) **did not hold up** in the 2025
meta-analysis; what does hold is the **Ovsiankina effect**, a general urge to *resume/finish* an interrupted task
(MODERATE-STRONG). So "open loops" are better justified as "people want to see things finished" than as "open loops
are remembered better". Curiosity (Section 1) is the better-evidenced mechanism for holding a viewer.

### Cited Findings
- Ghibellini & Meier (2025), "The hypothesis of the Zeigarnik effect: a meta-analysis", *Humanities and Social Sciences Communications* 12 (DOI 10.1057/s41599-025-05000-w): found **no memory advantage for unfinished tasks**, but a general tendency to **resume** them; "the Ovsiankina effect represents a general tendency, whereas the Zeigarnik effect lacks universal validity". The authors suggest the old effect depended on experimenter authority, situational demands and task involvement, which were more common in historical studies. — [IDEAS/RePEc abstract](https://ideas.repec.org/a/pal/palcom/v12y2025i1d10.1057_s41599-025-05000-w.html); [Wikipedia summary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zeigarnik_effect)
- Study counts reported in secondary sources conflict (one says 1,455 records screened / 59 kept; another says 37 studies after excluding Zeigarnik's 1927 data), and a ~67% resumption rate quoted by a blog could not be verified in the abstract. Treat these numbers as unverified. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zeigarnik_effect); [Yu-kai Chou blog](https://yukaichou.com/behavioral-analysis/zeigarnik-effect-incomplete-tasks-memory-tension/)
- Ghibellini's dissertation reports a later experiment in which a memory advantage for interrupted anagram tasks appeared but could not be attributed to the Zeigarnik mechanism. — [BORIS Theses](https://boristheses.unibe.ch/4796/)

### Inferences
- Keep the channel rule "open a loop in the first 5 s that only the ending closes", but justify it by curiosity +
  the urge to finish (Ovsiankina), not by Zeigarnik memory claims. Creator advice that cites "Zeigarnik" is citing a
  non-replicating effect.
- The Ovsiankina reading implies the loop must feel like a *task the viewer is doing* (guessing the riddle, picking a
  side), which favours "pause and guess" moments and character dilemmas over passive teasers.
- An open loop in a Short is closed within 40 s, so the open-loop effect may matter mostly as curiosity; stacking
  several unresolved teasers adds little evidence-backed value.

### Gaps
- Pooled effect sizes and CIs for both effects could not be retrieved (paywalled/redirected full text).
- No study found testing open loops in video retention specifically.

## 3. Narrative transportation, identification, and suspense vs surprise vs curiosity

### Takeaway
Being "lost in a story" reliably increases story-consistent beliefs and liking of characters (STRONG: two
meta-analyses), but the effects are small-to-moderate (r about .17-.23 for narratives overall). Structural-affect
theory gives three distinct tools: **curiosity** (viewer knows something is missing from the past), **suspense**
(viewer fears/hopes about what will happen) and **surprise** (hidden info revealed, forcing reinterpretation). Recent
audiovisual experiments show each structure produces narrative tension (MODERATE). Spoiler research is mixed (WEAK).

### Cited Findings
- Green & Brock (2000, *JPSP* 79, 701-721): transportation (attention + imagery + feelings) increased story-consistent beliefs and favourable evaluations of protagonists; highly transported readers found fewer "false notes". — [PDF](http://www.communicationcache.com/uploads/1/0/8/8/10887248/the_role_of_transportation_in_the_persuasiveness_of_public_narratives.pdf)
- van Laer, de Ruyter, Visconti & Wetzels (2014, *Journal of Consumer Research* 40(5), 797-817), "The Extended Transportation-Imagery Model": meta-analysis of **132 effect sizes from 76 articles**; transportation's antecedents include identifiable characters, imaginable plot and verisimilitude (pooled values not retrieved here). A 2019 follow-up (64 articles, 138 effect sizes, *Journal of Business Research* 96) found transportation effects stronger in commercial than non-commercial domains. — [City Research Online](https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/id/eprint/6755/); [IDEAS 2019 follow-up](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jbrese/v96y2019icp135-146.html)
- Braddock & Dillard (2016, *Communication Monographs*), meta-analysis of 74 studies: narrative exposure shifted story-consistent **beliefs r = .17 (k = 37, N = 7,376), attitudes r = .19 (k = 40, N = 7,132), intentions r = .17 (k = 28, N = 5,211), behaviours r = .23 (k = 5, N = 978)**; little evidence that medium or fictionality changed the size. Zebregs et al. (2015) instead found narratives work better for intentions than for beliefs/attitudes. — [SSRC MediaWell](https://mediawell.ssrc.org/?p=52168); [The Decision Lab](https://thedecisionlab.com/insights/society/can-stories-change-us)
- Brewer & Lichtenstein (1982) structural-affect theory: surprise = critical early info withheld then revealed, forcing reinterpretation; curiosity = an event omitted but the reader knows it is missing; suspense = an initiating event with an uncertain outcome the reader cares about. Sternberg: curiosity concerns the story's past, suspense its future. — [Project MUSE excerpt](https://muse.jhu.edu/article/51535/pdf); [Connotations](https://www.connotations.de/wp-content/uploads/2017/07/fishelov01413.pdf)
- Bermejo-Berros, López-Díez & Gil Martínez (2022, *Poetics*): four versions of one audiovisual story, **94 participants**; the suspense, surprise and curiosity structures each induced narrative tension, and curiosity played a triggering/organising role in suspense and surprise. Single moderate study. — [Complutense repository](https://docta.ucm.es/entities/publication/a6f0066a-69e1-4b27-82f7-ee38de07d4b9)
- Spoilers: Leavitt & Christenfeld (2011) found spoiled short stories were enjoyed **more**; a replication with **215 students** (Levine, Betzner & Autry) found spoilers before a story **reduced** enjoyment and mid-story spoilers had no effect; reviewers conclude the direction is unsettled. — [OnFiction research bulletin](https://onfiction.ca/2017/04/research-bulletin-spoiler-alert-not-all.html)

### Inferences
- Genie/riddle stories naturally combine all three Brewer-Lichtenstein tools: curiosity (how will the kid beat the
  rule?), suspense (will the genie grant it?), surprise (the twist line). Make the surprise a *reinterpretation* (the
  rule turns on itself), which is the theory's definition, not just a random shock.
- Transportation needs an identifiable character in an imaginable scene: this independently supports "line 1 =
  character mid-action" and against "Meet X" or topic statements.
- Effects of narrative on belief are small (r about .2), so stories help a big-question debate land, but the logic
  still has to be stated clearly.
- Since spoiler evidence is mixed, do not reveal the twist in the title or hook; it is not shown to help and may hurt.

### Gaps
- No study found on transportation in sub-60-second video; transportation scales are built for longer stories.
- Character-identification effect sizes specific to animated/cartoon characters were not found.

## 4. Emotion and virality: what drives shares vs comments vs completion, and the risks of outrage

### Takeaway
High-arousal emotions (awe, anger, anxiety, amusement) predict sharing; low-arousal sadness predicts less sharing
(MODERATE: one large field study + small lab studies; the awe/anger finding is widely cited but not meta-analysed).
Moral-emotional words and out-group language reliably raise shares (STRONG for moral contagion after a 4.8 million-post
meta-analysis, at a smaller size than first reported: ~12% per word, not 20%). Outrage boosts engagement but drives
"angry" reactions and in-group-only spread. No good peer-reviewed data link specific emotions to *completion* of
short videos.

### Cited Findings
- Berger & Milkman (2012, *Journal of Marketing Research*, DOI 10.1509/jmr.10.0353): **6,956 New York Times articles** over three months, outcome = making the most-emailed list; a random **2,566** were coded by three blind raters per dimension (interrater alphas > .70). A one-SD increase in **anger raised the odds of making the list by 34%** (equal to 2.9 extra hours as the lead story) and a one-SD increase in **awe by 30%**; sadness was negatively linked (Figure 2 shows about -16%); more positive content was more viral overall; results held controlling for surprise, interest and practical value. Lab follow-ups were small (N = 49, 45, 47) and showed arousal mediated willingness to share. — [Wharton PDF](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2011/11/Virality.pdf); [Northeastern copy](https://cssh.northeastern.edu/pandemic-teaching-initiative/wp-content/uploads/sites/43/2020/09/What-Makes-Online-Content-Viral.pdf)
- Brady, Wills, Jost, Tucker & Van Bavel (2017, *PNAS*): ~563,312 tweets on three polarising moral/political issues; each additional moral-emotional word increased retweets by about **20%** (IRR 1.20); diffusion was stronger **within** liberal or conservative networks than between them. — [ResearchGate PDF](https://researchgate.net/profile/Jay-Van-Bavel/publication/317947723_Emotion_shapes_the_diffusion_of_moralized_content_in_social_networks/links/5f92152f458515b7cf96abb2/Emotion-shapes-the-diffusion-of-moralized-content-in-social-networks.pdf); [NYU CSMaP](https://csmapnyu.org/research/academic-research/emotion-shapes-the-diffusion-of-moralized-content-in-social-networks)
- Brady, Rathje, Globig & Van Bavel (2025, *PNAS Nexus*): preregistered replication (**N = 849,266**, IRR = 1.15) plus meta-analysis (5 labs, 27 studies, **N = 4,821,006**): **IRR = 1.12, 95% CI [1.06, 1.19]**, i.e., ~12% more shares per moral-emotional word; smaller than the original 20% but "highly robust"; conceptually replicated with GPT-3.5 coding. — [Kellogg summary](https://www.kellogg.northwestern.edu/academics-research/research/detail/2025/estimating-the-effect-size-of-moral-contagion-in-online/)
- Rathje, Van Bavel & van der Linden (2021, *PNAS*): 2.7 million+ Facebook and Twitter posts from news media and US Congress members; each word referring to the political **out-group** increased the odds of sharing by about **67%** (pooled; ~35-57% in news-media data, ~65-180% in Congress data); out-group language strongly predicted "angry" reactions; out-group posts were shared about twice as often as in-group posts. — [PMC full text](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8256037/); [BPS Research Digest](https://www.bps.org.uk/research-digest/social-media-posts-are-hostile-towards-political-opponents-get-more-shares)
- Robertson et al. (2023): negative words raise headline clicks ~2.3% per word (see Section 1); coverage reports sad words were more effective for clicks than angry or fearful ones, i.e., the emotion that raises *clicks* (sadness) differs from the one that raises *sharing* in Berger & Milkman (arousal). — [Nieman Lab](https://www.niemanlab.org/2023/03/negative-words-in-news-headlines-generate-more-clicks-but-sad-words-are-more-effective-than-angry-or-scary-ones/%20)
- Short-video emotion data are thin: a 2025 study of 50 Samsung posts found joy/affection videos got significantly more likes than curiosity videos, but no significant emotion-engagement link on Instagram (WEAK, small, brand content). Industry (non-peer-reviewed) claims, e.g., Realeyes facial-coding figures cited by WARC, should not be relied on. — [Rhetoric.bg PDF](https://rhetoric.bg/wp-content/uploads/2025/12/Roland-Impuscatu-issue-64-July-2025-NEW_DOI.pdf); [WARC](https://www.warc.com/newsandopinion/news/mastering-short-form-video/en-gb/43157)

### Inferences
- For shares, aim for an arousing emotion: awe (a mind-bending paradox), amusement (the genie's freeze and the
  callback), mild indignation on the kid's behalf at an unfair rule. Avoid ending on sadness or a flat mood.
- Moral words ("fair", "cheat", "deserve", "punish") reliably add spread, but the boost is mostly **within** like-minded
  groups; for a debate channel that wants both sides commenting, frame the moral stakes around a dilemma rather than
  an out-group to blame.
- Out-group animosity is the strongest single share predictor found (~67% per term) and also the most toxic
  (anger reactions); for religion/money topics this is the main rage-bait trap the owner's "always meaningful, no
  rage-bait" rule already excludes.
- Different behaviours have different levers: titles (clicks) respond to negativity; sharing responds to arousal and
  moral words; comments respond to moderate controversy (Section 5); completion responds mostly to curiosity and
  clarity (Sections 1, 7). One video can use all four at different points.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed study found that measures which emotions drive **completion** of short videos.
- Humour's effect on sharing was not separately quantified in the sources retrieved (Berger & Milkman did not code
  amusement); this remains a gap.
- Berger & Milkman's per-emotion odds for anxiety, interest, surprise and practical value are in a figure whose
  extracted labels were ambiguous; only the anger (34%) and awe (30%) values stated in the text are reported here.

## 5. Moral dilemmas, controversy and debate: how much controversy, and how to keep it constructive

### Takeaway
Controversy increases conversation only **up to a moderate level**; beyond that, discomfort makes people talk less
(MODERATE: five studies incl. a field study of comment counts). Anonymity and talking with close friends shift the
peak upward. Online comment sections are relatively anonymous, which allows somewhat hotter topics, but the most
controversial topics still drew fewer comments in the field data.

### Cited Findings
- Chen & Berger (2013, *Journal of Consumer Research*, DOI 10.1086/671465), "When, Why, and How Controversy Causes Conversation", five studies: controversy increases likelihood of discussion at low levels, but beyond a moderate level, additional controversy decreases it (inverted U). Mechanism: controversy raises **interest** (more talk) and **discomfort** (less talk). Field data from Topix.com: articles on the **most** controversial topics had fewer comments than moderately controversial ones. Anonymity and conversation partner moderate it; with a close friend people were more willing to discuss highly controversial topics. — [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/jconrs/doi10.1086-671465.html); [Penn repository](https://repository.upenn.edu/handle/20.500.14332/39527); [Psychology Today summary](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/ulterior-motives/201309/controversy-and-conversation)
- Brady et al. (2017) found moral-emotional content spreads mainly within ideological groups, and Rathje et al. (2021) found out-group terms drive "angry" reactions (see Section 4), the main risks of high-controversy framing. — [NYU CSMaP](https://csmapnyu.org/research/academic-research/emotion-shapes-the-diffusion-of-moralized-content-in-social-networks); [PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8256037/)

### Inferences
- The "moderate controversy" sweet spot fits genie/riddle dilemmas ("Did the kid cheat, or win fair?") and
  philosophical either/ors (free will, time-travel paradoxes) better than direct identity fights (whose religion is
  right). For God/religion topics, frame the question around an idea or thought experiment (e.g., "Can an all-powerful
  being make a rock it can't lift?") rather than group membership, to keep interest high and discomfort lower.
- Discomfort is the brake: giving viewers a safe "side" to take (two defensible answers, both presented fairly, one
  character arguing each) lowers the social cost of commenting; this matches the owner's debate rule.
- Comment replies that end on a question keep the interest side of the curve active without raising the stakes.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed study of trolley-problem or moral-dilemma *videos* and comment counts was found within this search.
- No experiment found testing "both sides shown fairly" vs one-sided framing on comment volume or civility for
  short video.

## 6. Short-form video specifics (pacing, captions, faces, speech rate, music, humour)

### Takeaway
Peer-reviewed evidence specific to TikTok/Shorts retention is sparse; most "first 3 seconds" and "cut every 2 s" rules
come from industry data (covered in the earlier notes). The academic results that transfer best are Cutting's
shot-length work (1/f rhythm, shorter shots carry more motion), clickbait and negativity experiments for titles, and
Mayer's signaling/segmenting meta-analyses for on-screen text (Section 7).

### Cited Findings
- Cutting, DeLong & Nothelfer (2010, *Psychological Science*): 150 Hollywood films 1935-2005; shot lengths increasingly follow a 1/f pattern resembling human attention fluctuations. Already covered in visual_pacing_captions.md. — [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0956797610361679)
- WARC (industry, not peer-reviewed) reports consumers are almost twice as likely to watch a six-second ad to the end as a 30-second ad. — [WARC](https://www.warc.com/newsandopinion/news/mastering-short-form-video/en-gb/43157)
- Searches for peer-reviewed 2023-2025 studies linking narrative or emotional content to TikTok completion rate returned only industry pieces, small brand-post studies and theses. — [Rhetoric.bg PDF](https://rhetoric.bg/wp-content/uploads/2025/12/Roland-Impuscatu-issue-64-July-2025-NEW_DOI.pdf); [Univ. of Vaasa thesis](https://osuva.uwasa.fi/bitstreams/567b4c88-fe73-4ca6-9377-9c68cef57e65/download)

### Inferences
- Treat platform-specific "rules" as hypotheses to test against the channel's own retention data (which already
  outperforms generic hook scorers, per CLAUDE.md), and rely on the better-evidenced general mechanisms (curiosity,
  clarity, arousal, moderate controversy).

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed evidence retrieved on: faces/eye contact in the first frame of short video and swipe rate; captions
  and sound-off viewing in Shorts; music in short story videos; humour and completion. (Speech-rate and caption
  findings from other media are in narration_pace_audio.md and visual_pacing_captions.md.)

## 7. Memory and learning: the "aha" reward, explaining paradoxes, and one-step-per-sentence clarity

### Takeaway
Solving something with an "aha" feeling is remembered better (MODERATE: replicated across magic tricks and other
tasks) and engages reward-related brain activity in reward-sensitive people (WEAK-MODERATE: one EEG study). Mayer's
signaling (d = .38, meta-analysis) and segmenting (small-to-medium, meta-analysis) principles support highlighting each
step on screen and breaking logic into short chunks (STRONG for learning outcomes, though mostly from lab lessons
longer than a Short).

### Cited Findings
- Danek, Fraps, von Müller, Grothe & Öllinger (2013, *Psychological Research*), "Aha! experiences leave a mark": **50 participants**, 34 magic-trick videos; after 14 days, **64.4%** of solutions reached with an Aha! were recalled vs **52.4%** of non-insight solutions. — [Heidelberg record](https://heibib.ub.uni-heidelberg.de/search/Record/1910700959)
- Danek & Wiley (2020, *Cognition*), "What causes the insight memory advantage?": best explained by reaching a correct solution plus confidence plus enjoyment; sudden restructuring itself was not required. — [PsychArchives](https://www.psycharchives.org/handle/20.500.12034/2732); [PDF](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/cognitive-bias/illusion-of-depth/2020-danek.pdf)
- A 2024 review (*Cognitive, Affective, & Behavioral Neuroscience*) notes the insight memory boost has also appeared in other tasks such as degraded-image identification. — [NSF PAR PDF](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10557988)
- Oh, Chesebrough, Erickson, Zhang & Kounios (2020, *NeuroImage*): EEG during anagram solving; insight solutions triggered a burst of reward-related (orbitofrontal gamma) activity, but **only in people high in reward sensitivity**; low-sensitivity people's insights were "lacking in hedonic content". Dopamine not measured; popular "orgasmic" headlines overstate it. — [NSF story](https://www.nsf.gov/news/creativity-feels-really-good-some-aha-aaaah); [Drexel record](https://researchdiscovery.drexel.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/An-insight-related-neural-reward-signal/991019167321004721)
- Signaling meta-analysis (ETR&D 2020, 68; usually attributed to Alpizar, Adesope & Wong): **44 effect sizes, 29 experiments, 2,726 participants**, overall **d = .38**; benefits larger in higher-quality studies. — [WSU record](https://rex.libraries.wsu.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/A-meta-analysis-of-signaling-principle-in/99900601052901842)

### Inferences
- The insight advantage depends on the viewer *reaching* the solution with confidence and enjoyment, so the reveal
  should land as "oh, of course" (the rule turns on itself) a beat after the viewer has had a chance to guess. A short
  pause before the answer (already done with `clear_dialogue`) is consistent with this.
- Paradox explainers feel rewarding when the final step makes the earlier confusion click; a skipped step makes
  confidence impossible, which removes the memory/enjoyment benefit. This supports the channel's
  one-step-per-sentence rule.
- Signaling (d about .4): show each logic step as a card/counter/highlight at the moment it is spoken. Segmenting: a
  pause or visual beat between steps; in a 40-50 s Short this means sentence-level takes with real pauses, as the
  pipeline already does.
- Reward responses vary by person (Oh et al.), so the aha alone will not hook everyone; pair it with humour or a
  debate question.

### Gaps
- Mayer-principle meta-analyses come from instructional lessons, not entertainment Shorts; transfer to 40 s is assumed.
- The segmenting meta-analysis's exact pooled d values were not retrieved.
- No study found on whether paradox *resolution* (vs leaving it open) changes comments or shares.
