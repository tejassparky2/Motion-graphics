# Make viewers care before the jokes stop

Developer-and-AI skits connect when the viewer knows what someone wants before anything sad happens, when the jokes stop at the turn and the scene is left quiet for a moment, and when the ending brings two characters closer instead of leaving them sad. Our two rejected episodes did the reverse: they ran at 132 and 143 words a minute with a cut before every line, against 84-97 words a minute and 3.6-7.5 s average shots in the owner's five reference skits. Their clipped lines ("So?" "I'm busy." "No.") had no context underneath, so they read as cold rather than restrained. The research on being moved explains why the warm ending matters. The emotion is set off by a *sudden increase in closeness* between people (kama muta), while sadness on its own lowers the odds of sharing by about 16% ([Zickfeld et al. 2019](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/2c09161x); [Berger & Milkman](https://jonahberger.com/wp-content/uploads/2013/02/ViralityB.pdf)). In the 2025-2026 niche itself, the big numbers go to live-action comedy with a sting: job dread, a CEO punished, AI turning villain. In the animated, personified-AI corner, one existential gag stands out: Agent Poly's 83-second "Prove you're human." reached 355,913 views on a 418-subscriber channel. Its tender episodes stayed at 118-1,970 views, and companion-style scenes drew "don't vent to a soulless AI" pushback ([Agent Poly Shorts](https://www.youtube.com/@theAgentPoly/shorts); [h-mp0yKHuk8](https://www.youtube.com/shorts/h-mp0yKHuk8)). Developers' own public writing supplies the material: mourning the craft (Hacker News essays at 824-865 points), feeling like a fraud after an AI-assisted pull request, a 60-year-old "chasing the midnight hour" (1,086 points), parents building with their kids, agents that apologize more dramatically than the person they hurt. Hacker News is abbreviated HN from here on. The practical rules are these. Give Dev a want in the first 10 s. Run a comedy game, then stop it. Hold one 2-5 s quiet beat on the listener's face, with the music out and room tone kept under it. Land one sincere act. End on closeness plus a callback laugh. Pace at about 90 words a minute with 8-14 cuts per minute of video.

## Our rejected episodes ran 50% faster and cut three to six times as often as the references

The clearest evidence in this report is our own measurement. Whisper-medium transcripts and ffmpeg scene detection on the owner's five reference skits, each 51-60 s, found **84-97 words per minute overall** and **6-14 cuts per video**, an average shot of **3.6-7.5 s**. Each had **10-21 pauses longer than 0.6 s**, and the longest pause per video ran **2.2-5.5 s**. Episodes 14 ("I got promoted", 39.6 s) and 15 ("My junior asked me", 32.3 s), which the owner rejected as "not connecting", ran **132 and 143 words per minute** with a cut before every line, about **1.3 s per shot** (owner reference study, Whisper medium plus ffmpeg scene detection, Oct 2026).

| Measure | Owner's 5 reference skits | Ep 14 / Ep 15 (rejected) |
|---|---|---|
| Length | 51-60 s | 39.6 s / 32.3 s |
| Words per minute (overall) | 84-97 | 132 / 143 |
| Cuts per video | 6-14 | about one per line |
| Average shot | 3.6-7.5 s | about 1.3 s |
| Pauses over 0.6 s | 10-21 | few; scripted gaps between lines of 0.2-0.6 s |
| Longest pause | 2.2-5.5 s | three scripted holds of 1.5-2.6 s in ep 14 |

The arithmetic shows the fix is air, not cutting words. Ep 14 held about 87 words and ep 15 about 77. Spoken at the references' ~90 words a minute, the same scripts would run about 58 s and 51 s, inside the 51-60 s reference band. The scripts were not too long; they were played too fast and with no room to breathe. The scripts also show why the speed hurt. Ep 15 runs "Just ask Claude." / "I did. It fixed it." / "So?" / "I still don't know why it broke." / "I'm busy." with gaps of 0.35-0.5 s and a new shot on each line, and its one sincere line ("Someone did this for me once.") arrives 0.5 s after a gasp tag and is cut away from almost at once ([ep15_junior.py](../channels/lowpoly/ep15_junior.py)). Ep 14 already had three silent holds, but its spoken lines were packed so tightly that the overall rate still came out at 132 words a minute ([ep14_promoted.py](../channels/lowpoly/ep14_promoted.py)).

This overturns one rule in the earlier retention report: "cut on every speaker change with a reaction beat, no held two-shots and no dead air". That rule came from long-form production advice and from a vendor's "cut every 2-4 s" claim that cites no data ([MrBeast doc](https://prog.sijanec.eu/dir/How-To-Succeed-At-MrBeast-Production.pdf); [OpusClip, unsourced](https://opus.pro/blog/ideal-youtube-shorts-length-format-retention)). The references the owner chose for feeling, comedy references included, hold shots three to six times longer. The way to reconcile the two is to separate *dead air* from a *held beat*. Dead air is a frame where nothing changes. A held beat is a frame where a character is visibly thinking (eyes dart, a blink, breathing, a slow push-in), and it is exactly what Lasseter's "moving hold" and Murch's silence describe ([Lasseter, SIGGRAPH '94](https://education.siggraph.hosting.acm.org/static/HyperGraph/animation/character_animation/principles/lasseter_s94.htm); [Designing Sound on Murch](https://designingsound.org/2011/11/29/silence/)). One more caution comes from inside the repo. The Interestingly Strange narrator settings (0.24 s after a full stop) were measured on narrated story Shorts whose human narrators "don't make long silences" ([out/voice_rhythm.md](../out/voice_rhythm.md)). Those settings are right for a narrator and wrong for a two-character emotional skit, and the two must not share defaults.

The reference set is five videos chosen by one viewer, so it measures the owner's taste, not the market. It is still the right benchmark, because the owner is the person who said "not connecting".

## Emotion spreads when it rises into closeness, not when it lingers in sadness

The story-craft sources agree on what makes an audience invest quickly, and each rule maps onto a failure in eps 14-15. Andrew Stanton calls "Make me care" the first commandment. He describes the character's "inner motor, a dominant, unconscious goal that they're striving for, an itch that they can't scratch", and argues for "the unifying theory of two plus two. Make the audience put things together. Don't give them four" ([Stanton TED transcript, LingQ copy](https://www.lingq.com/de/englisch-lernen-online/courses/58847/andrew-stanton-the-clues-to-a-great-st-235924/)). Emma Coats' Pixar rules add that "You admire a character for trying more than for their successes", "Give your characters opinions. Passive/malleable... is poison to the audience", and "Come up with your ending before you figure out your middle" ([Pixar Touch](https://www.pixartouchbook.com/blog/2011/5/15/pixar-story-rules-one-version.html)). A Dev who says "I'm busy" is not trying at anything we can see, and a junior who says "I'll figure it out" has no stake we were shown. Each needs, inside the first 5-10 s, one specific thing they want or stand to lose.

The psychology says what the peak should feel like. Kama muta, measured with 3,542 people in 19 countries and 15 languages, is "evoked by experiencing or observing a sudden intensification of communal sharing". It shows up as "a warm feeling in the chest, moist eyes or tears, chills... a lump in the throat" ([Zickfeld et al. 2019](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/2c09161x)). A five-country video study found the same signs when people watched short heartwarming clips, and found that the feeling made viewers feel communal with the characters ([University of Haifa record](https://cris.haifa.ac.il/en/publications/kama-muta-similar-emotional-responses-to-touching-videos-across-t/)). Berger and Milkman supply the warning. In their sharing data, high-arousal emotions spread (anger +34%, awe +30%, anxiety +21% per standard deviation), while sadness *lowered* the odds by about 16%. In their experiment a sadder version of the same story was both less arousing (2.75 vs 3.89) and less likely to be shared (2.39 vs 3.80) ([Berger & Milkman PDF](https://jonahberger.com/wp-content/uploads/2013/02/ViralityB.pdf)). Their data are New York Times articles, not Shorts, so they show a direction, not an exact size. Still, the two lines of evidence agree: aim for a warm rise (two characters suddenly closer, loyal or self-sacrificing), not a sad fade. Poignancy, the mixed emotion "of meaningful endings, of no longer having something that one once had", is the bittersweet middle ground, and developer life is full of it: deprecated models, last commits, a craft that changed ([Advances in Psychological Science, 2013](https://journal.psych.ac.cn/adps/EN/Y2013/V21/I9/1643)).

Comedy and feeling have to take turns. Jane Espenson: "when the going gets really serious, the joking should stop", because "When an audience laughs, they're relieving tension." Her exceptions are the jokes that *don't* release tension: a character joking to relieve their own tension when "it isn't working", being "bitterly self-deprecating", or trying to make an angry person laugh. "They're endearing. Heart-crushing. But they're not funny" ([Espenson](https://janeespenson.com/102006/how-to-write-jokes-that-arent-funny)). Ep 14 broke this rule. Its sincere run ("You knew what to build... That part was you.") was followed 0.5 s later by "The typing part was me." and "Fair. [chuckle]", so the laugh let the tension out before it had registered. The fix is a deliberate order: comedy setup, then the jokes stop, one held beat, one sincere line or act, and only *then* a callback laugh.

Dialogue research explains why clipped lines failed. Subtext is "the gap between what a character says and what they mean", and writers often "mistake subtext for withholding or vagueness" ([Final Draft](https://www.finaldraft.com/blog/how-to-write-subtext-in-dialogue); [Final Draft, mastering subtext](https://www.finaldraft.com/blog/mastering-subtext-in-screenwriting)). Stanton's version: "It's this well-organized absence of information that draws us in" ([LingQ transcript](https://www.lingq.com/de/englisch-lernen-online/courses/58847/andrew-stanton-the-clues-to-a-great-st-235924/)). A one-word "No." moves an audience only when they already hold what lies underneath it. Apple's "Misunderstood" is the model. A teenager looks cold and glued to his phone all through a family Christmas, then plays the home movie he had been secretly filming, and every cold moment before it is reframed ([Engadget](https://www.engadget.com/2013-12-17-apple-airs-new-holiday-ad-misunderstood-featuring-iphone-5s-a.html)). Thai Life's "Unsung Hero" repeats one small unrewarded act several times before revealing its effect (17 million views at four weeks, agency-reported) ([Cannes Lions](https://lovethework.com/work-awards/campaigns/unsung-hero-222662)). Both are cheap to animate and fit 60-90 s.

Google's "Loretta" carries the strongest warning for an AI-character channel. An old widower asks the assistant to help him remember his late wife's favourite flowers and films. It drew an intense emotional response from 49% of test viewers, the highest Unruly had measured ([Unruly](https://unruly.co/?p=36918)). Other viewers found it creepy that an AI held a dead wife's memories ([WBUR](https://www.wbur.org/hereandnow/2020/02/04/googles-super-bowl-commercial)). The working rule for our cast follows: the AI is the witness or bridge to a human bond, never the replacement for it.

Some premises are now saturated. Since February 2026, "mourning the craft" has been its own genre on Hacker News, and the grief is about losing the part of the job people loved, more than about losing the job. I don't miss writing code at all. Agentic engineering is much more fun" ([HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=46926657)). ## The craft rulebook: pacing, story, dialogue, acting, camera, music, voice

Each rule below carries an evidence grade. **[Ours]** means measured on the owner's references or our episodes. **[Source]** means stated by a cited authority or study. **[Start]** means a starting value chosen by the researchers, to be tuned against the owner's reaction and stayed-to-watch. No source gave push-in speeds, BPM ranges for "tender", dB levels for room tone or a frame-exact "thought beat", so those numbers are starting points.

| Area | Rule | Values | Grade and source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pacing | Speak at reference pace; get length from air, not padding | 85-100 words per minute overall; 0.6-1.2 s between lines; 10-20 pauses over 0.6 s per minute | [Ours] reference study |
| Pacing | Hold shots; cut on thought changes, not on every line | 8-14 cuts per minute of video; average shot 3.5-7.5 s; no reaction cut before every line on emotional episodes | [Ours] |

Put together for a 60 s episode of about 90 words, the rules give a beat map. 0-3 s: frame 0 already moving, something wrong, hook text on screen. 3-10 s: Dev's want and the story device. 10-30 s: the comedy game, stepping up two or three times, with real pauses between lines. Near 30 s: a reveal or reversal that starts the turn. 30-45 s: the jokes stop, the music fades, the camera pushes in, a 2-4 s held beat on the listener, then one sincere line or act. 45-55 s: closeness restored, the motif returns warmer. 55-60 s: the callback laugh, cut about 0.5 s after. The timings are inference, built from the reference measurements, Espenson's order and the earlier finding that viewers swipe most at the start and the tail ([Dashlet](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2204.12954)). The two-sided question goes in the pinned comment and description, as Agent Poly does ("Claude or Codex: who's your backup when the limit hits?"), not in the audio ([evbWVHiCfAI](https://www.youtube.com/shorts/evbWVHiCfAI)).

## Conclusion

The owner's complaint about eps 14-15 was not mainly about which emotion we chose or which premise. It was about tempo. The same words, played at the references' pace with fewer cuts, would have filled the 51-60 s the references run. The comedy-first rulebook from the earlier research (cut every line, no dead air) tuned the episodes for a laugh and left no room for a feeling. Speed is not the whole problem, though: a slower ep 15 would still have opened on a Dev with no visible want and spent its sincere line before the silence earned it. A fix that changes only the pacing parameters will not be enough. Each episode needs a want in the first 10 s, a withheld piece, a turn where the jokes stop, and a warm rise. The new pace matters because it gives those beats room to land.

The market evidence calls for a hedge. The tender, companion-style episode the owner admires has the weakest record of reach, even on the reference channel. The two angles with evidence behind them are an AI facing its own identity or ending, framed so viewers feel for it without being asked to befriend it, and a warm human bond with the AI as the bridge. Developers have documented the second in detail and nobody has yet animated it. Both should be tested against the channel's own stayed-to-watch, alongside a comedy control, before the channel commits to a register. The grief-versus-joy splits in developer writing (two 60-year-olds, one tool, opposite verdicts) offer what the comedy premises have lost to saturation: material nobody else in the format has used, with a debate already built in.
