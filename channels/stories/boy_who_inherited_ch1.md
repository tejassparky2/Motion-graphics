## Part A. Continuity sheet: confirmed facts (from the bible)

**Story**
- A psychological thriller about death, consciousness transfer, wealth, identity, revenge and survival.
- Themes:
  - identity versus physical appearance;
  - the cost of survival;
  - wealth and power;
  - family succession and betrayal;
  - love, sacrifice and grief;
  - being alive versus truly living;
  - whether intelligence can overcome physical weakness and social danger.

**Neil**
- Age 17. A talented, analytical trader. His intelligence and trading skill are central to who he is.
- Starts with **$1,000** and grows his trading account to **$5,014,382.61**.
- Dies while protecting **Maya**.
- After death, his consciousness transfers by a cosmic law into the **nearest eligible living human body**.
- Limits: his trading mind does not make him an expert in other fields, and it gives his elderly body no youthful
  abilities.

**Maya**
- Deeply important to Neil. He dies protecting her.
- Their relationship and his death must carry emotional weight, and her importance to the plot is preserved.

**The transfer rule**
- Consciousness goes to the nearest eligible living human body.
- In the established case, the nearest eligible person was about **100 metres** away. That distance stays relevant.
- The rule is a central mystery. Its full explanation comes only when the plot supports it.

**Arthur Vance**
- Age **82**. A billionaire. His body is old, and his health is failing.
- His wealth, social position, family, and enemies become Neil's reality.
- Neil inherits Arthur's **circumstances**, not necessarily his memories, personality or abilities. Any access to
  memories must be developed explicitly in the story.

**Established plot points (in order)**
1. Neil grows $1,000 into $5,014,382.61.
2. His life is bound up with Maya; he protects her at the cost of his life.
3. Neil dies.
4. He transfers into Arthur Vance, 82, a billionaire about 100 m away.
5. He wakes in Arthur's failing body and realises he has survived death in a form he never chose.
6. He must handle Arthur's wealth, a family succession conflict, dangerous enemies and a complicated social position.
7. He may attend his own funeral while keeping his new identity secret.
8. The question the story asks: is a person still the same person when their body, identity and place in society are
   taken away?

## Part C. Consistency checks (rule 8: finance, legal, medical)

1. **A minor's account.** In most countries a 17-year-old trades through a custodial or guardian account (or an
   adult's account). That decides who controls the $5,014,382.61 when he dies, usually his parents or estate, which
   matters a lot for Neil-as-Arthur. It also limits what he could trade: custodial accounts usually can't short or use
   margin. The chapter below uses a plain sell for that reason.
2. **The growth.** $1,000 → $5,014,382.61 is about 5,000x. That's believable only with concentrated or leveraged bets
   over years, so fix the timeframe before any on-screen chart or date shows it.
3. **"Nearest eligible" and Maya.** If Maya was beside him, the canon itself implies she wasn't eligible. That's a clue
   to the mystery, and it must not be explained yet. The chapter shows it without explaining it.
4. **An 82-year-old brain.** Is Neil's thinking still 17 inside Arthur's brain, or slowed by it? This affects whether he
   can still trade well (rule 11). It's unknown, so decide before Chapter 2.
5. **Medical.** "Failing" stays vague in the chapter: a monitor, oxygen and weakness, no named disease. Name it later
   and keep it consistent.

## Chapter 1 (reconstructed, close third person, past tense†)

### I. The Market Was Lying

The market was lying, and Neil was the only one watching closely enough to see it.

Nineteen minutes before the closing bell†, the stock he'd been holding for three weeks† jumped eleven percent† on a
headline. Every chat he followed lit up with the same word. *Hold.* Hold for more. This was the move.

Neil didn't look at the headline. He looked at the order book.

Thick walls of buy orders sat a few cents under the price, the kind that tell a crowd there's a floor. Except they
kept vanishing the moment anyone tried to sell into them, and reappearing a cent lower. Big orders, never filled.
Someone wanted the crowd to believe in a floor that wasn't there.†

He'd learned to read lies like that with a thousand dollars. A thousand dollars meant every mistake hurt, so he'd
stopped making the loud ones. He'd learned to wait. To be wrong cheaply and right for a long time.

He sold everything into the spike.†

Then he did the hardest thing in trading: nothing.

Six minutes later† the walls disappeared all at once, and the price fell through the place they'd been, like a floor
that had only been painted on.

The account refreshed.

**$5,014,382.61**

He read it twice, the way you read a test score you're afraid to believe. He didn't post it. He didn't call anyone.
He opened one conversation.

*Maya.*

He typed *Five million*, deleted it, and typed *You awake?*†

*Always. Why are you?*

He grinned at the screen like an idiot. Of everyone he knew, she was the only one who had never once asked him how
much.†

### II. The Last Thing Neil Bought

They met the next afternoon outside the café by the station†, because Maya said she wanted to see his face when he
told her, not read it.

"So?"

"So," Neil said. "I was right."

"You're always right. That's what makes you unbearable." She pulled her sleeves over her hands against the cold.
"How right?"

He told her.

She didn't scream. She didn't ask him to buy her anything. She went very still, and then she laughed quietly, the way
people laugh at a miracle they don't want to scare off.

"Neil. You could do anything."

"I know." He looked at the menu instead of at her. "I'm starting with hot chocolate."

He paid with the debit card he'd had since he was fourteen†. Two hot chocolates. Nine dollars and twenty cents.†

Later, the police would find the receipt in his jacket pocket.† Of everything Neil had ever bought (shares, a
second monitor, a thousand small bets that became five million dollars), it was the last.

### III. The Last Position

They walked, because Maya walked when she was thinking†, and she was thinking about him, which he liked more than
he'd ever admit.

"Promise me something," she said at the crossing†. "Don't turn into one of those people who only talks about money."

"I talk about other things."

"Name one."

He opened his mouth. The light turned green. She stepped off the kerb, laughing at him. And Neil, who had trained
himself to notice the one number on a screen that didn't fit, noticed the one thing on the street that didn't fit.

A car† that wasn't slowing down.

Traders say you never choose your last position when you're calm. You choose it in the half-second when the price is
already moving.

He didn't think. He moved.

His hands found her back and shoved, hard. Maya went sprawling onto the far kerb, safe, her hot chocolate spinning
across the road.

Neil's last position was the middle of the crossing.

The impact didn't hurt. That was the strangest part. There was a sound, enormous and flat, and the sky turning over.
Then a quiet like the moment after the market closes, when the numbers stop moving and you finally find out what
you're worth.

He heard Maya say his name.

Then he didn't hear anything.

---

Neil opened his eyes, and his body was wrong.

It was heavy, all of it, as if he'd been poured in and left to set. A machine beside him beeped in a slow, tired
rhythm. Something plastic sat under his nose, hissing cool air.†

He lifted a hand to pull it away, and stopped.

The hand wasn't his. The skin was thin and spotted, loose over the knuckles, with a heavy gold ring† on the third
finger. It trembled, not from fear. It just trembled, the way it had probably trembled for years.

"Mr. Vance?" A nurse† was at the door, then at his side, quick and careful. "Mr. Vance, can you hear me? Don't try to
sit up."

Neil didn't know anyone called Vance.

On the wristband by his face, in small black print: VANCE, ARTHUR. AGE 82.†

He tried to say *My name is Neil.* What came out was a dry, cracked whisper, an old man's voice, and that frightened
him more than the hand.

In the dark window across the room† there was a reflection: a bed, a monitor, and an old man with a hollow face and
white hair, staring back with Neil's own terror.

Beyond the reflection, far below†, blue lights flashed on the street. An ambulance. A crowd. A small figure kneeling
at the crossing, refusing to let go of something on the ground.†

About a hundred metres away.

Neil had made five million dollars by understanding things faster than everyone else. He understood this, too,
before he wanted to.

He wasn't dead.

He was someone else.
