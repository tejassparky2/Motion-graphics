## 1. kitten_evil: "She Asked Her Dad 3 Questions About God 🐱"
Alt titles: "Could God Have Saved Her Kitten? 🐱" / "The Oldest Question About God 🐱"
Frame 0: the girl kneeling at a tiny grave with flowers in the garden, Dad behind her; big text **"COULD GOD HAVE SAVED HER?"**
Setting: back garden at sunset. Characters: a girl, her dad. Outcome: nobody wins (Dad has no answer).

| # | Who | Line | On screen |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Narrator | A girl buries her kitten in the garden. Then she asks her dad three questions. | Girl patting the small mound, a collar with a bell on it |
| 2 | Girl | Could God have saved her? | Close on her wet eyes |
| 3 | Dad | Yes. God can do anything. | Card **CAN = YES** |
| 4 | Girl | Did God want her to live? | |
| 5 | Dad | Of course. God is good. | Card **WANTS = YES** |
| 6 | Girl | Then why is she gone? | Both cards crack; Dad freezes (music drops out) |
| 7 | Narrator | It's the oldest puzzle about God. It's often credited to Epicurus, over two thousand years ago. | Old scroll, "EPICURUS?" tag |
| 8 | Narrator | If God can stop suffering but won't, is he good? If he wants to but can't, is he all-powerful? | Two doors, each with a red X |
| 9 | Narrator | One famous answer is free will. A world where we can truly choose good must let us choose harm too. | Card **FREE WILL** with a choice fork |
| 10 | Girl | But nobody chose to make my kitten sick. | Back to her; the fork card tears |
| 11 | Narrator | And that's the part people still argue about. Her dad just hugs her. [sigh] | Dad kneels and hugs her |
| 12 | Narrator | So, is suffering proof against a good God, or the price of a free world? | Buttons **PROOF / PRICE** |

Sources: the "Epicurean paradox", known from Lactantius (De Ira Dei, ch. 13) and David Hume, Dialogues Concerning
Natural Religion (1779), Part X ("Epicurus's old questions are yet unanswered"); the free-will defence: Alvin
Plantinga, God, Freedom, and Evil (1974); the objection from natural suffering (disease, which nobody chose) is the
standard reply to it.

## 3. prayer_plan: "She Prayed Before Her Exam. Her Brother Asked 1 Question 🙏"
Alt titles: "If God Already Knows, Why Pray? 🙏" / "Does Prayer Change God's Plan? 🙏"
Frame 0: the girl with folded hands, eyes shut, exam papers and pens beside her, her little brother peeking in;
big text **"WHY PRAY IF GOD ALREADY KNOWS?"**
Setting: home, exam morning. Characters: a teenage girl, her little brother. Outcome: the question gets a real
answer, and it backfires on her (she still has to study).

| # | Who | Line | On screen |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Narrator | Exam morning. A girl folds her hands and prays to pass. | Clock 7:45, admit card on the table |
| 2 | Brother | Wait. Doesn't God already know if you'll pass? | He leans in the doorway, cereal in hand |
| 3 | Girl | Of course. He knows everything. | |
| 4 | Brother | And his plan is perfect? | |
| 5 | Girl | Yes. | |
| 6 | Brother | Then why pray? You can't change a perfect plan. | She opens one eye (music drops out) |
| 7 | Narrator | If prayer changes God's plan, the plan wasn't perfect. If it can't, why pray? | Two doors, both with red X |
| 8 | Narrator | Thomas Aquinas answered this seven hundred years ago. | Card **THOMAS AQUINAS · 1270s** |
| 9 | Narrator | We don't pray to change God's plan. Our prayers are part of the plan. | Plan drawn as a path; the prayer is one stepping stone on it |
| 10 | Brother | So God's plan includes you studying too? | He slides her textbook across |
| 11 | Narrator | She groans. [laugh] So, does prayer change what happens, or does it change the one who prays? | Buttons **CHANGES WHAT HAPPENS / CHANGES YOU** |

Sources: Thomas Aquinas, Summa Theologiae II-II, q. 83, a. 2: "we pray not that we may change the Divine
disposition, but that we may impetrate that which God has disposed to be fulfilled by our prayers" (line 9 is a
plain-English paraphrase, and the description says so).

## 4. pascal_bet: "A Bet You Can't Lose? 1 Question Broke It 🎲"
Alt titles: "Believing Is a Free Bet... Or Is It? 🎲" / "Pascal's Wager in 40 Seconds 🎲"
Frame 0: a 2x2 bet table on the board (BELIEVE / DON'T × GOD / NO GOD) with **WIN EVERYTHING** circled; big text
**"A BET YOU CAN'T LOSE?"**
Setting: a different classroom with "Sir" (the male teacher), maths lesson. Characters: Sir, the last bencher.
Outcome: kid wins, with a funny callback.

| # | Who | Line | On screen |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Narrator | The maths teacher draws a bet on the board. Believe in God, or don't. | The 2x2 table appears cell by cell |
| 2 | Sir | Believe and you're right, you win heaven. Believe and you're wrong, you lose almost nothing. | Two cells fill: **WIN EVERYTHING / LOSE A LITTLE** |
| 3 | Sir | Don't believe and you're wrong, you lose everything. So believing is the smart bet. | Last cell **LOSE EVERYTHING** in red |
| 4 | Narrator | This is Pascal's Wager, from sixteen seventy. The whole class nods. | Card **PASCAL · 1670**; nodding heads |
| 5 | Narrator | Except the last bencher. | Camera snaps to the back row |
| 6 | Kid | Sir, which God? | Freeze; chalk drops (music drops out) |
| 7 | Narrator | There are thousands of religions. Many say believing in the wrong one won't save you. | The table explodes into hundreds of tiny boxes |
| 8 | Narrator | So the bet isn't two boxes. It's thousands. And you can only pick one. | Counter **2 → 4,000+** |
| 9 | Narrator | The French writer Denis Diderot made this same point in seventeen forty six. | Card **DIDEROT · 1746** |
| 10 | Sir | Then you'd better start studying all of them. [chuckle] | He drops a huge stack of books on the kid's desk |
| 11 | Narrator | So, is Pascal's bet still smart, or did one question break it? | Buttons **STILL SMART / BROKEN** |

Sources: Blaise Pascal, Pensées (published 1670), the wager; Denis Diderot, Pensées philosophiques (1746), §59
("an imam could reason just as well"), the "many gods" objection. "4,000+" = the commonly cited estimate of the
number of religions in the world, shown as an estimate.
