## 67. "God Already Knows. So Is It My Fault?" (~44 s)
**Title:** God Already Knew. So the Kid Says It's Not His Fault 😏
**Frame 0:** sir holding out his hand for homework; big text "GOD ALREADY KNEW"

| # | Who | Line | On screen |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Narrator | The last bencher hasn't done his homework. And he has the perfect excuse. | Empty notebook; sir's hand out |
| 2 | Kid | Sir, God knows everything, right? So yesterday, God already knew I wouldn't do it. | Kid leaning back, smug |
| 3 | Kid | If God knew, I couldn't do anything else. So it's not my fault. [chuckle] | Card: GOD KNEW → NO CHOICE? |
| 4 | Narrator | The class goes silent. Even sir stops writing. | Chalk stops mid-word |
| 5 | Narrator | Because it's a real puzzle. If someone knows your future for sure, can you still choose? | Big question card |
| 6 | Sir | Fifteen hundred years ago, a man named Boethius asked the same thing. In prison. | Old man in a cell, writing |
| 7 | Sir | His answer. God isn't stuck in time like us. He sees every moment at once, like you see a whole road from a hill. | A hill above a winding road, every car visible |
| 8 | Sir | Seeing you walk doesn't make you walk. Knowing your choice doesn't make it for you. | Card: KNOWING ≠ CAUSING |
| 9 | Kid | Then God also knows I'm copying from my friend tomorrow. | Kid winks |
| 10 | Sir | And I know about your detention. | Sir smiles; DETENTION stamp |
| 11 | Narrator | So, if your whole future is already known, are you still free? Yes, or no? | Buttons: FREE / NOT FREE |

**Source:** Boethius, *The Consolation of Philosophy*, Book V (written in prison, about 524 AD).

## 68. "Why Don't They Just Print More Money?" (~44 s)
**Title:** This Note Says 100 TRILLION. It Couldn't Buy Bread 💵
**Frame 0:** a girl holding a banknote with "100 TRILLION"; big text "WE'RE RICH?"

| # | Who | Line | On screen |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Narrator | A girl finds an old banknote in her grandpa's drawer. It says, one hundred trillion dollars. | The note, zeros counting up |
| 2 | Girl | Grandpa! We're rich! | Girl jumping |
| 3 | Grandpa | That note couldn't buy a bus ticket. [chuckle] | Grandpa laughing |
| 4 | Narrator | It's real money. Zimbabwe printed it in two thousand nine. | Map pin: ZIMBABWE 2009 |
| 5 | Girl | But why not just print more money, so everyone's rich? | Girl at a printing press |
| 6 | Narrator | Here's the catch. Printing money doesn't make more bread. | One loaf, piles of cash |
| 7 | Narrator | Same bread, but everyone has twice the money? The baker just doubles the price. | Price tag 10 → 20 |
| 8 | Narrator | Zimbabwe printed so much that prices doubled about every day. | Price tag spinning up |
| 9 | Grandpa | People carried bags of cash, to buy one loaf. | Man with a sack of notes |
| 10 | Narrator | But in twenty twenty, many countries printed money to get through the pandemic. Some say it saved jobs. Others say it caused the price rises after. | Two cards: SAVED JOBS / PRICES UP |
| 11 | Narrator | So should a country print money in a crisis? Yes, or no? | Buttons: YES / NO |

**Sources:** Z$100 trillion note issued January 2009; at the peak (Nov 2008) prices doubled roughly every 24.7 hours
(Steve Hanke, Cato Institute). 2020 money creation: central banks' pandemic programmes; the debate over its link to
2021-23 inflation is real and unsettled, so both sides are given.

## 69. "Pull the Lever. But Don't Push?" (~44 s)
**Title:** Everyone Pulled the Lever. Nobody Would Push 🚃
**Frame 0:** a runaway train drawn on the board heading for five people; big text "5 PEOPLE. 1 LEVER."

| # | Who | Line | On screen |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Narrator | The teacher draws a runaway train on the board. Five people stand on the track ahead. | Chalk train racing |
| 2 | Teacher | You can pull a lever. The train turns, and hits one person instead. Do you pull it? | Lever, side track with one person |
| 3 | Narrator | Almost every hand goes up. One life, to save five. Easy. | Hands up |
| 4 | Teacher | New version. No lever. You're on a bridge, next to a big man. Push him, and the train stops. Five saved. | Bridge scene |
| 5 | Narrator | Every hand goes down. [gasp] | Hands drop |
| 6 | Narrator | But it's the same math. One dies. Five live. | Card: 1 vs 5 = 1 vs 5 |
| 7 | Narrator | Philosophers have argued about this since nineteen sixty seven. | Card: THE TROLLEY PROBLEM |
| 8 | Narrator | Some say only the result matters. Others say using a person as a tool is wrong, whatever the math. | Two cards: RESULT / RIGHTS |
| 9 | Narrator | So, would you push him? And if not, why did you pull the lever? | Buttons: PUSH / DON'T PUSH |

**Sources:** Philippa Foot (1967) introduced the trolley case; Judith Jarvis Thomson (1976, 1985) added the footbridge.

## 70. "She Tried to Stop Her Parents From Meeting" (~42 s)
**Title:** She Went Back in Time to Stop Her Parents Meeting ⏳
**Frame 0:** a girl at a glowing time machine in a garage; big text "STOP MY PARENTS MEETING?"

| # | Who | Line | On screen |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Narrator | A girl finishes her time machine. And her first plan is terrible. | Machine sparks on |
| 2 | Girl | I'll go back, and stop my parents from meeting. Just to see what happens. [chuckle] | Girl grinning, dial set to 2008 |
| 3 | Narrator | Think about it. If they never meet, she's never born. | Her photo fading |
| 4 | Narrator | If she's never born, she never builds the machine. So nobody stops them. | Machine vanishing |
| 5 | Narrator | So they meet. She's born. She goes back. And stops them again. | A loop arrow spinning |
| 6 | Narrator | It's called the grandfather paradox. And physicists take it seriously. | Card |
| 7 | Narrator | One idea. The past fixes itself. Whatever she tries, her parents meet anyway. | She trips; parents bump into each other |
| 8 | Narrator | Another idea. She lands in a different branch of the universe, where she's never born. Her own world is untouched. | The timeline splits in two |
| 9 | Narrator | Either way, she can't erase herself. [laugh] | Girl shrugging |
| 10 | Narrator | So which is it? A past you can't change, or a second world? | Buttons: FIXED PAST / NEW WORLD |

**Sources:** Novikov self-consistency principle (1980s); David Deutsch's many-worlds answer to time-travel paradoxes
(1991). Both are ideas, not proven, and the script says "one idea / another idea".

## 71. "Is It Wrong Because God Says So?" (~44 s)
**Title:** 1 Question. The Teacher Couldn't Pick an Answer 🤔
**Frame 0:** moral-science class, "LYING IS WRONG" on the board; big text "BECAUSE GOD SAYS SO?"

| # | Who | Line | On screen |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Narrator | Moral science class. The teacher says lying is wrong, because God says so. Then the last bencher raises his hand. | Board: LYING IS WRONG |
| 2 | Kid | Ma'am, is lying wrong because God says so? Or does God say so, because it's wrong? | Big question text |
| 3 | Narrator | The teacher opens her mouth. And closes it. | Teacher freezes |
| 4 | Narrator | Because both answers have a problem. | Two doors |
| 5 | Narrator | If it's wrong only because God says so, then if God said lie, lying would be good. | Card with ✗ |
| 6 | Narrator | If God says so because it's already wrong, then right and wrong exist without God. | Card with ✗ |
| 7 | Narrator | This question is two thousand four hundred years old. Plato wrote it down. | Old scroll |
| 8 | Teacher | Some thinkers answer. Goodness isn't a rule God picks. It's what God is. | Teacher smiles, chalk up |
| 9 | Kid | So God can't command a lie, because God is truth? | Kid thinking |
| 10 | Teacher | Exactly. Now do your homework. Truthfully. [chuckle] | Homework stamp |
| 11 | Narrator | So, does right and wrong need God? Yes, or no? | Buttons: YES / NO |

**Sources:** Plato, *Euthyphro* (about 399 BC); the "goodness is God's nature" reply is classical theism (Aquinas,
*Summa Theologiae* I, q.6).
