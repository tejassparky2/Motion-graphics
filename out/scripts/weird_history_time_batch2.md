# Batch 2 for approval: weird history, time paradoxes and the multiverse

Every script follows the same format as batch 1:
- about 110–140 spoken words (35–50 s)
- a hook in the first second
- numbers written on screen as they're spoken, marked **[on screen]**
- a last line that loops back into the first

Everything is written in our own words. Each fact note lists the source (Wikipedia, checked for this batch) and what
the script leaves out because it's disputed.

# Part A: weird history

## A1. The Soldier Who Fought for 29 Years After the War Ended

**Hook:** World War Two ended in 1945. This soldier kept fighting until 1974.

> World War Two ended in nineteen forty-five. This soldier kept fighting until **nineteen seventy-four**. **[on screen: 1945 → 1974]**
> His name was Hiroo Onoda. In nineteen forty-four, Japan sent him to a jungle island in the Philippines, with one order: never surrender.
> Then the war ended, and nobody told him. Or rather, they did: leaflets, newspapers, letters from his family. He was sure it was all enemy tricks.
> So he stayed in the jungle for **twenty-nine years**. **[on screen: 29 YEARS]**
> In nineteen seventy-four, a young Japanese traveler went looking for him. He said he was searching for three things: Lieutenant Onoda, a panda, and the abominable snowman. In that order.
> He found Onoda first. But Onoda still refused to quit, until his old commander flew in and gave him the order, in person.
> World War Two ended in nineteen forty-five...

**Visual idea:** a calendar flipping 1945 → 1974 while the soldier's beard grows. Leaflets drop and he tosses them away.
The traveler has a checklist that reads "Onoda ✓ / panda / yeti".

**Fact notes:** sent to Lubang on 26 Dec 1944. He surrendered on 10 Mar 1974 after his former commander, Maj. Yoshimi
Taniguchi, relieved him of duty. Norio Suzuki found him on 20 Feb 1974, and the "Onoda, a panda, and the abominable
snowman, in that order" line is from Wikipedia. The script leaves out the civilians killed during his holdout. That's
a serious part of the story, and a 40-second Short can't handle it fairly, so it goes in the pinned comment or
description as context.

## A2. The Shortest War in History

**Hook:** The shortest war in history lasted about as long as a TV episode.

> The shortest war in history lasted about as long as a TV episode.
> August, **eighteen ninety-six**. The Sultan of Zanzibar dies, and his nephew grabs the palace, without Britain's approval. **[on screen: 1896]**
> Britain says: leave by nine a.m. **[on screen: 9:00]**
> He doesn't. At nine o'clock, British warships open fire.
> By about nine forty, the palace is wrecked, the flag is down, and the new sultan has escaped out the back.
> The war is over in around **thirty-eight minutes**. **[on screen: 38 MIN]**
> Britain puts its own choice on the throne.
> So next time a show feels long, remember: an entire war was shorter than your average TV episode.

**Visual idea:** a big stopwatch in the corner the whole time, a palace with a flagpole, and cartoon ships. The flag drops at
"38".

**Fact notes:** 27 August 1896, UK vs the Sultanate of Zanzibar. The article gives the length as 38–45 minutes and says
"38" is the figure most often cited, so the script says "around thirty-eight". Khalid bin Barghash escaped to German East
Africa. About 500 Zanzibaris were killed or wounded, so the tone stays light on the timing and never jokes about the
casualties.

## A3. The Chicken That Lived 18 Months Without a Head

**Hook:** In 1945, a farmer chopped off a chicken's head... and it just kept walking.

> In nineteen forty-five, a Colorado farmer chopped off a chicken's head for dinner. **[on screen: 1945]**
> The chicken got up... and kept walking.
> The next morning, it was still alive. So was the next week. And the next month.
> The farmer named him **Mike**. He fed him milk and water with an eyedropper, straight down his neck.
> How? The axe missed the main vein, a clot stopped the bleeding, and most of the brain stem, the part that runs breathing and heartbeat, was still there.
> Mike lived for **eighteen months**. **[on screen: 18 MONTHS]** He even went on tour, and people paid to see him.
> Today, his town throws a festival for him every year. There's even a race called: Run Like a Headless Chicken.

**Visual idea:** a comedy-horror farm with no gore (the head is just "gone", shown with a cartoon scribble). Calendar pages
flip, the eyedropper feeds him, and then a festival banner.

**Fact notes:** born April 1945 in Fruita, Colorado; owner Lloyd Olsen; fed milk and water by eyedropper; the axe missed the
jugular, a clot prevented him bleeding to death, and most of the brain stem was left. He lived 18 months (died 17 Mar 1947).
"Mike the Headless Chicken Day" has been held since 1999, and its events include the "5K Run Like a Headless Chicken Race".

## A4. The Summer a Whole Town Couldn't Stop Dancing

**Hook:** In 1518, a woman started dancing in the street... and couldn't stop.

> In **fifteen eighteen**, a woman in Strasbourg walked into the street, and started dancing. **[on screen: 1518]**
> No music. No reason. She just... couldn't stop.
> Days later, others joined her. Then more. By the end, somewhere between **fifty and four hundred** people were dancing. **[on screen: 50–400]**
> So what did the city do? It decided the cure was... more dancing.
> They hired musicians. They opened up halls. They even paid strong men to hold the dancers up.
> It did not work.
> The outbreak went on for about **two months** before it finally faded out. **[on screen: 2 MONTHS]**
> Nobody knows for sure what caused it. And it wasn't the only one. Europe had outbreaks like this for centuries.

**Visual idea:** a medieval street with the crowd growing as a counter ticks up. Musicians with lutes, and the camera bops to
the beat.

**Fact notes:** started 14 July 1518 with Frau Troffea. There were 50–400 participants. The outbreak ran from July into
September. The council refurbished guild halls, hired musicians, and paid strong people to support the dancers, and later
banned public music and dancing. **Deaths are left out on purpose:** Wikipedia says the city's own records of the time
don't mention any, and the "15 a day" claim comes from later accounts. The script says "about two months" to stay safe
inside the stated July-to-September window.

## A5. The Deadly Wave of... Molasses

**Hook:** In 1919, a wave of syrup swept through a city at 35 miles an hour.

> In **nineteen nineteen**, a giant tank of molasses burst in Boston. **[on screen: 1919]**
> **Two point three million gallons** of syrup came out, all at once. **[on screen: 2.3 MILLION GALLONS]**
> It became a wave **twenty-five feet** tall, **[on screen: 25 FT]** moving at **thirty-five miles an hour**. **[on screen: 35 MPH]**
> It knocked buildings off their foundations, and bent the steel of an elevated railway.
> Then it got worse. It was January, and cold, and the molasses turned thick, like glue. People who got caught couldn't get out.
> **Twenty-one** people died. **[on screen: 21]** About a hundred and fifty were hurt.
> The cleanup took weeks. And locals say that on hot summer days, the neighborhood smelled sweet for decades.

**Visual idea:** a brown tidal wave with a cartoon speedometer at 35 mph and a height ruler at 25 ft. The goo thickens as
frost creeps in.

**Fact notes:** 15 Jan 1919 in Boston's North End. It released 2.3 million US gallons, the wave was 25 ft at its peak and
moved at 35 mph, 21 people died and 150 were injured, and the cold made the molasses viscous and trapped people. Because
people died, the tone stays serious rather than jokey. The "smelled sweet for decades" line is a local claim, so the script
presents it as "locals say".

## A6. The Year Without a Summer, and How It Created Frankenstein

**Hook:** In 1816, it snowed in June. And that snow gave us Frankenstein.

> In **eighteen sixteen**, it snowed in New York... in **June**. **[on screen: JUNE 1816]**
> Frost killed crops across New England. Europe had one of its coldest, wettest summers ever.
> The reason? A year earlier, a volcano in Indonesia, **Mount Tambora**, had one of the biggest eruptions in recorded history. Its ash dimmed the sun around the world.
> Meanwhile, at a lake in Switzerland, a group of writers were stuck inside because of the endless rain.
> One of them, Lord Byron, said: let's each write a ghost story.
> An eighteen-year-old named Mary Shelley went to bed, and imagined a scientist kneeling over the creature he'd built.
> That story became **Frankenstein**. **[on screen: FRANKENSTEIN]**
> So one volcano's eruption ended up creating the most famous monster ever written.

**Visual idea:** a snowman in a summer garden and a volcano across the globe puffing an ash cloud that spreads, then a
candlelit villa with rain on the window and a lightning flash onto a doodle of the creature.

**Fact notes:** Tambora erupted in April 1815 (VEI 7). On 6 June 1816, snow fell in Albany, NY and Dennysville, Maine, and
frost killed crops in New England. The writers were at Villa Diodati on Lake Geneva, Byron proposed the ghost-story contest,
and Shelley had the waking image of Victor kneeling over his creation. Mary Shelley was born in August 1797, so she was 18
that summer.

# Part B: time paradoxes and the multiverse

## B1. The Bootstrap Paradox: "Who Wrote This Song?"

**Hook:** This song has no songwriter. Nobody ever wrote it.

> You love an old song. So you build a time machine, go back **fifty years**, **[on screen: 50 YEARS AGO]** and play it for the band, before they ever wrote it.
> They love it. They record it. It becomes a hit. Fifty years later, you hear it, and fall in love with it.
> So... who wrote the song?
> The band didn't. They copied you. You didn't. You copied them.
> The song just... exists. It loops through time, and nobody ever created it. **[on screen: ∞ LOOP]**
> Physicists call this a causal loop. And some say that if time travel into the past is possible, loops like this might be the only kind of history it allows.
> So next time you hear a song you love, ask yourself: who wrote it?

**Visual idea:** a vinyl record spinning as a loop arrow circles through the "now" and "then" panels. The songwriter credit
flickers between names and then "???".

**Fact notes:** this is the standard "bootstrap/ontological paradox" thought experiment, told with an original band instead
of the usual Beethoven example (to avoid copying a well-known TV episode). The "only loops that stay consistent" point is
the Novikov self-consistency idea, framed as "some say".

## B2. Stephen Hawking Threw a Party for Time Travelers

**Hook:** Stephen Hawking threw a party. Nobody came. That was the point.

> In **two thousand nine**, Stephen Hawking threw a party. Balloons, snacks, champagne. **[on screen: JUNE 28, 2009]**
> Then he waited... and nobody came.
> That was the plan. Because he only sent out the invitations **after** the party was over. **[on screen: INVITES SENT AFTER]**
> The only people who could show up were time travelers from the future.
> Nobody did.
> Hawking said it was a joke experiment. But he really did think the universe might stop time travel into the past, to protect history from paradoxes.
> So if you're watching this from the future... the party was June twenty-eighth, two thousand nine. You're late.

**Visual idea:** a festive room and a banner reading "Welcome, Time Travellers". A clock spins while the door stays shut
and a lone balloon deflates. At the end, a portal flickers... and closes.

**Fact notes:** 28 June 2009. Wikipedia says he presented the party as a "tongue-in-cheek test" of his chronology
protection conjecture, publicised it only after it was over, and "as expected, nobody showed up". "Champagne" appears in
Hawking's own account (in Wikipedia's wording it's "food and drinks"), so if we want to be strict, say "snacks and drinks".

## B3. The Many-Worlds Theory: "There's Another You"

**Hook:** Some physicists think there's a version of you that never clicked on this video.

> Some physicists think there's a version of you that didn't click on this video.
> Here's the idea. In quantum physics, tiny particles don't pick one outcome. Until you measure them, they're in many possibilities at once.
> In **nineteen fifty-seven**, a physicist named Hugh Everett asked: what if they never have to choose? **[on screen: 1957]**
> What if every possible outcome happens, each in its own branch of reality?
> That's the **many-worlds** theory. **[on screen: MANY WORLDS]** It says the universe is constantly splitting, into more versions than we could ever count.
> Nobody can visit the other branches, so nobody can prove it. But it's taken seriously, and it's still debated today.
> So somewhere out there, there's a version of you who never clicked on this video.

**Visual idea:** our mascot at a fork. Every "click" splits the screen into two, then four, then eight panels, each with a
slightly different mascot (one with a mustache, one as a cat).

**Fact notes:** Hugh Everett's 1957 Princeton dissertation; the term "many-worlds" was popularised by Bryce DeWitt. Wikipedia
calls it a mainstream but contested interpretation that current experiments can't test. The script avoids the common
mistake of saying "every choice you make splits the universe": it's quantum outcomes that branch.

## B4. You're Already a Time Traveler

**Hook:** Time travel is real, and your phone uses it every day.

> Time travel is real, and your phone has to deal with it every single day.
> Einstein showed that time runs at different speeds. The faster you move, the slower your clock ticks. And the weaker the gravity, the faster it ticks.
> GPS satellites fly fast, and far above Earth's gravity. Put together, their clocks run fast, by about **thirty-eight microseconds a day**. **[on screen: +38 μs / DAY]**
> Tiny, right? But if nobody corrected it, your map would drift by about **eleven kilometers every day**. **[on screen: ~11 KM / DAY]**
> It even happened to twins. Astronaut Scott Kelly spent almost a year in space, and came back a few milliseconds younger than his identical twin brother. **[on screen: a few ms YOUNGER]**
> So yes, time travel is real. It's just really, really slow.

**Visual idea:** a satellite with a ticking clock that runs ahead of the Earth clock. A map pin slides across town as the
error grows. Two identical twins, one in a spacesuit.

**Fact notes:** GPS clocks lose 7.2 μs a day from their speed and gain 45.8 μs a day from weaker gravity, for a net gain of
about 38.6 μs a day. Left uncorrected, that would add up to an error of about 11.4 km a day (Wikipedia, "Error analysis for
GPS"). Scott Kelly: Wikipedia's twin-paradox page says "about 8.6 milliseconds", while other sources give about 5 ms, so
the script says "a few milliseconds" to stay safe.

## B5. The Mandela Effect: "Did We Switch Universes?"

**Hook:** Thousands of people remember something that never happened. Same memory, same mistake.

> Quick: does the Monopoly man wear a monocle?
> Most people say yes. He doesn't. He never has. **[on screen: NO MONOCLE]**
> The kids' books you remember as the Berenstein Bears? They were always Berenstain. **[on screen: BERENSTAIN]**
> And Darth Vader never says "Luke, I am your father". He says: "No. I am your father."
> This is called the Mandela Effect, named after thousands of people who remembered Nelson Mandela dying in prison in the eighties. He was released, and became president.
> Some people online say it's proof we slipped into a parallel universe.
> Psychologists have a less exciting answer: our brains fill in memories with what *seems* right, and we all make the same guesses.
> So... does the Monopoly man wear a monocle?

**Visual idea:** a "memory" card flips between two versions with a glitch effect, then a brain doodle "auto-filling" a
monocle onto the Monopoly-style man. We draw an original look-alike top-hat character, never the trademarked mascot.

**Fact notes:** the term was popularised by Fiona Broome, whose memory was of Mandela dying in prison in the 1980s. In fact
he was released in 1990 and died in 2013. The article lists the Berenstain spelling, the monocle, and "No, I am your
father". It describes the parallel-realities idea as being "in opposition to" psychological explanations, and the
scientific explanation is false memory. **Trademark caution:** don't draw the real Monopoly mascot or Darth Vader. Use
original silhouettes and text only.

## B6. Hilbert's Infinite Hotel: "Full, But There's Still Room"

**Hook:** This hotel is completely full... and it can still fit infinitely more guests.

> Imagine a hotel with **infinite rooms**, **[on screen: ∞ ROOMS]** and every single one is full.
> A new guest shows up. Sorry, we're full? Nope.
> The manager asks everyone to move one room up. Room one goes to two, two goes to three, forever. Room one is now empty. **[on screen: n → n+1]**
> Then a bus shows up, with **infinite** new guests.
> Easy. Everyone moves to double their room number. One goes to two, two to four, three to six. **[on screen: n → 2n]** Now every odd-numbered room is free, and there are infinitely many of those.
> A mathematician named David Hilbert came up with this about **a hundred years ago**, to show that infinity doesn't behave like a normal number.
> So... how many rooms are left?

**Visual idea:** a hotel corridor that scrolls forever, with guests hopping one door to the right in a wave, then the doubling
jump. Odd doors light up with vacancy signs.

**Fact notes:** from Hilbert's 1924–25 lecture "Über das Unendliche", popularised by George Gamow in 1947. The n → n+1 and
n → 2n moves are from the article. "About a hundred years ago" matches 1924.
