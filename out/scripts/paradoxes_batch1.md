# Paradoxes & strange theories: scripts, batch 1 (for approval)

Each script is ready for the narration pipeline:
- about 110–130 spoken words (35–45 s)
- a hook in the first second
- numbers written on screen as they're spoken (marked **[on screen]**)
- a last line that loops back into the first

Everything is written in our own words. The fact notes list what each script rests on, and what it avoids because it's
commonly gotten wrong.

---

## 1. The Monty Hall Problem: "Always Switch"

**Hook:** Three doors. One car. Two goats. Pick one.

> Three doors. Behind one, a car. Behind the other two, goats. You pick door number one.
> Now the host, who knows where the car is, opens door three. Goat.
> He asks: do you want to switch to door two?
> Most people say it doesn't matter. Fifty-fifty, right?
> Wrong. If you switch, you win **two out of three** times. **[on screen: STAY = 1/3, SWITCH = 2/3]**
> Here's why. When you first picked, you had a **one in three** chance. That doesn't change.
> So the other **two thirds** don't disappear. They all pile onto the one door the host left closed.
> When a columnist explained this in 1990, thousands of readers wrote in to say she was wrong. Many of them had PhDs.
> She wasn't. So, next time you get a second chance... switch.

**Visual idea:** a game-show stage, a host character, three big doors, goats, and a car that slides out. The "two thirds"
is shown as two glowing doors merging into one.

**Fact notes:** switching wins with probability 2/3 under the standard rules (the host always opens a goat door and
always offers the switch). Marilyn vos Savant's *Parade* column in 1990 drew a flood of letters disagreeing with her, many
from readers with PhDs. The script says "thousands" rather than an exact count.

## 2. The Birthday Paradox: "23 People"

**Hook:** How many people do you need in a room for two of them to share a birthday?

> How many people do you need in a room, for two of them to probably share a birthday?
> Most people guess around a hundred and eighty. Half the year.
> The real answer? **Twenty-three.** **[on screen: 23 → 50%]**
> With just twenty-three people, the chance is already over fifty percent.
> With **seventy** people, it's **ninety-nine point nine** percent. **[on screen: 70 → 99.9%]**
> The trick is you're not comparing everyone to *you*. You're comparing every pair.
> Twenty-three people make **two hundred and fifty-three** pairs. **[on screen: 253 pairs]**
> That's a lot of chances for a match.
> So look around your classroom, or your office... two of you might be celebrating the same day. How many people did you guess?

**Visual idea:** a room fills with cartoon people, and string lines connect every pair until it's a messy web. A
counter shows the pair count, and a meter fills to 50%.

**Fact notes:** P(shared birthday) is 50.7% at 23 people and 99.9% at 70 (365 days, leap years ignored).
23 × 22 / 2 = 253 pairs.

## 3. Zeno's Paradox: "You Can Never Win This Race"

**Hook:** The fastest runner in the world can never catch a tortoise. At least, that's what one Greek philosopher proved.

> The fastest runner in the world can never catch a tortoise. Here's the proof.
> The tortoise gets a head start. By the time the runner reaches where the tortoise was, it has crawled a bit further.
> He reaches that spot. It's moved again. A tiny bit. Then a tinier bit.
> Forever. **[on screen: ∞ steps]**
> A philosopher named Zeno came up with this about **twenty-five hundred years ago**, and it bothered people for centuries.
> The answer came from math: an infinite number of steps can add up to a finite distance.
> One half, plus a quarter, plus an eighth... adds up to exactly **one**. **[on screen: 1/2 + 1/4 + 1/8 + ... = 1]**
> So the runner wins. Infinity just finishes surprisingly fast.

**Visual idea:** a Greek-style runner and a smug tortoise. Each step, the camera zooms in further and further, then a
bar splits into halves, quarters and eighths that fill up to exactly 1.

**Fact notes:** Zeno of Elea, 5th century BC, so about 2,500 years ago. The standard resolution is a convergent infinite
series.

## 4. The Ship of Theseus: "Are You Still You?"

**Hook:** If you replace every part of a ship... is it still the same ship?

> A ship sails home, and one old plank gets swapped for a new one. Still the same ship, right?
> Next year, another plank. Then another. Until one day, **every single piece** has been replaced.
> Is it still the same ship?
> Now it gets weird. Someone collects all the old planks, and builds a second ship out of them.
> So which one is the real ship?
> Ancient Greek writers argued about this. And here's the twist: it's about you.
> Your body replaces **billions of cells every single day**. **[on screen: billions / day]**
> Most of you has been rebuilt, bit by bit. But some cells, like many in your brain, stay with you for life.
> So... are you still the same person who started watching this video?

**Visual idea:** a hand-drawn ship where planks glow and swap one by one, and the old planks pile up into a ghost-ship
twin. Then a cut to our mascot, whose body pixels swap out.

**Fact notes:** the thought experiment comes from Plutarch. The script avoids the popular myth that "every cell is
replaced every 7 years"; it isn't true, since many cortical neurons last a lifetime. "Billions of cells a day" is
conservative: a 2021 estimate puts it at roughly 330 billion.

## 5. The Fermi Paradox: "Where Is Everybody?"

**Hook:** There could be billions of planets like Earth. So why haven't we heard from anyone?

> Our galaxy has **hundreds of billions** of stars. **[on screen: 100–400 billion stars]**
> Many of them have planets. Some of those could be a lot like Earth.
> So in 1950, over lunch, the physicist Enrico Fermi asked a simple question: where is everybody?
> If aliens are out there, why haven't we seen a single sign?
> Here are the strangest answers.
> One: they're out there, but they're watching us like animals in a zoo.
> Two: every civilization hits a wall, a "Great Filter", that wipes it out before it reaches the stars.
> And three: we really are alone.
> Which answer scares you the most?

**Visual idea:** a zoom out from Earth to a galaxy of dots, then aliens behind a zoo fence with binoculars, then a big
brick wall labelled "Great Filter", and finally a lone Earth.

**Fact notes:** the Milky Way has an estimated 100–400 billion stars. Fermi's "Where is everybody?" came at a 1950 lunch
at Los Alamos. The zoo hypothesis and the Great Filter (Robin Hanson) are named, well-known proposals.

## 6. The Simulation Argument: "Are We in a Video Game?"

**Hook:** One philosopher says there's a real chance you're living inside a computer.

> In two thousand three, philosopher Nick Bostrom made an argument that still bothers scientists.
> Imagine a future civilization with computers powerful enough to simulate whole worlds, with people who think they're real.
> Then one of three things has to be true.
> One: civilizations almost always die out before they get that powerful.
> Two: they get that powerful, but almost nobody bothers to run simulations.
> Or three: we're almost certainly living inside one right now.
> Because if they run even a few, the fake worlds would massively outnumber the real one.
> He doesn't say which is true. He says at least one of them has to be.
> So... which one would you bet on?

**Visual idea:** our world cracks into pixels, then three big labelled doors for the three options. For option three,
worlds nest inside computers inside worlds.

**Fact notes:** Nick Bostrom, "Are You Living in a Computer Simulation?" (2003). The script states his trilemma
accurately: he argues at least one of the three is true, not that we are definitely in a simulation.

## 7. The Grandfather Paradox: "Don't Touch the Past"

**Hook:** What happens if you travel back in time... and stop your own grandparents from meeting?

> You build a time machine, go back sixty years, and accidentally stop your grandparents from ever meeting.
> So your parent is never born. So you are never born.
> But if you were never born... who went back in time?
> That's the grandfather paradox, and physicists have some wild answers.
> Answer one: you can't. Some physicists argue the universe only allows histories that stay consistent, so something
> would stop you, every single time. **[on screen: "can't happen"]**
> Answer two: you can, but it happens in a *different* timeline, and your original timeline stays the same.
> **[on screen: "new timeline"]**
> So if you ever get a time machine... go forward instead.

**Visual idea:** a DIY time machine (a washing machine with a clock taped on) and cartoon grandparents at a 1960s
diner. The time traveller flickers in and out, then the timeline splits into two branches.

**Fact notes:** the self-consistency idea is the Novikov self-consistency principle. The branching-timeline idea draws on
many-worlds-style interpretations. Both are presented as proposals, not settled physics.
