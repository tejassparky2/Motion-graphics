# Pinocchio-style paradoxes: 4 scripts for approval

Each one follows the format that worked for Pinocchio: one simple setup, one line that breaks it, then both answers
tested one step at a time, so anyone can follow. Each ends on a question people will argue about. Polished
animation style (like the honeybee, Last Bencher 4 and Achilles videos). Narrator am_fenrir.
Nothing is rendered until you approve or edit a script.

---

## 1. Sun Oct 18, 9:30 PM: "He Sued His Own Student. And Nobody Could Win." ⚖️ (Paradox of the Court)

| # | Line | On screen |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | A teacher sued his own student. And the judges couldn't decide who wins. | Courtroom; teacher and student glare; "?" over the judge |
| 2 | Ancient Greece. Protagoras teaches people how to win in court. | Greek columns; Protagoras with a scroll |
| 3 | His student makes a deal. He'll pay only after he wins his first case. | Contract card: "PAY AFTER FIRST WIN" |
| 4 | Then the student never takes a case. So he never pays. | Student on a couch; calendar pages flying; empty wallet |
| 5 | So Protagoras sues him. Now listen to both sides. | Judge's gavel; split screen |
| 6 | Protagoras says: if I win, the court makes you pay. | Teacher side, card 1 ✔ PAY |
| 7 | If I lose, you've won your first case. So you pay anyway. | Card 2 ✔ PAY |
| 8 | The student says: if I win, the court says I don't pay. | Student side, card 1 ✘ NO PAY |
| 9 | If I lose, I still haven't won a case. So I don't pay. | Card 2 ✘ NO PAY |
| 10 | Both of them are right. And both of them are wrong. | The two sets of cards crash together; the judge sweats |
| 11 | The old story says the judges gave up. They postponed the case to a day far, far away. | Calendar flips into the distance; judges walk off |
| 12 | So who should win? The teacher, or the student? | Two buttons: TEACHER / STUDENT |

*Source:* Aulus Gellius, *Attic Nights*, Book 5, chapter 10 (2nd century AD), where the jurors find both pleas
"uncertain and insoluble" and put the case off to a distant day. [Wikipedia: Paradox of the Court](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paradox_of_the_Court).
The student's name (Euathlus) is left out of the narration on purpose: it's hard to say and easy to mishear.

## 2. Mon Oct 19, 4:30 AM: "A Crocodile Made a Deal. One Answer Breaks It." 🐊 (The Crocodile Dilemma)

| # | Line | On screen |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | A crocodile steals a child. Then it offers the mother a deal. | Riverbank; crocodile with a baby in a basket; mother gasps |
| 2 | Guess what I'm going to do. Guess right, and you get your child back. | Crocodile, smug, speech bubble "GUESS RIGHT = CHILD BACK" |
| 3 | The mother thinks. Then she says: you will not give my child back. | Mother points; bold card: "YOU WON'T GIVE IT BACK" |
| 4 | Now the crocodile is stuck. | Crocodile freezes; record-scratch |
| 5 | If it gives the child back, her guess was wrong. So it shouldn't give it back. | Path A: child handed back, then a red ✘ "GUESS WRONG" |
| 6 | If it keeps the child, her guess was right. So it has to give it back. | Path B: child kept, then a green ✔ "GUESS RIGHT", "MUST RETURN" |
| 7 | Whatever it does, it breaks its own promise. | The two paths loop into a knot; crocodile's head spins |
| 8 | Teachers in ancient Rome already knew this puzzle, almost two thousand years ago. | Roman teacher at a chalkboard with a crocodile doodle |
| 9 | So what should the crocodile do? | Two buttons: GIVE BACK / KEEP |

*Source:* the Crocodile dilemma. The term is mentioned by the Roman teacher Quintilian (*Institutio Oratoria* I.10.5, c. 95 AD).
[Wikipedia: Crocodile dilemma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crocodile_dilemma).

## 3. Mon Oct 19, 9:30 PM: "The Barber Who Can't Exist" 💈 (Barber paradox)

| # | Line | On screen |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | This barber cannot exist. Here's why. | Barber shop sign; the barber fades in and out like a glitch |
| 2 | In one village, the barber has one rule. | Village; the barber with scissors |
| 3 | He shaves everyone who doesn't shave themselves. And only them. | Rule card; a queue of bearded villagers; one man shaving himself is sent away |
| 4 | Simple. So who shaves the barber? | The barber looks in a mirror at his own stubble; "?" |
| 5 | If he shaves himself, he's a man who shaves himself. And the barber never shaves those men. So he can't. | Path A: razor to his face, then a red ✘ |
| 6 | If he doesn't shave himself, he's a man who doesn't shave himself. And the barber must shave all of those. So he has to. | Path B: beard grows, then his own rule card points at him: "MUST SHAVE" |
| 7 | Either way, he breaks his own rule. | Loop knot; the barber's beard grows and shrinks, flickering |
| 8 | The famous thinker Bertrand Russell wrote about this puzzle in nineteen eighteen. | Russell portrait card, 1918 |
| 9 | And the answer most people accept? A barber like that simply can't exist. | The barber glitches out of existence; an empty chair spins |
| 10 | Do you agree? Or can you save the barber? | Buttons: AGREE / I CAN SAVE HIM |

*Source:* Bertrand Russell, *The Philosophy of Logical Atomism* (The Monist, 1918–19). He mentions the barber as a
puzzle someone suggested to him. [Wikipedia: Barber paradox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barber_paradox).
*Mishearing risk:* none expected.

## 4. Tue Oct 20, 4:30 AM: "One Salesman Invented the Chinese Word for Contradiction" 🛡️ (Spear and Shield)

| # | Line | On screen |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | One salesman's lie became a word that over a billion people still use. | Busy ancient Chinese market; a salesman on a crate |
| 2 | Ancient China. A man is selling a shield. Nothing can pierce my shield! | He bangs a shiny shield; "UNBREAKABLE" sign |
| 3 | Then he holds up a spear. My spear can pierce anything! | He raises a gleaming spear; "PIERCES ANYTHING" sign |
| 4 | Someone in the crowd asks: what if your spear hits your shield? | A kid in the crowd points; the crowd goes "ooooh" |
| 5 | The salesman has no answer. | The salesman freezes, sweating |
| 6 | If the spear goes through, the shield wasn't unbreakable. | Path A: spear pierces, "SHIELD LIED" ✘ |
| 7 | If the shield stops it, the spear wasn't unstoppable. | Path B: spear bends, "SPEAR LIED" ✘ |
| 8 | Both things can't be true. | Both claims crash into each other |
| 9 | In Chinese, spear is mao. Shield is dun. Together, mao dun. | Big characters 矛 + 盾 = 矛盾 with the sounds "máo" "dùn" |
| 10 | And that's still the Chinese word for contradiction. | 矛盾 = "CONTRADICTION" stamp |
| 11 | The story comes from a book by Han Fei, written over two thousand years ago. | Bamboo-scroll book card |
| 12 | So what really happens? Does the spear win, or the shield? | Buttons: SPEAR / SHIELD |

*Source:* *Han Feizi*, the chapter "Difficulties" (3rd century BC). 矛盾 (máodùn) means "contradiction".
[The China Story: Contradiction and the Stubborn Bystander](https://www.thechinastory.org/yearbooks/yearbook-2021-contradiction/forum-introduction/contradiction-and-the-stubborn-bystander/).
*Mishearing risk:* "mao" and "dun" may be heard as "now" or "done". The captions will show both the Chinese characters and "máo / dùn", so I'd keep them.
