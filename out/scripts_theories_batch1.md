## 44. Tue Oct 20, 9:30 PM: "You Might Be Living Inside a Computer Game" 🎮 (simulation theory)

1. A philosopher at Oxford argued that you might be living inside a computer simulation.
2. In two thousand three, Nick Bostrom said: at least one of these three things must be true.
3. One. Humans die out before we can build super-realistic simulations.
4. Two. We could build them, but we never bother.
5. Three. We are almost certainly living inside one right now.
6. Why number three? Look at video games.
7. About fifty years ago, the best game was Pong. Two lines and a dot.
8. Today, games look almost real. Keep improving, and one day you won't be able to tell them from reality.
9. A future civilization could run billions of simulated worlds, full of people who think they're real.
10. So for every real world, there could be billions of fake ones.
11. Imagine a billion dream worlds and one real one. You wake up somewhere. Where are you, probably?
12. In twenty sixteen, Elon Musk said the odds that we are in base reality are one in billions. *(reworded: "the chance we're in" was heard as "the chance wherein")*
13. But Bostrom himself says he doesn't know which of the three is true.
14. So be honest. If this were a simulation, what's the first thing you'd do?

*Sources:* N. Bostrom, "Are You Living in a Computer Simulation?", *Philosophical Quarterly* 53 (2003) ([PDF](https://simulation-argument.com/simulation.pdf));
Elon Musk at the 2016 Code Conference ("one in billions", the Pong comparison) as reported by [Game Informer](https://www.gameinformer.com/b/news/archive/2016/06/02/elon-musk-discusses-possibility-of-life-existing-as-a-simulation.aspx);
[Wikipedia: Simulation hypothesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simulation_hypothesis). Pong came out in 1972.

## 45. Wed Oct 21, 4:30 AM: "Who Made God? The 700-Year-Old Debate" ✨ (the first-cause argument)

1. Two brilliant minds. One huge question. Does God exist?
2. Over seven hundred years ago, Thomas Aquinas made this argument.
3. Everything that happens has a cause. You exist because of your parents. They exist because of theirs.
4. Follow the chain back. It can't go back forever, he said. Something had to start it.
5. A first cause. That, Aquinas wrote, is what everyone calls God. *(reworded: "And that" was heard as "In that")*
6. Like a line of falling dominoes. Someone had to push the first one.
7. Then, in nineteen twenty-seven, Bertrand Russell pushed back. (Russell, in his own voice:) If everything must have a cause, then God must have a cause.
8. And if anything can exist without a cause, he said, it may just as well be the world.
9. Believers have an answer too. God isn't something that began. So God doesn't need a cause.
10. Seven hundred years later, people are still arguing about it.
11. So who wins this one? Aquinas, or Russell? Keep it respectful in the comments.

*Sources:* Thomas Aquinas, *Summa Theologiae* I, q.2, a.3 (the "second way", written 1265–1274): "...a first efficient cause,
to which everyone gives the name of God"; Bertrand Russell, "Why I Am Not a Christian" (lecture, 1927): "If everything must
have a cause, then God must have a cause. If there can be anything without a cause, it may just as well be the world as God..."
([text](https://users.manchester.edu/Facstaff/SSNaragon/Online/texts/201/Russell,%20Not%20a%20Christian.pdf)).
Line 9 is the classical reply (only what begins to exist needs a cause). Both sides are given, with no verdict.

## 46. Wed Oct 21, 9:30 PM: "Where Is Everybody?" 👽 (the Fermi paradox)

1. Our galaxy could have billions of planets like Earth. So where is everybody?
2. Nineteen fifty. The physicist Enrico Fermi is having lunch with friends, joking about flying saucers.
3. Suddenly he asks. But where is everybody?
4. Here's why that's scary. Our galaxy has at least a hundred billion stars.
5. And scientists estimate billions of Earth-size planets sit in the zone where water can be liquid.
6. Even if only a tiny fraction had life, the galaxy should be buzzing.
7. But we've heard nothing. No signals. No visitors. Just silence.
8. So, the answers. Maybe life is incredibly rare, and we're just lucky.
9. Maybe something wipes out civilizations before they reach the stars. Some scientists call it the Great Filter.
10. Or maybe they're out there, staying quiet on purpose.
11. It's like standing in a dark forest at night. Total silence. Either you're alone, or everyone else is hiding.
12. Which answer scares you more? That we're alone? Or that we're not?

*Sources:* E. M. Jones, "Where Is Everybody?" An Account of Fermi's Question (Los Alamos, LA-10311-MS, 1985) (lunch in summer 1950
with Konopinski, Teller and York); Milky Way 100–400 billion stars; Kepler-based estimate of at least 8.8 billion Earth-size planets in
the habitable zone of Sun-like stars (Petigura et al., 2013); "Great Filter" (Robin Hanson, 1996).
