# "Researchers found…" videos: research for a new series

The owner's reference was a Hindi Short by Abhay Create (71 s): a creator talking to camera, cut with 2D cartoons, built
around one famous claim about society ("5 rich vs 95 poor", a Napoleon quote about religion and the rich, "PRAYER vs
SYSTEM", "systemic oppression", ending "95 > 5"). The download had no audio track, so this is from the visuals.

## What makes the format work
- **Authority hook:** "A famous person / researchers said X." The name makes a surprising claim feel earned.
- **One idea, explained with simple cartoons:** crowds of silhouettes, a split screen, a number on a monitor.
- **About the viewer's own life:** money, effort, happiness, how people see you. Viewers test it on themselves and
  argue in the comments.
- **Ends on a punchy takeaway** ("95 > 5") that people repeat in the comments.

## What we change for our channel
- **Only claims with a real source.** The Napoleon line has no known original source (it lives on quote sites), so it's
  out. Religion-vs-rich commentary is also risky for advertisers. We keep the format, not that topic.
- **Real experiments over quotes:** each video names the researcher, the year and the actual numbers, and shows the
  experiment as a cartoon.
- Same rules as the paradoxes: impossible-sounding hook, one step per sentence, end on a question.

## Topics checked (strongest first)
1. **The Spotlight Effect** (Gilovich, Medvec & Savitsky, Cornell, 2000). Students wore an embarrassing Barry Manilow
   T-shirt into a room. They guessed about half the room noticed; only about a quarter did. Hook: "Nobody is looking at
   you as much as you think. Researchers proved it with one embarrassing T-shirt."
2. **The Great Hanoi Rat Hunt** (French colonial Hanoi, 1902; historian Michael G. Vann, archive records). The city
   paid one cent per rat tail; people cut off tails, let the rats go to breed, and even farmed rats. Hook: "A city paid
   people to kill rats. It ended up with more rats."
3. **Lottery winners weren't happier** (Brickman, Coates & Janoff-Bulman, 1978). 22 major lottery winners were barely
   happier than ordinary people (4.00 vs 3.82) and enjoyed everyday things less. Small study: say "22 winners".
4. **Rewards that kill motivation** (Lepper, Greene & Nisbett, Stanford, 1973). Kids who loved drawing were promised
   an award for drawing; afterwards they drew about half as much in free time.
5. **The Ringelmann effect** (Max Ringelmann, rope-pulling, published 1913). The more people pulled a rope, the less
   each one pulled: eight people pulled at about half their solo strength each.
6. **The Ben Franklin effect** (Franklin's autobiography; Jecker & Landy, 1969). Franklin won over a rival by asking to
   borrow a book; in the 1969 study, people asked for a favour by the researcher liked him more.
7. **The marshmallow test, revisited** (Mischel, 1970s; Watts, Duncan & Quan, 2018). The famous "willpower predicts
   success" link shrank by about two thirds once family background was counted. Hook: "The most famous test of
   willpower… was mostly about money."

## Left out
- Napoleon's religion quote (no original source).
- The Economist "decoy" pricing story (from Dan Ariely, whose research has faced data-integrity questions; disputed).
- Kitty Genovese and the "38 witnesses" (the newspaper story was wrong).
- Stanford Prison Experiment (method heavily disputed).
